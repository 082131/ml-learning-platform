<Section kind="intuition" title="If every cell had its best price, the arrows would draw themselves">

In the last lesson someone handed us a policy and we computed what each cell is worth under it. Now flip the
question: *nobody gives us a policy. What is the best one?*

Here is the key insight. Suppose you somehow knew the value of every cell **assuming you play perfectly from then
on**. Then choosing a move would be trivial: from any cell, look one step ahead, and take the action that leads, on
average, to the best reward plus best next-cell value. The arrows would draw themselves.

So the problem shrinks to: *compute the values of perfect play*. Value iteration does exactly that, with one small
change to the sweep you already know. Instead of averaging over what a given policy would do, each cell assumes it
will take its **best** action.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="From the expectation equation to the optimality equation">

### The big picture

Start with all values at 0 → sweep every cell, giving it the value of its **best** action (not the policy's average)
→ repeat until the values settle: these are the **optimal values** → in every cell, pick the action that achieves that
best value: that is the **optimal policy**.

### What "best policy" means

A policy $\pi$ is better than or equal to another policy $\pi'$ if its value is at least as high **in every state**.
An <Term id="optimal-policy">optimal policy</Term> $\pi^*$ is one that is better than or equal to every other policy.
It always exists for a finite MDP, and all optimal policies share the same values, written $V^*(s)$ and $Q^*(s, a)$.
(There can be several optimal policies when two actions tie; they just choose differently between equally good moves.)

### The Bellman optimality equation

Last lesson's Bellman equation averaged over the policy's choices:
$V^\pi(s) = \sum_a \pi(a \mid s) \, Q^\pi(s, a)$. If you act optimally, you do not average: you take the best action.
That gives the <Term id="bellman-optimality">Bellman optimality equation</Term>:

$$
V^*(s) = \max_a \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a) \bigl[\, r(s, a, s') + \gamma\, V^*(s') \,\bigr]
$$

Compare it piece by piece with the expectation version:

- The inner part is unchanged: for one action, average "reward + γ × value of the landing cell" over where it might
  land. That is $Q^*(s, a)$.
- The outer $\sum_a \pi(a \mid s)$ is replaced by $\max_a$. "The value of a state is the value of its best action,
  assuming optimal play afterwards."

The difference is one word, average versus max, but it changes what the equation describes: no longer *a* policy, but
*the best* one.

### Value iteration: sweep the max

The new equation has the same circularity as before ($V^*$ on both sides), and we break it the same way. This is
<Term id="value-iteration">value iteration</Term>:

1. Start with $V(s) = 0$ everywhere.
2. Sweep: for every state, compute every action's value from the current $V$ and keep the largest.
3. Repeat until the largest change is tiny.
4. Read off the <Term id="greedy-policy">greedy policy</Term>: in each state, the action with the highest one-step
   lookahead value.

Why does it converge? The same argument as policy evaluation: after $k$ sweeps, $V$ is the best possible expected
return over the next $k$ steps, and each extra step adds less (because of γ or because episodes end). On our grid it
settles after 22 sweeps, and the values are exactly the safe policy's from the last lesson: 0.705 at the start. So the
"safe route" we evaluated was in fact optimal.

A practical detail: the **arrows usually settle long before the values do**. The greedy policy only needs the
*ranking* of actions to be right, not the precise numbers. In the explorer you can watch the red arrows (ones that
will still change) disappear by sweep 12, while the numbers keep creeping toward their final values until sweep 22.

### The reward shapes the policy

The optimal policy is not a property of the map alone; it depends on the rewards, the slip and γ. This is the most
instructive part of the lesson, because it shows the reward design idea from lesson 1 at work:

| Reward per move | What the optimal policy does | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| −0.04 | the safe route up the left; at (1,2) go up; at (2,3), below the pit, go *left*, away from it | moves are cheap, so a longer but safer route pays |
| −0.4 | the bottom row now heads right and goes up beside the pit | moves are expensive enough that the shortcut's risk is worth taking |
| −2 | from (1,2) and (2,3), step straight into the pit | every move costs more than the pit; ending the episode at −1 is the cheapest way out |
| 0 | very cautious: at (1,2) go left into the wall, never risking the pit | waiting is free, so there is no reason to accept any risk |
| +0.1 | with γ = 1, avoid both exits forever | every move pays, so ending the episode would stop the income; the values grow without limit |

That last row is a warning: with γ = 1 and a positive step reward, "the best return" is infinite and value iteration
never converges. Lower γ below 1 in the explorer and it converges again, because an endless stream of +0.1 is then
worth a finite $0.1/(1-\gamma)$.

### Policy iteration: the other classic method

<Term id="policy-iteration">Policy iteration</Term> reaches the same answer by alternating the two things you already
know: **evaluate** the current policy fully (lesson 5), then **improve** it by making it greedy with respect to those
values, and repeat until the policy stops changing. Each round is more work (a full evaluation), but it usually needs
very few rounds. Value iteration can be seen as policy iteration that stops each evaluation after a single sweep.
Both rely on the same fact: being greedy with respect to a policy's values never makes the policy worse.

### The limit of planning

Value iteration needs the full rulebook, $P$ and $r$ for every state and action, and it sweeps over **every** state.
That is fine for 11 cells. It is impossible for chess (around $10^{44}$ positions), and it is useless when nobody can
write down $P$, as for a robot on a real floor. The next chapter removes both requirements: the agent will learn from
sampled experience, visiting only the states it actually reaches.

</Section>

<Section kind="mistakes" title="Value iteration mistakes">

<CommonMistake
  title="Taking the max over next states instead of over actions"
  wrong={'V[s] = max(r + gamma * V[n] for a in MOVES for p, n, r, done in outcomes(s, a))'}
  error="(no error) every cell beside the pit looks great, because the agent assumes it always lands on the best cell"
  cause="The agent chooses the action, not where it lands. The landing cell must be averaged with its probabilities; only the action is maximised."
  fix="max over actions of the probability-weighted sum over next states."
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Trusting the values before they converge"
  wrong="V(start) after 5 sweeps is 0.16, so the start is worth 0.16."
  error="(misconception) after 5 sweeps the values only count the next 5 steps"
  cause="Early sweeps are short-horizon estimates; the arrows may be right while the numbers are still too low."
  fix="Run until the largest change is below a threshold, and report the converged values."
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Expecting convergence with γ = 1 and endless rewards"
  wrong={'value_iteration(gamma=1.0, step=+0.1)'}
  error="(no error) the loop never ends: the values grow by about 0.1 every sweep"
  cause="If staying in the game forever earns more and more, the optimal value is infinite."
  fix="Use γ < 1 for tasks that can continue forever, or make sure every policy ends the episode."
/>

<Section kind="summary">

The chain of this lesson: if we knew the values of **perfect play**, choosing actions would be a one-step lookahead.
Those values satisfy the **Bellman optimality equation**, which is last lesson's equation with the policy's average
replaced by a **max over actions**. **Value iteration** sweeps that max from zeros until the values settle, then
reads off the **greedy policy**, which is optimal. The optimal policy depends on the rewards: a tiny step cost gives
the safe route, a big one makes the pit attractive, and a positive one makes the agent avoid ending at all. **Policy
iteration** gets the same answer by alternating full evaluation and greedy improvement.

All of this needed the complete model $P$ and a sweep over every state. Real agents usually have neither. The next
chapter keeps the Bellman idea but learns from samples instead of from the rulebook.

Next: [Learning from experience: Monte Carlo and TD](#/learn/rl/learning/monte-carlo-td).

</Section>

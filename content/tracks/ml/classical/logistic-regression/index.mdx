<Section kind="intuition" title="From a score to a probability">

Adelie and Chinstrap penguins look alike, but Chinstraps tend to have longer bills. Measure a penguin's bill and
you can make a good guess at its species, and you would like the guess to come with a confidence: "almost
certainly a Chinstrap" for a very long bill, "could be either" for one in between.

<Term id="logistic-regression">Logistic regression</Term> does exactly this. It starts like linear regression:
a weighted sum of the <Term id="feature">features</Term>, $z = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + b$, called the score. A score
can be any number, so it is passed through the <Term id="sigmoid">sigmoid</Term>, an S-shaped function that maps
every number into the range 0 to 1. The result $p$ is read as the probability that the penguin is a Chinstrap.
Predicting "Chinstrap" whenever $p \ge 0.5$ splits the plane of bill measurements into two halves; the line
between them is the <Term id="decision-boundary">decision boundary</Term>.

Training chooses $w_1$, $w_2$ and $b$ so that the probabilities fit the <Term id="label">labels</Term> well, using
<Term id="gradient-descent">gradient descent</Term> as in the previous lesson, but on a loss made for
probabilities.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="Sigmoid, log loss and its gradient">

**Data.** The 219 Adelie and Chinstrap penguins in `penguins.csv` with both bill measurements (one Adelie row has
none). Every 4th of them in file order (positions 3, 7, 11, …) is held out as the <Term id="test-set">test set</Term>
(54 penguins); the other 165 form the <Term id="training-set">training set</Term> (114 Adelie, 51 Chinstrap). The
label is $y = 1$ for Chinstrap and $y = 0$ for Adelie.

**Standardizing.** Bill length (around 42 mm) and depth (around 18.5 mm) have different scales, so each is
replaced by its distance from the training mean in training standard deviations:
$x_1 = (\text{length} - 42.3582) / 5.4750$ and $x_2 = (\text{depth} - 18.5376) / 1.2126$. The training matrix $Z$
has shape $(165, 2)$. Test penguins are scaled with the same training numbers.

**Model.** For one penguin,

$$
z = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + b, \qquad p = \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}
$$

$\sigma(0) = 0.5$, $\sigma(2) = 1 / (1 + 0.1353) = 0.8808$, and $\sigma(-z) = 1 - \sigma(z)$. Because $\sigma$ only
increases, $p \ge 0.5$ exactly when $z \ge 0$, so the decision boundary is the line $w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + b = 0$.

**Loss.** MSE is a poor fit for probabilities (and makes the loss surface non-convex with a sigmoid). The
<Term id="log-loss">log loss</Term> charges each penguin $-\log$ of the probability the model gave its true species:

$$
L = -\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i} \ell_i
$$

$$
\ell_i = y_i \log p_i + (1 - y_i) \log (1 - p_i)
$$

A Chinstrap with $p = 0.9$ costs $-\log 0.9 = 0.1054$; with $p = 0.1$ it costs $-\log 0.1 = 2.3026$. At the start,
with every weight 0, every $p$ is 0.5 and $L = \log 2 = 0.6931$.

**Gradient.** With the sigmoid and log loss together, the derivative simplifies to

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = \frac{1}{n} Z^\top (p - y)
$$

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_i (p_i - y_i)
$$

where $p - y$ has shape $(165,)$ and $Z^\top (p - y)$ has shape $(2,)$. Batch gradient descent repeats
$w \leftarrow w - \eta \, \partial L / \partial w$ and $b \leftarrow b - \eta \, \partial L / \partial b$ with
<Term id="learning-rate">learning rate</Term> $\eta$. The log loss is convex in $(w, b)$, so there are no bad local
minima. Unlike linear regression there is no closed-form answer, so an iterative method is always needed.

<Callout kind="simplification">
  When the classes are almost separable, as here, the unpenalized log loss keeps decreasing as the weights grow,
  so plain gradient descent keeps making the weights larger and the probabilities more extreme. Libraries add a
  penalty on the weights to stop this (see the scikit-learn example in the Code section).
</Callout>

</Section>

`LogisticRegression()` uses an L2 penalty by default with $C = 1.0$: it minimizes
$C \sum_i \text{loss}_i + \tfrac{1}{2} \lVert w \rVert^2$ (the intercept is not penalized). Dividing by $C n$, that
is the mean log loss plus $\lVert w \rVert^2 / (2 C n)$, a pull toward small weights. That is why its weights
(3.8127, −1.1936) are much smaller than those of long, unpenalized gradient descent, while `C=np.inf` (no penalty)
gives 10.8703 and −4.5112, which the experiment approaches with $\eta = 30$ and 500 steps (10.8691, −4.5138). Both
put the boundary in a similar place; the penalized model gives less extreme probabilities.

<Callout kind="note">
  With `C=np.inf`, scikit-learn 1.8.0 also printed this warning before the second line (its own internal
  handling of the new parameters, not an error in the code):
  `UserWarning: Setting penalty=None will ignore the C and l1_ratio parameters`. In 1.8, `C=np.inf` is the
  documented way to switch the penalty off; the old `penalty=None` is deprecated.
</Callout>

Things to try:

- Set the steps to 0: every weight is 0, the shading is uniform, and the log loss is $\log 2 = 0.6931$.
- $\eta = 0.01$: after 50 steps the log loss has only dropped from 0.6931 to 0.6085. The learning rate is far too
  small.
- $\eta = 30$, 500 steps: the weights grow towards the unpenalized answer and the shading turns into a sharp edge.
  The training accuracy hardly changes, but the probabilities become extreme.
- Compare the training and test log loss as you train longer. Here both keep falling (0.1303 and 0.1412 after 50
  steps at $\eta = 0.5$, 0.0565 and 0.0698 after 500), because a straight line cannot overfit these two features
  much. On noisier data the test loss can start rising while the training loss still falls.

<CommonMistake
  title="Passing species names straight into the loss"
  wrong={'y = train["species"].to_numpy()\nloss = -np.mean(y * np.log(p) + (1 - y) * np.log(1 - p))'}
  error="TypeError: can't multiply sequence by non-int of type 'float'"
  cause="The labels are strings such as 'Adelie'. The log loss needs numbers: 1 for the positive class and 0 for the other. (scikit-learn accepts string labels, but your own NumPy code does not.)"
  fix={'y = (train["species"] == "Chinstrap").to_numpy().astype(float)'}
/>

<Callout kind="warning">
  A probability of 0.99 from a model is not a guarantee. With nearly separable training data and no penalty, the
  probabilities become extreme even for penguins near the boundary. Check the test log loss, not just the
  accuracy, and prefer a regularized model when probabilities matter.
</Callout>

<Section kind="practice" title="Where you will meet it">

- **As the classification baseline.** Logistic regression is fast, its weights are readable (a positive weight
  pushes towards the positive class), and it gives probabilities. Try it before anything more complex.
- **With a threshold other than 0.5.** A medical screen may flag anything above $p = 0.1$. The next lesson,
  [Evaluation metrics and error analysis](#/learn/ml/classical/metrics-error-analysis), moves the threshold and
  measures what changes.
- **In neural networks.** The last layer of a binary classifier is a logistic regression on learned features, and
  binary cross-entropy is this log loss. With more than two classes, the sigmoid becomes the softmax.
- **When the boundary must bend.** Add features such as $x_1^2$ or $x_1 x_2$, or use trees or neural networks.

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

- Logistic regression computes a score $z = w \cdot x + b$ and a probability $p = \sigma(z) = 1 / (1 + e^{-z})$.
- With threshold 0.5, the decision boundary is the line $w \cdot x + b = 0$.
- It is trained by minimizing log loss; the gradient is $Z^\top (p - y) / n$ for $w$ and $\text{mean}(p - y)$ for $b$.
- Standardize with training statistics only, and score test data with the same numbers.
- scikit-learn's `LogisticRegression` adds an L2 penalty by default ($C = 1.0$), so its weights are smaller than
  plain gradient descent's; `C=np.inf` removes the penalty.

Next: [Evaluation metrics and error analysis](#/learn/ml/classical/metrics-error-analysis) asks how good these
predictions really are, beyond accuracy.

</Section>

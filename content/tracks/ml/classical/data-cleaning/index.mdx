<Section kind="intuition" title="Real data has holes and typos">

The Palmer penguins <Term id="dataset">dataset</Term> has 344 rows, one per penguin: species, island, four body
measurements, sex and year. It is a clean, well-known dataset, and it still has gaps. Two penguins (rows 3 and 271
of the file) have no measurements at all, and 9 more have every measurement but no recorded sex. Each gap is a
<Term id="missing-value">missing value</Term>.

Most models can't work with a hole in a row, so before any training you decide, column by column, what to do:

- **Drop** the rows with holes. Simple, but you lose the rest of each row too.
- **Fill** the holes with a stand-in, such as the column's median. This is called
  <Term id="imputation">imputation</Term>. You keep the rows, but the stand-ins are made up and they change the
  column's statistics.

A second problem is values that are present but wrong. Imagine someone typed a penguin's body mass as 37500 g
instead of 3750 g. That typo is **not in the real data**; we will inject it on purpose to see what it does. A
value far from the rest is an <Term id="outlier">outlier</Term>. Some outliers are mistakes and some are real,
rare animals, and a rule can only point at candidates.

Finally, a model needs numbers, so text categories like species have to be turned into numbers in a way that
doesn't invent an order.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="Missing values, quartiles and fences">

**Finding missing values.** In a pandas <Term id="dataframe">DataFrame</Term> `df`, `df.isna()` marks every missing cell and `df.isna().sum()` counts them per
column. pandas shows a missing number as `NaN` ("not a number"). Statistics such as the <Term id="mean">mean</Term> (`mean()`) and the median (`median()`) skip
missing values, so they quietly describe fewer rows than the DataFrame has.

**Dropping.** `df.dropna()` removes every row with at least one missing cell. On the full dataset that leaves
$344 - 11 = 333$ rows: the 2 rows with no measurements and the 9 with no sex. `df.dropna(subset=cols)` looks
only at the listed columns.

**Filling.** `df.fillna(value)` fills holes. For a number column the usual fills are the median or the mean of
the values that are present. `sex` is categorical text: it has no median or mean. You either drop those rows,
fill with the most common category, or add an honest new category such as `"unknown"`.

What a fill does to the statistics:

- Filling with the **mean** leaves the mean unchanged but shrinks the <Term id="standard-deviation">standard deviation</Term>: every filled value is
  at distance 0 from the mean, yet it adds 1 to the count.
- Filling with the **median** leaves the median unchanged and also pulls the spread inward.
- If the column contains a typo, the mean you fill with is wrong too.

**Quartiles.** Sort the $n$ values and number the positions from 0. The $q$-quantile sits at position
$(n - 1) \cdot q$. When that position falls between two values, take the value at the whole part and move the
fractional part of the way to the next one (linear interpolation, the default of pandas' `quantile` and NumPy's
`np.quantile`). The first quartile $Q_1$ is the 0.25-quantile and the third quartile $Q_3$ is the 0.75-quantile.

**Worked example.** With 12 body masses, sorted
3250, 3300, 3450, 3475, 3750, 3800, 4150, 4250, 4500, 4875, 5750, 5850:

$Q_1$ sits at position $11 \cdot 0.25 = 2.75$, between 3450 (position 2) and 3475 (position 3);
$Q_3$ sits at position $11 \cdot 0.75 = 8.25$, between 4500 and 4875:

$$
Q_1 = 3450 + (3475 - 3450) \cdot 0.75 = 3468.75
$$

$$
Q_3 = 4500 + (4875 - 4500) \cdot 0.25 = 4593.75
$$

**The 1.5·IQR rule.** The <Term id="interquartile-range">interquartile range</Term> is
$\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1$, the width of the middle half of the data. Values outside the fences

$$
Q_1 - 1.5 \cdot \text{IQR} \quad \text{and} \quad Q_3 + 1.5 \cdot \text{IQR}
$$

are flagged. Here $\text{IQR} = 4593.75 - 3468.75 = 1125$, so the fences are
$3468.75 - 1687.5 = 1781.25$ and $4593.75 + 1687.5 = 6281.25$, and none of the 12 values is flagged.

**Mean versus median.** The mean uses the size of every value: one typo of $+33750$ g among 9 values raises it by
$33750 / 9 = 3750$ g. The median uses only their order, so the same typo moves it by at most one position. The
quartiles are order-based too, which is why the IQR rule still works when the data contains the very outlier it
is looking for.

<Callout kind="simplification">
  The 1.5 in the fences is a convention (from John Tukey's box plots), not a law, and the rule assumes a single
  group of roughly symmetric values. Penguins of three species with different sizes are not one group: a heavy
  male Gentoo can be flagged although nothing is wrong with it. A flag means "look at this row", not "delete it".
</Callout>

**Categories to numbers.** <Term id="one-hot-encoding">One-hot encoding</Term> replaces a column with one 0/1
column per category, with a single 1 per row. Numbering the categories instead (Adelie = 0, Chinstrap = 1,
Gentoo = 2) tells a model that Gentoo is "more" than Chinstrap and that Adelie is closer to Chinstrap than to
Gentoo. Species have no such order, so for categories without an order (called nominal categories) one-hot is
the safe default. A <Term id="feature">feature</Term> with a real order, such as small, medium, large, can keep
ordered numbers.

</Section>

<Section kind="practice" title="Cleaning as a decision record">

Cleaning choices change what a model learns, so write them down with their reason. For the penguins:

| Column             | Missing          | Decision                         | Why                                                         |
| ------------------ | ---------------- | -------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| The 4 measurements | 2 rows, all four | Drop the 2 rows                  | Nothing measured; a fill would invent a whole penguin      |
| `sex`              | 11 rows          | Category `"unknown"` or drop     | Text has no median; a new category keeps the other columns |
| `body_mass_g`      | (typos)          | Flag with 1.5·IQR, check by hand | The rule can't tell a typo from a heavy Gentoo              |
| `species`, `island`| none             | One-hot encode                   | Nominal categories have no order                            |

Two habits make this safe in real projects. First, compute fill values (medians, means) from the training data
only and reuse them for new data; [Train, validation and test sets](#/learn/ml/classical/splits-leakage) shows
why. Second, keep the raw data untouched and make every cleaning step a line of code, so it can be rerun.

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

- Find holes with `df.isna().sum()`. Statistics skip missing values, so check the count behind every number.
- Dropping loses whole rows; filling keeps them but invents values. Mean and median fills shrink the spread.
- Categorical text has no median: drop, use the most common category, or add `"unknown"`.
- The 1.5·IQR rule flags values outside $Q_1 - 1.5\,\text{IQR}$ and $Q_3 + 1.5\,\text{IQR}$. A flag is a
  question, not a verdict.
- The median and quartiles resist a typo; the mean and standard deviation do not. Fix typos before imputing.
- One-hot encode nominal categories with `pd.get_dummies`; don't number them 0, 1, 2.

measurements say about each other.

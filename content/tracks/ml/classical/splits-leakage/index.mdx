<Section kind="intuition" title="Don't study with the exam paper">

A teacher wants to know whether you understand the material, not whether you remember particular questions. So the
practice problems you study with are different from the questions on the final exam. If you got hold of the exam
paper a week early, you could score 100% without understanding anything, and the score would tell the teacher
nothing.

A <Term id="model">model</Term> is in the same position. We fit it on some rows of a <Term id="dataset">dataset</Term>
and want to know how it will do on rows it has never seen: its <Term id="generalization">generalization</Term>. So
before doing anything else we split the rows into three groups:

| Split                                         | Exam analogy                          | Used for                                                       |
| --------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| <Term id="training-set">Training set</Term>   | Practice problems                     | Fitting the model: every learned number comes from these rows  |
| <Term id="validation-set">Validation set</Term> | Mock exams, to decide how to study  | Comparing models and choosing settings                         |
| <Term id="test-set">Test set</Term>           | The real exam, taken once             | One final, honest estimate of performance on new data          |

<Term id="data-leakage">Data leakage</Term> is the model getting a peek at the exam paper: information from the
validation or test rows (or from the answer itself) slips into training. The score goes up; the model does not get
any better. This lesson shows a leak inflating a score from 50% to about 80% on data where no real pattern exists.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="Splits, and the rule that keeps them honest">

**Splitting.** Take $n$ rows and choose a validation share and a test share. Each held-out count is the share times
$n$, rounded to a whole row; training gets the rest. With $n = 60$, 15% validation and 30% test:

$$
n_\text{val} = 60 \cdot 0.15 = 9, \qquad n_\text{test} = 60 \cdot 0.30 = 18, \qquad n_\text{train} = 60 - 9 - 18 = 33
$$

The rows are **shuffled first** with a seeded random generator, then cut in order: the first 33 shuffled rows train,
the next 9 validate, the last 18 test. Shuffling matters because files are often sorted (`penguins.csv` lists every
Adelie penguin before any Gentoo), and a <Term id="random-seed">seed</Term> makes the split the same every time you run the code.

**What each split is for.** The model learns its <Term id="parameter">parameters</Term> from the training rows.
Settings you choose yourself, such as how many features to keep, are <Term id="hyperparameter">hyperparameters</Term>:
you try several and keep the one with the best validation score. Because you picked the winner by looking at
validation scores, the winner's validation score is a little optimistic. The test set has played no part in any
choice, so its score is the honest estimate. Use it once, at the end.

**The rule.** _Everything that is fitted is fitted on training rows only._ That includes the model, but also every
preprocessing step that learns numbers from data: a scaler's mean and standard deviation, the values used to fill in
missing entries, and which features to keep. The validation and test rows are only ever _transformed_ with numbers
learned from the training rows.

**The leakage experiment.** To see what breaking the rule costs, we use data where the right answer is known:

- $X$ has shape $(60, 300)$: 60 rows, 300 <Term id="feature">features</Term>, every value an independent draw from a
  standard normal distribution (mean 0, standard deviation 1). The dataset is generated once from a fixed seed.
- $y$ has shape $(60,)$: a <Term id="label">label</Term> of 0 or 1 per row, 30 of each, assigned at random.

The features say nothing about the labels, so on new rows _any_ model is right 50% of the time, on average.
The pipeline has two steps:

1. <Term id="feature-selection">Feature selection</Term>: score each feature $j$ by the size of its Pearson
   <Term id="correlation">correlation</Term> with the label, $|r_j|$, and keep the $k$ features with the largest scores.

   $$
   r_j = \frac{\sum_i (x_{ij} - \bar{x}_j)(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum_i (x_{ij} - \bar{x}_j)^2 \, \sum_i (y_i - \bar{y})^2}}
   $$

   The sums run over the rows the selection is allowed to see.

2. A **nearest-centroid** classifier on the $k$ kept features: for each class $c$, average the training rows of
   that class to get a centroid $\mu_c$ (a vector of $k$ means). A test row $x$ gets the class whose centroid is
   closer: class 1 if $\lVert x - \mu_1 \rVert^2 < \lVert x - \mu_0 \rVert^2$, otherwise class 0.

**Accuracy** is the share of test rows classified correctly, for example $9 / 18 = 50\%$. The two versions of the
pipeline differ in one thing only: whether step 1 sums over the 33 training rows (honest) or over all 60 rows,
test rows included (leak). The centroids always use training rows only.

**Why the leak works on pure noise.** Even unrelated numbers correlate a little in a finite sample. For independent
data, $r$ computed on $n$ rows has a spread of roughly $1 / \sqrt{n - 1}$, which is $1 / \sqrt{59} \approx 0.13$
for 60 rows (an approximation for large $n$). With 300 features, a few will reach $|r|$ around $0.3$ to $0.4$ by
chance alone. When the selection sees the test rows, it prefers features whose chance pattern _includes_ the test
rows, so those same features then appear to predict the test labels. On new data the pattern is gone.

<Callout kind="simplification">
  The experiment uses one split and an 18-row test set, so a single score moves in steps of $1/18 \approx 5.6$
  percentage points and varies a lot between seeds. That is why it also shows the mean over 50 splits. Real
  projects have more rows, but the same leak with many features and few rows is a documented cause of results that
  fail to replicate.
</Callout>

<Section kind="mistakes" title="Leaks that raise no error, and errors that do">

Leaks are dangerous because nothing fails. The score just looks good. The common ones:

| Leak                                    | What happens                                                                                                  | Fix                                                                                     |
| --------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Preprocessing fitted on all rows        | A scaler, imputer or feature selector learns numbers from the test rows                                       | Split first; fit preprocessing on training rows (a scikit-learn <Term id="ml-pipeline">pipeline</Term> does this) |
| Duplicates across splits                | The same penguin, patient or photo appears in train and test, so the test row is a memorized one              | Remove exact duplicates before splitting; keep all rows of one entity in one split      |
| Target leakage                          | A feature is computed from the answer or only known after it, such as "refund issued" when predicting fraud   | For each feature ask: would I know this value at prediction time?                      |
| Time order ignored                      | Shuffling time-stamped rows trains on the future and tests on the past                                        | Split by time: train on earlier rows, validate and test on later ones                   |
| Tuning on the test set                  | Trying many settings and keeping the best test score                                                          | Choose with validation (or cross-validation); use the test set once                     |

Here is the first one with real numbers: the flipper lengths (mm) of six Adelie penguins (rows 0, 1, 2, 4, 5 and 6
of `penguins.csv`) as training rows, and two Gentoo penguins (rows 152 and 153) as test rows.

<Code example={scalerLeak} title="scaler_leak.py" />

Fitted on training rows only, the mean is $1126 / 6 = 187.67$ and the test penguins come out 4.27 and 7.74 standard
deviations above it: clearly unlike anything in training. Fitted on all eight rows, the mean moves to
$1567 / 8 = 195.875$ and they look ordinary (0.96 and 2.17). The leaky version has hidden exactly the thing a test
set is meant to reveal.

<Section kind="practice" title="Where you will meet it">

- **Every model you train.** The next lessons fit linear and logistic regression on penguins; each starts with a
  split, and each fitted step goes in a pipeline.
- **Choosing hyperparameters.** [Overfitting and regularization](#/learn/ml/classical/fit-regularization) picks a
  polynomial degree and a penalty with a validation set, and
  [Cross-validation](#/learn/ml/classical/cross-validation) replaces the single validation split with several.
- **Medical and scientific data.** Several patients' scans, or one patient's many scans? Group splits (all rows of
  one patient in one split) prevent the duplicates leak.
- **Forecasting.** Sales, prices and sensor readings are split by time, never shuffled.

<Section kind="summary">

- Split before anything else: train to fit, validation to choose, test once to report.
- Shuffle with a seed so the split is random and reproducible. Shares become row counts: $60 \cdot 0.3 = 18$.
- Fit everything that learns from data (model, scaler, imputer, feature selection) on training rows only.
- On pure noise, choosing 10 of 300 features with the test rows in view lifts test accuracy from about 50% to
  about 80%. Leaks raise no error; they make the score lie.
- Watch for duplicates across splits, features that encode the answer, and time order.

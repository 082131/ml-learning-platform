<Section kind="intuition" title="Two budgets: memory and time">

Training a neural network is mostly matrix multiplication, and a GPU is a machine for doing thousands of
multiply-adds at once (the [CPU vs GPU lesson](#/learn/inference/hardware/cpu-vs-gpu) explains why). Before a run,
two questions decide whether it is practical. **Does it fit?** Everything the training step needs must be in GPU
memory at the same time. **How long will it take?** That depends on how many operations the model needs per token,
how fast the GPU really runs them, and whether the data arrives fast enough to keep it busy.

Both can be estimated on paper from the model size, the batch and the GPU's datasheet, to within a factor that is
good enough for planning. This lesson builds that estimate for the four GPT-2 sizes, labels every part of it as an
estimate, and then shows the PyTorch settings that move each number.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="Where the memory and the time go">

**Model size.** A GPT-2-style transformer with $L$ layers, hidden size $h$, vocabulary $V = 50{,}257$ and context
$s = 1024$ has

$$
P = 12 L h^2 + 13 L h + (V + s) h + 2h
$$

parameters: GPT-2 small ($L = 12$, $h = 768$) has 124,439,808, matching the published count.

**Memory, part 1: states.** Training with AdamW keeps, for every parameter, a float32 weight (4 bytes), a float32
gradient (4) and two float32 moment estimates (8): **16 bytes per parameter**, about 2 GB for GPT-2 small and 25 GB
for GPT-2 XL. <Term id="mixed-precision">Mixed precision</Term> with `torch.autocast` does not change this: the
weights stay float32 and only the computation is cast.

**Memory, part 2: activations.** The backward pass needs values saved during the forward pass. Korthikanti et al.
(2022) count, for one layer with batch $b$ and $a$ attention heads, with 16-bit activations:

$$
\text{activations per layer} = s\,b\,h\left(34 + \frac{5\,a\,s}{h}\right) \text{ bytes}
$$

The $5as/h$ term is the attention scores. Fused attention kernels, such as the one behind PyTorch's
`scaled_dot_product_attention`, recompute the scores instead of storing them, which removes that term. In float32
the activations take about twice as much. With <Term id="activation-checkpointing">activation checkpointing</Term>
only each layer's input ($2sbh$ bytes) is kept, plus one layer's full activations while it is recomputed.

**Memory, part 3: logits.** The output layer produces $b \cdot s \cdot V$ scores; the estimate counts them once in
float32 ($4bsV$ bytes), 3.3 GB at batch 16. Real implementations may hold more copies (for example the gradient
of the logits), so this part is a lower bound.

**Time.** A training step on $b \cdot s$ tokens costs about

$$
\text{FLOPs} \approx 6 \cdot P \cdot b \cdot s
$$

(2 per parameter per token forward, about 4 backward; Kaplan et al., 2020), or $8 P b s$ with checkpointing, which
repeats the forward pass. The GPU does not run at its datasheet peak; the share it achieves is the
<Term id="model-flops-utilization">model FLOPs utilization</Term> $u$, often 30 to 50% for well-tuned
transformer training and lower for small models. So

$$
t_\text{compute} = \frac{\text{FLOPs}}{u \cdot \text{peak}}, \qquad
t_\text{load} = \frac{b}{\text{loader samples per second}}, \qquad
t_\text{step} = \begin{cases} \max(t_\text{compute}, t_\text{load}) & \text{overlapped} \\ t_\text{compute} + t_\text{load} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
$$

For GPT-2 small on an A100 (312 TFLOPS dense bfloat16) at 40% utilization, batch 16: 0.098 s of compute per step,
167,149 tokens per second, 16.6 hours for 10 billion tokens.

| GPU | Memory | float32 | 16-bit tensor cores (dense) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| T4 | 16 GB | 8.1 TFLOPS | 65 TFLOPS float16 (no bfloat16) |
| L4 | 24 GB | 30.3 TFLOPS | 121 TFLOPS bfloat16 |
| A100 80GB | 80 GB | 19.5 TFLOPS | 312 TFLOPS bfloat16 |
| H100 SXM | 80 GB | 67 TFLOPS | 989.5 TFLOPS bfloat16 |

The numbers come from NVIDIA's datasheets (linked in the references). For L4 and H100 the datasheets print the
16-bit figure "with sparsity"; dense training gets half of it, which is what the table shows. The float32 column
is for plain float32 math; A100 and H100 can also run float32 matrix multiplications as TF32 on tensor cores, which
PyTorch leaves off by default.

<Callout kind="simplification">
  This is an estimate, not a measurement. It leaves out the attention-score FLOPs (a few percent here), the CUDA
  context and allocator fragmentation (often a gigabyte or more), temporary buffers, and the fact that utilization
  itself drops at small batch sizes, which the fixed utilization slider does not model. Use it to rule options in
  or out, then measure the real run with `torch.cuda.max_memory_allocated()` and a synchronized timer.
</Callout>

Things to try:

- Switch the precision to float32. Activations double to 10.27 GB, and the step takes 16 times as long (1.568 s)
  because the A100's float32 rate is 19.5 TFLOPS instead of 312.
- Switch the attention kernel to naive. The stored scores more than triple the activations, from 5.13 to 17.21 GB.
- Choose GPT-2 XL. At batch 16 it needs 71.00 GB, just under 80. The states alone are 24.92 GB.
- Choose the T4 with GPT-2 small. Batch 16 needs 10.42 GB with mixed precision and 15.55 GB in float32, just
  under 16; at batch 20 float32 no longer fits (18.94 GB). In float32 the T4 needs 640 hours for 10 billion tokens.
- Drop the loader to 50 samples per second. Loading takes 0.320 s, the GPU is busy 31% of the step and 10 billion
  tokens take 54.3 hours instead of 16.6. Then switch to the H100: even at 400 samples per second the loader
  becomes the bottleneck (GPU busy 77%).
- Turn on checkpointing. Activations drop from 5.13 to 0.73 GB and the time rises from 16.6 to 22.2 hours.

<Section kind="mistakes" title="Measuring and fitting">

<CommonMistake
  title="Timing GPU code without synchronizing"
  wrong={'start = time.perf_counter()\nfor _ in range(100):\n    loss = train_step(batch)\nprint(time.perf_counter() - start)  # suspiciously fast'}
  error="No error. The printed time is mostly how long Python took to queue the work."
  cause="CUDA calls are asynchronous: they return as soon as the kernel is queued. The GPU may still be working when the clock is read."
  fix={'for _ in range(10):\n    train_step(batch)          # warm-up: kernel selection, allocator\ntorch.cuda.synchronize()\nstart = time.perf_counter()\nfor _ in range(100):\n    loss = train_step(batch)\ntorch.cuda.synchronize()\nprint((time.perf_counter() - start) / 100, "s per step")'}
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Using float16 without loss scaling"
  wrong={'with torch.autocast(device_type="cuda", dtype=torch.float16):\n    loss = loss_fn(model(x), y)\nloss.backward()\noptimizer.step()'}
  error="Often no error, but small gradients round to zero in float16 and training stalls or diverges."
  cause="float16 has a narrow exponent range: values below about 6e-8 become zero."
  fix={'scaler = torch.amp.GradScaler("cuda")\n...\nscaler.scale(loss).backward()\nscaler.step(optimizer)\nscaler.update()\n# or use dtype=torch.bfloat16 where the GPU supports it'}
/>

<Callout kind="warning">
  When a run runs out of memory, reduce the batch and use gradient accumulation (several small batches, one
  optimizer step) to keep the effective batch size. Changing the effective batch changes the training dynamics,
  so the learning rate may need to change with it.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section kind="practice" title="Making a real run fast">

- **Profile before optimizing.** Watch GPU utilization (`nvidia-smi`) during training; if it dips between steps,
  the input pipeline is the bottleneck. The PyTorch profiler shows where each step's time goes.
- **Feed the GPU.** `DataLoader(num_workers=..., pin_memory=True)` and `.to(device, non_blocking=True)` overlap
  loading and copying with compute. Decode and tokenize once, ahead of time, rather than every epoch.
- **Use the tensor cores.** Mixed precision (bfloat16 on A100, H100 and L4) is usually the single largest speedup.
  Matrix dimensions that are multiples of 8 or 64 help the kernels.
- **Fit more.** Fused attention, activation checkpointing and gradient accumulation, in roughly that order. Past
  one GPU, data parallelism and sharded optimizer states (ZeRO, PyTorch FSDP) split the 16 bytes per parameter
  across devices.
- **Record the hardware with the run.** The GPU type, precision and batch size belong in the run record (see
  [experiment tracking](#/learn/ml/practice/experiment-tracking)): they change speed, memory and sometimes results.

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

- Training memory is states (16 bytes per parameter with AdamW), activations (grow with batch and sequence) and
  smaller buffers. Activations usually decide the batch size.
- Training compute is about 6 · parameters · tokens FLOPs; time is that divided by the achieved throughput, a
  fraction of the datasheet peak.
- Mixed precision speeds up compute and halves activations; float16 needs loss scaling, bfloat16 does not.
- Activation checkpointing trades about a third more compute for much less activation memory.
- If loading is slower than compute, the GPU waits. Overlap loading and make the pipeline faster before buying a
  faster GPU.
- Every number here is an estimate. Measure the real run, with synchronization, before relying on it.

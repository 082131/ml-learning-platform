<Section kind="intuition" title="Many knobs, one error">

A model has many weights, and its error depends on all of them. To improve it you want to know, for each weight
separately, "if I nudge this one a little, does the error go up or down, and how much?" That is a
<Term id="partial-derivative">partial derivative</Term>: an ordinary
<Term id="derivative">derivative</Term> with every other weight held still. Listing them all gives the
<Term id="gradient">gradient</Term>, an arrow in weight space that points uphill.

The error is not a direct formula of the weights, though. It is built in steps: weights make a prediction, the
prediction makes an error, the error makes a loss. The <Term id="chain-rule">chain rule</Term> says how to get the
derivative through such a chain: multiply the derivatives of the individual steps. Doing that from the loss back to
every weight is called backpropagation, and it is how every neural network is trained.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="Partial derivatives, the gradient and the chain rule">

**Partial derivatives.** For a function of several inputs, $\partial f / \partial w$ is the derivative with respect
to $w$ while every other input is treated as a constant. For $f(w, b) = 3w^2 + wb$:

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial w} = 6w + b \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial b} = w
$$

**The gradient** collects them in a vector: $\nabla f = \left(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial w}, \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial b}\right)$.
At $(w, b) = (1, 4)$ it is $(10, 1)$. It points in the direction in which $f$ increases fastest, and its length
says how steep that is. So $-\nabla f$ is the direction of steepest descent, the direction training moves in.

**The chain rule.** If $L$ depends on $e$, and $e$ depends on $w$, then

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial e} \cdot \frac{\partial e}{\partial w}
$$

and longer chains multiply more factors. Each factor is a *local* derivative: how one step's output responds to
its own input, which is usually trivial (1 for an addition, the other factor for a multiplication).

**A model as a <Term id="computation-graph">computation graph</Term>.** A one-weight linear model with a squared
error is

$$
p = w x, \quad \hat y = p + b, \quad e = \hat y - y, \quad L = e^2
$$

With $w = 1$, $x = 2$, $b = 0$ and target $y = 6$: $p = 2$, $\hat y = 2$, $e = -4$, $L = 16$. Going backward:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial e} = 2e = -8, \quad
\frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial e} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\partial e}{\partial \hat y}}_{1} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\partial \hat y}{\partial b}}_{1} = -8, \quad
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = -8 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\partial p}{\partial w}}_{x\,=\,2} = -16
$$

Both partials are negative: increasing $w$ or $b$ raises the prediction toward 6 and lowers the loss. The gradient
is $(-16, -8)$, and the steepest way down is $(16, 8)$.

<Callout kind="simplification">
  Real networks have millions of nodes, and libraries such as PyTorch build the graph automatically while the
  forward pass runs, then apply exactly these local rules backward (automatic differentiation). The arithmetic is
  the same; only the bookkeeping is automated.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section kind="mistakes" title="Missing factors and mismatched shapes">

<Callout kind="warning">
  The most common hand-derivation bug raises no error: dropping a factor of the chain. Writing
  $\partial L / \partial w = 2e$ forgets $\partial p / \partial w = x$; it gives $-8$ instead of $-16$ in the
  example. A gradient check like the one above catches it.
</Callout>

<Section kind="practice" title="A whole batch at once">

With many examples, the loss is the mean squared error and the gradient is the mean of the per-example gradients.
NumPy computes all of them with one matrix product:

<Code example={batchGradient} title="batch_gradient.py" />

$\partial L / \partial w_j = \frac{2}{n} \sum_i x_{ij}\,e_i$ is the single-example $2e \cdot x$, averaged. Check
the first weight: $\frac{2}{4}\big(1 \cdot (-2) + 2 \cdot (-1) + 3 \cdot (-4) + 0 \cdot (-3)\big) = \frac{2}{4} \cdot (-16) = -8$.
The same pattern, repeated layer by layer, is
[Backpropagation](#/learn/ml/deep/backpropagation).

<Section kind="summary">

- A partial derivative holds every other input fixed; the gradient lists all of them.
- The gradient points uphill; $-\nabla L$ is the direction of steepest descent.
- Chain rule: multiply local derivatives along the path from the loss to a parameter.
- A backward pass computes every gradient in one sweep from the loss to the inputs.
- For $L = (wx + b - y)^2$: $\partial L / \partial w = 2e\,x$ and $\partial L / \partial b = 2e$.

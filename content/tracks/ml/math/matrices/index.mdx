<Section kind="intuition" title="A spreadsheet of numbers">

A spreadsheet with one row per house and one column per feature is already a
<Term id="matrix">matrix</Term>: a rectangular grid of numbers. Every row is one house's feature
<Term id="vector">vector</Term>; every column is one feature across all houses.

Three things you do with a spreadsheet carry straight over. You look up a cell by its row and column. You add two
sheets of the same layout cell by cell. And you sometimes flip the sheet so rows become columns, which is called
the <Term id="transpose">transpose</Term>.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="Shape, indices, addition and transpose">

**Shape.** A matrix with $m$ rows and $n$ columns has <Term id="shape">shape</Term> $(m, n)$, rows first.

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{shape } (2, 3)
$$

**Indexing.** $A_{ij}$ is the entry in row $i$ and column $j$, both counted from 0 as in Python. Here
$A_{02} = 3$ and $A_{10} = 4$. In NumPy that is `A[0, 2]` and `A[1, 0]`; `A[1]` is the whole row
$[4, 5, 6]$ and `A[:, 1]` the whole column $[2, 5]$.

**Addition** is <Term id="element-wise">element-wise</Term> and needs equal shapes:

$$
(A + B)_{ij} = A_{ij} + B_{ij}
\qquad
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix} +
\begin{bmatrix} 10 & 0 & -1 \\ 2 & 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} =
\begin{bmatrix} 11 & 2 & 2 \\ 6 & 8 & 10 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Multiplying by a scalar also works entry by entry: $(kA)_{ij} = k\,A_{ij}$.

**Transpose.** $A^\top$ swaps rows and columns:

$$
(A^\top)_{ij} = A_{ji}
\qquad
A^\top = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 2 & 5 \\ 3 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{shape } (3, 2)
$$

Row 0 of $A$, $[1, 2, 3]$, is column 0 of $A^\top$. The shape $(m, n)$ becomes $(n, m)$, entries on the diagonal
($i = j$) stay put, and transposing twice gives back $A$: $(A^\top)^\top = A$.

<Callout kind="simplification">
  NumPy's `A.T` doesn't copy any numbers. It returns a view that reads the same memory with the row and column
  steps swapped, which is why it is instant even for huge arrays. Changing an entry of `A.T` changes `A`.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section kind="mistakes" title="Shapes that don’t match, indices that don’t exist">

<CommonMistake
  title="Adding a matrix to its transpose"
  wrong={'A = np.array([[1, 2, 3], [4, 5, 6]])  # (2, 3)\nA + A.T'}
  error="ValueError: operands could not be broadcast together with shapes (2,3) (3,2) "
  cause="Element-wise addition pairs entries in the same position, so both matrices need the same shape. A.T has shape (3, 2)."
  fix={'print(A.shape, A.T.shape)  # add only matrices with equal shapes'}
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Indexing past the last row"
  wrong={'A = np.array([[1, 2, 3], [4, 5, 6]])\nA[2, 0]'}
  error="IndexError: index 2 is out of bounds for axis 0 with size 2"
  cause="A has 2 rows, numbered 0 and 1. Axis 0 is the row axis; NumPy tells you which axis and its size."
  fix={'A[1, 0]  # the last row is A.shape[0] - 1'}
/>

<Section kind="summary">

- A matrix of shape $(m, n)$ has $m$ rows and $n$ columns; `A[i, j]` is row $i$, column $j$, from 0.
- Addition and scalar multiplication work entry by entry; addition needs equal shapes.
- $A^\top$ swaps rows and columns: $(A^\top)_{ij} = A_{ji}$, shape $(n, m)$.
- A dataset is a matrix with one row per example and one column per feature.

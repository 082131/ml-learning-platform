<Section kind="intuition" title="A shopping bill for several people at several stores">

Two friends are shopping for the same three items: apples, bread and cheese. Friend 0 wants 1, 2 and 3 of them;
friend 1 wants 4, 5 and 6. Two stores sell all three items at different prices. How much would each friend pay at
each store?

For one friend at one store, you pair each quantity with that store's price, multiply, and add:
friend 0 at store 0 pays $1 \cdot 7 + 2 \cdot 9 + 3 \cdot 11 = 58$. Doing that for every friend and every store
fills a small table of totals, one number per (friend, store) pair.

|          | Store 0                    | Store 1                     |
| -------- | -------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| Friend 0 | 1·7 + 2·9 + 3·11 = **58**  | 1·8 + 2·10 + 3·12 = **64**  |
| Friend 1 | 4·7 + 5·9 + 6·11 = **139** | 4·8 + 5·10 + 6·12 = **154** |

That table is a <Term id="matrix-multiplication">matrix product</Term>. The quantities form one
<Term id="matrix">matrix</Term> (a grid of numbers: a row per friend, a column per item), the prices form
another (a row per item, a column per store), and the totals are the result. Each total is a
<Term id="dot-product">dot product</Term>: pair up two lists of numbers, multiply the pairs, and add.

The same pattern runs inside every neural network: a row of input features is combined with a column of weights,
for every input and every output at once.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="Row of A times column of B">

Call the quantities $A$ and the prices $B$. $A$ has 2 rows and 3 columns, so its
<Term id="shape">shape</Term> is $(2, 3)$. $B$ has shape $(3, 2)$.

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix}
\qquad
B = \begin{bmatrix} 7 & 8 \\ 9 & 10 \\ 11 & 12 \end{bmatrix}
$$

**Definition.** If $A$ has shape $(m, n)$ and $B$ has shape $(n, p)$, their product $C = AB$ has shape $(m, p)$,
and the entry in row $i$, column $j$ is

$$
C_{ij} = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} A_{ik} \, B_{kj} = A_{i0} B_{0j} + A_{i1} B_{1j} + \dots + A_{i,n-1} B_{n-1,j}
$$

In words: walk along row $i$ of $A$ and down column $j$ of $B$ at the same time, multiply the numbers you meet,
and add the products. The index $k$ is the position you are at in both. Indices start at 0 here, as they do in
Python.

**Worked example.** $C_{10}$ uses row 1 of $A$, $[4, 5, 6]$, and column 0 of $B$, $[7, 9, 11]$:

$$
C_{10} = 4 \cdot 7 + 5 \cdot 9 + 6 \cdot 11 = 28 + 45 + 66 = 139
$$

Doing this for all four positions gives

$$
C = AB = \begin{bmatrix} 58 & 64 \\ 139 & 154 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \text{shape } (2, 2)
$$

**The shape rule.** Row $i$ of $A$ has $n$ numbers and column $j$ of $B$ has as many numbers as $B$ has rows,
so pairing them only works when those counts are equal:

$$
\underbrace{(m, \; n)}_{A} \times \underbrace{(n, \; p)}_{B} \;\rightarrow\; \underbrace{(m, \; p)}_{C}
$$

The inner sizes must match and disappear; the outer sizes give the result. $(2, 3) \times (3, 2)$ gives
$(2, 2)$, while $(2, 3) \times (2, 3)$ is not defined.

**Order matters.** $BA$ has shape $(3, 3)$ here, a different matrix altogether. In general $AB \neq BA$, even
when both exist.

**Cost.** $C$ has $m \cdot p$ entries and each needs $n$ multiply-adds, so the product takes $m \cdot n \cdot p$
multiply-adds: $2 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 = 12$ here. Counting each multiply and each add separately gives the
$2mnp$ floating-point operations (FLOPs) that engineers use to estimate how long a layer takes.

<Callout kind="simplification">
  The loop in the experiment below shows the definition, not how libraries compute it. NumPy hands the work to
  an optimized linear algebra library (a BLAS) that reorders and splits the multiply-adds for speed. The
  result is the same up to floating-point rounding, which is why results for large float matrices can differ
  in the last digits between machines.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section kind="mistakes" title="Shape errors and the wrong operator">

<CommonMistake
  title="Multiplying matrices whose inner sizes differ"
  wrong={'A = np.array([[1, 2, 3], [4, 5, 6]])  # shape (2, 3)\nA @ A'}
  error="ValueError: matmul: Input operand 1 has a mismatch in its core dimension 0, with gufunc signature (n?,k),(k,m?)->(n?,m?) (size 2 is different from 3)"
  cause="A @ B needs the column count of A to equal the row count of B. Here the left A has 3 columns but the right A has 2 rows. NumPy calls the shared size `k` in the signature."
  fix={'A @ A.T  # (2, 3) @ (3, 2) -> (2, 2)'}
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Using * when you mean matrix multiplication"
  wrong={
    'A = np.array([[1, 2, 3], [4, 5, 6]])           # (2, 3)\nB = np.array([[7, 8], [9, 10], [11, 12]])     # (3, 2)\nA * B'
  }
  error="ValueError: operands could not be broadcast together with shapes (2,3) (3,2) "
  cause="On NumPy arrays `*` multiplies element by element, so it needs arrays of the same shape (or shapes that broadcast). It never sums over a shared dimension."
  fix={'A @ B  # matrix product, shape (2, 2)'}
/>

<Callout kind="warning">
  Some mistakes raise no error. If `A` and `B` are both square, `A * B` and `B @ A` run fine but give a
  different matrix from `A @ B`. Print `.shape` and check one entry by hand when a result looks wrong.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section kind="practice" title="Where you will meet it">

A layer of a neural network multiplies a batch of inputs by a weight matrix. With 32 images of 784 pixels each and
a layer with 128 outputs:

<Code example={linearLayer} title="linear_layer.py" />

The batch $X$ has shape $(32, 784)$ and the weights $W$ have shape $(784, 128)$, so $H = XW$ has shape
$(32, 128)$: one row of 128 outputs per image. That takes $32 \cdot 784 \cdot 128 = 3{,}211{,}264$
multiply-adds, which is why ML runs on hardware built to do many of them in parallel.

| Where                             | Product                            | Shapes                                                                                            |
| --------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| A dense (fully connected) layer   | inputs × weights                   | $(\text{batch}, \text{in}) \times (\text{in}, \text{out}) \rightarrow (\text{batch}, \text{out})$ |
| Attention scores in a transformer | queries × keys transposed          | $(\text{seq}, d) \times (d, \text{seq}) \rightarrow (\text{seq}, \text{seq})$                     |
| Comparing many embeddings at once | embeddings × embeddings transposed | $(N, d) \times (d, N) \rightarrow (N, N)$                                                         |

These come back in [Layers and forward propagation](#/learn/ml/deep/forward-propagation) and
[Attention](#/learn/ml/deep/attention).

<Section kind="summary">

- $C = AB$ is defined when $A$ is $(m, n)$ and $B$ is $(n, p)$. The result is $(m, p)$.
- Each entry $C_{ij}$ is the dot product of row $i$ of $A$ and column $j$ of $B$.
- The product costs $m \cdot n \cdot p$ multiply-adds. Order matters: $AB \neq BA$ in general.
- In NumPy, use `@`. `*` multiplies element by element.
- Check `.shape` first when something goes wrong.

Next: [Tensors and shapes](#/learn/ml/math/tensors) extends shapes to more than two dimensions, such as a batch
of images, where the same row-by-column rule is applied to every item in the batch.

</Section>

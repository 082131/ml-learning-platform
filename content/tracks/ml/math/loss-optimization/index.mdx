<Section kind="intuition" title="Downhill in the fog">

Training a model means choosing its <Term id="parameter">parameters</Term> so that its predictions are good. To
make "good" precise you pick a <Term id="loss-function">loss function</Term>: one number that is large when the
predictions are far from the targets and small when they are close. Training is then a search for the parameters
with the smallest loss.

You cannot see the whole loss landscape, any more than a hiker in thick fog can see the valley floor. But you can
feel the slope under your feet: that is the <Term id="gradient">gradient</Term>.
<Term id="gradient-descent">Gradient descent</Term> takes a small step downhill, feels the slope again, and
repeats. How big each step is, the <Term id="learning-rate">learning rate</Term>, decides whether you walk down
steadily, crawl, or leap clean over the valley.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="Loss functions and the update rule">

**Mean squared error.** For predictions $\hat y_i$ and targets $y_i$, $i = 1 \dots n$:

$$
L = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n (\hat y_i - y_i)^2
$$

Squaring makes every error count as positive and punishes large errors more than small ones. It is the standard
<Term id="mean-squared-error">loss for regression</Term>; classification uses cross-entropy instead, which you meet
with logistic regression.

**Gradient descent** updates every parameter $\theta$ against its partial derivative, with learning rate $\eta$:

$$
\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \, \frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta}
$$

For $f(x) = (x - 2)^2 + 1$, $f'(x) = 2(x - 2)$, so from $x_0 = 6$ with $\eta = 0.25$:
$x_1 = 6 - 0.25 \cdot 8 = 4$, $x_2 = 4 - 0.25 \cdot 4 = 3$. Each step halves the distance to the minimum at 2.

**The learning rate.** On this function each step multiplies the distance to the minimum by $1 - 2\eta$. So
$\eta = 0.5$ lands on the minimum in one step, $0.5 < \eta < 1$ overshoots but still converges, $\eta = 1$ bounces
between 6 and −2 forever, and $\eta > 1$ diverges. For a general loss the safe range depends on how sharply it
curves: $\eta < 2 / \text{curvature}$, along the most sharply curved direction.

**Convex or not.** A <Term id="convex-function">convex</Term> loss has a single valley, so any point with zero
gradient is the global minimum. Mean squared error for a linear model is convex. Neural network losses are not:
gradient descent can settle in a <Term id="local-minimum">local minimum</Term>, and where it ends depends on where
it starts.

</Section>

- Lower $\eta$ to 0.02: the path is smooth but crawls along the valley floor and is far from the minimum after

<Section kind="mistakes" title="When the learning rate is too large">

<CommonMistake
  title="Diverging until the numbers overflow"
  wrong={'x = 6.0\nlr = 1.5\nfor step in range(1000):\n    x = x - lr * df(x)\n    loss = f(x)'}
  error="OverflowError: (34, 'Numerical result out of range')"
  cause="With η = 1.5 each step multiplies the distance to the minimum by −2, so x doubles in size every step. After about 500 steps (x − 2) ** 2 is too large for a float and Python raises."
  fix={'lr = 0.1  # below 1 for this function: each step shrinks the distance to the minimum'}
/>

<Callout kind="warning">
  Libraries such as NumPy and PyTorch do not raise on overflow: the loss becomes `inf` and then `nan`, and training
  carries on silently. If the loss goes up, or turns into `nan`, lower the learning rate first.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section kind="practice" title="Choosing a learning rate in practice">

There is no formula for the best learning rate of a real model. A common recipe:

1. Try a few values spaced by factors of 10 (0.1, 0.01, 0.001) and plot the loss per step for each.
2. Keep the largest one whose loss goes down steadily, without spikes.
3. If the loss falls fast and then stalls, lower the rate later in training (a learning-rate schedule).

The contour map shows why a single rate is a compromise: the steepest direction limits how large it can be, and
the gentlest direction then takes many steps. Optimizers such as momentum and Adam, covered in the deep learning
track, adapt the step per direction to ease exactly this.

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

- A loss function turns "how wrong" into one number; mean squared error is the regression default.
- Gradient descent repeats $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \, \partial L / \partial \theta$.
- Too small a learning rate crawls; too large overshoots and diverges.
- Convex losses have one minimum; non-convex ones can trap descent in a local minimum.

<Section kind="intuition" title="Uncertain, but not lawless">

You cannot say what the next roll of a die will be, but you can say a lot about the next ten thousand: each face
will come up about a sixth of the time, and the average will be close to 3.5. Probability is the language for that
kind of statement. Machine learning uses it everywhere: data are samples from some unknown process, a classifier
outputs probabilities, and training itself shuffles and samples at random.

A <Term id="random-variable">random variable</Term> is a quantity whose value depends on chance, such as the face of
a die. Its <Term id="probability-distribution">distribution</Term> lists what values it can take and how likely
each one is. Its <Term id="expected-value">expected value</Term> is the long-run average, and the
<Term id="law-of-large-numbers">law of large numbers</Term> says a sample's average really does settle there.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="Distributions and expected value">

**Probabilities** are numbers between 0 and 1. For a discrete random variable $X$, the probabilities of all its
possible values sum to 1. The probability of an event (a set of outcomes) is the sum over its outcomes: for a fair
die, $P(X > 4) = P(5) + P(6) = \tfrac{2}{6}$.

**A discrete distribution** is that table of values and probabilities. Three examples used below:

| Distribution | Values | Probabilities |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fair die | 1 to 6 | $\tfrac16$ each |
| Loaded die | 1 to 6 | 0.1 each for 1 to 5, 0.5 for 6 |
| Heads in 10 fair flips | 0 to 10 | $\binom{10}{k} / 2^{10}$ (binomial) |

**The expected value** weights each value by its probability:

$$
E[X] = \sum_x x \cdot P(X = x)
$$

Fair die: $\tfrac16 (1 + 2 + \dots + 6) = 3.5$. Loaded die: $0.1 \cdot 15 + 0.5 \cdot 6 = 4.5$. Ten flips:
$10 \cdot 0.5 = 5$. The expected value need not be a possible outcome; no die shows 3.5.

**The law of large numbers.** If $x_1, \dots, x_n$ are independent draws of $X$, the sample mean
$\bar x = \tfrac1n \sum_i x_i$ gets closer to $E[X]$ as $n$ grows, and each outcome's observed frequency gets
closer to its probability. The typical error shrinks in proportion to $1/\sqrt{n}$: a hundred times more data
gives about ten times less error.

**Seeds.** Computers produce pseudo-random numbers: a deterministic sequence that looks random. A
<Term id="random-seed">seed</Term> picks where the sequence starts, so the same seed reproduces the same draws.

</Section>

<Section kind="mistakes" title="Probabilities that do not add up">

<CommonMistake
  title="Probabilities that do not sum to 1"
  wrong={'rng = np.random.default_rng(0)\nrng.choice([1, 2, 3], size=5, p=[0.5, 0.3, 0.3])'}
  error="ValueError: Probabilities do not sum to 1. See Notes section of docstring for more information."
  cause="A distribution's probabilities must add up to 1; these add up to 1.1."
  fix={'rng.choice([1, 2, 3], size=5, p=[0.5, 0.3, 0.2])'}
/>

<Callout kind="warning">
  The gambler's fallacy: after a run of sixes, a fair die is not "due" a low number. Draws are independent. The
  law of large numbers works by dilution, not compensation: a lucky start matters less and less as $n$ grows.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

- A distribution lists a random variable's values and their probabilities, which sum to 1.
- $E[X] = \sum_x x \cdot P(X = x)$ is the long-run average.
- Sample frequencies and means approach the distribution's as $n$ grows; the error shrinks like $1/\sqrt n$.
- A seed makes random experiments reproducible.

<Section kind="intuition" title="How much do two arrows agree?">

Push a box with a rope. If you pull in the direction the box can slide, all your effort counts. Pull at an angle
and only part of it does; pull straight sideways and none of it does. The
<Term id="dot-product">dot product</Term> measures exactly this agreement between two
<Term id="vector">vectors</Term>: large and positive when they point the same way, zero when they are at a right
angle, negative when they point apart.

In machine learning the same number answers "how similar are these two examples?" Two documents, two users or two
word embeddings are vectors, and their dot product (scaled for length) says how alike they are.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="Products, lengths and the angle">

**Definition.** For two vectors with the same number of components,

$$
u \cdot v = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} u_k v_k = u_0 v_0 + u_1 v_1 + \dots + u_{n-1} v_{n-1}
$$

The result is a scalar. For $u = (3, 1)$ and $v = (1, 2)$: $u \cdot v = 3 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 2 = 3 + 2 = 5$.

**Length.** The dot product of a vector with itself is the sum of its squared components, so its square root is
the arrow's length (Pythagoras), called the <Term id="norm">norm</Term>:

$$
\lVert u \rVert = \sqrt{u \cdot u} \qquad \lVert (3, 1) \rVert = \sqrt{9 + 1} = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.1623
$$

**Angle.** Geometry gives a second formula for the same number, with $\theta$ the angle between the arrows:

$$
u \cdot v = \lVert u \rVert \, \lVert v \rVert \cos\theta
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\cos\theta = \frac{u \cdot v}{\lVert u \rVert \, \lVert v \rVert}
$$

For the example, $\lVert v \rVert = \sqrt{1 + 4} = \sqrt{5}$, so
$\cos\theta = 5 / (\sqrt{10} \cdot \sqrt{5}) = 5 / \sqrt{50} \approx 0.7071$, and $\theta = 45°$.

Since lengths are positive, the sign of $u \cdot v$ is the sign of $\cos\theta$:

| $u \cdot v$ | $\cos\theta$ | Angle             | Meaning                     |
| ----------- | ------------ | ----------------- | --------------------------- |
| $> 0$       | $(0, 1]$     | less than 90°     | roughly the same direction  |
| $= 0$       | $0$          | exactly 90°       | <Term id="orthogonal">orthogonal</Term> (perpendicular) |
| $< 0$       | $[-1, 0)$    | more than 90°     | roughly opposite directions |

**Cosine similarity** is $\cos\theta$ used as a similarity score. It ignores length: $(1, 2)$ and $(2, 4)$ have
similarity 1. A vector of length 0 has no direction, so its cosine is undefined.

<Callout kind="simplification">
  The angle picture is exact in 2-D and 3-D. In 300 dimensions you can't draw it, but the formula is the same,
  and "angle" just means the number $\arccos(\cos\theta)$.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section kind="mistakes" title="Element-wise products and exact comparisons">

<CommonMistake
  title="Dot product of vectors with different lengths"
  wrong={'np.dot(np.array([1, 2]), np.array([1, 2, 3]))'}
  error="ValueError: shapes (2,) and (3,) not aligned: 2 (dim 0) != 3 (dim 0)"
  cause="Every component of one vector needs a partner in the other. NumPy reports the two shapes and the sizes that disagree."
  fix={'print(u.shape, v.shape)  # make both vectors use the same features'}
/>

<Callout kind="warning">
  `u * v` is not the dot product. On arrays it is element-wise: `np.array([3, 1]) * np.array([1, 2])` gives
  `[3 2]`, a vector. The dot product is the sum of that vector: `np.dot(u, v)`, `u @ v`, or `(u * v).sum()`.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="warning">
  Don't compare a computed cosine with `==`. `cos == -1.0` is `False` for $(1, 1)$ and $(-1, -1)$, because the
  float is `-0.9999999999999998`. Use `np.isclose(cos, -1.0)`, which is `True`.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

- $u \cdot v = u_0 v_0 + u_1 v_1 + \dots$, a scalar. Both vectors need the same length.
- $\lVert u \rVert = \sqrt{u \cdot u}$ is the vector's length.
- $\cos\theta = u \cdot v / (\lVert u \rVert \lVert v \rVert)$: positive below 90°, 0 at 90°, negative beyond.
- Cosine similarity compares directions and ignores length; it is undefined for a zero vector.
- In NumPy use `np.dot(u, v)` or `u @ v`; `u * v` is element-wise. Compare floats with `np.isclose`.

<Section kind="intuition" title="A few numbers for a pile of data">

Before training anything, you look at the data. A handful of numbers do most of the work: where the values sit
(the <Term id="mean">mean</Term>), how spread out they are (the <Term id="variance">variance</Term> and
<Term id="standard-deviation">standard deviation</Term>), and whether two columns move together (the
<Term id="correlation">correlation</Term>).

Correlation is also where the most famous trap in statistics lives. On days when more ice cream is sold, more
people get sunburnt. Ice cream does not cause sunburn: sunny summer days cause both. A hidden variable that drives
two others is a <Term id="confounder">confounder</Term>, and a model trained on data like this will happily use
one as a stand-in for the other.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="Mean, spread and correlation">

For values $x_1, \dots, x_n$:

$$
\bar x = \frac1n \sum_i x_i \qquad
\operatorname{Var}(x) = \frac1n \sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2 \qquad
\operatorname{SD}(x) = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(x)}
$$

The variance is in squared units; the standard deviation is back in the units of the data, so it is the one to
quote. Dividing by $n - 1$ instead of $n$ gives the *sample* variance, which corrects a small sample's tendency to
underestimate the spread. NumPy divides by $n$ unless you pass `ddof=1`; pandas divides by $n - 1$ by default.

**Pearson correlation** measures how well a straight line describes the relationship between two variables:

$$
r = \frac{\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)}{\sqrt{\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2}\,\sqrt{\sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2}}
$$

It runs from $-1$ (a perfect falling line) through 0 (no linear trend) to $+1$ (a perfect rising line), and has
no units. Two cautions: $r$ sees only straight lines ($y = x^2$ on $-2 \dots 2$ has $r = 0$), and $r$ says nothing
about cause.

**Confounding.** If $Z$ affects both $X$ and $Y$, then $X$ and $Y$ correlate even when neither affects the other.
One check is to compare like with like: hold $Z$ fixed (look within one season) and see whether the correlation
survives. Only a controlled experiment, where you set $X$ yourself, shows causation directly.

</Section>

<Section kind="practice" title="Reading a correlation">

When you meet a correlation in data, ask three questions before you act on it:

1. **Is it linear?** Plot it. A curve or two clusters can give a misleading $r$.
2. **Is there a third variable?** Season, size, age and time are the usual suspects. Split by it and see if the
   correlation survives.
3. **Could the arrow point the other way?** Cities with more police may have more crime because crime brings
   police.

In machine learning, a confounded feature is a trap for later: a model that learns "lots of ice cream means lots of
sunburn" works until the data come from somewhere where the confounder behaves differently.

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

- The mean is where values sit; the standard deviation is how far they typically are from it, in the data's units.
- `ddof=0` divides by $n$ (NumPy's default); `ddof=1` divides by $n - 1$ (pandas' default).
- Pearson $r$ measures linear association from $-1$ to $1$, and says nothing about cause.
- A confounder drives two variables at once; splitting by it can make the correlation vanish.

<Section kind="intuition" title="Walking downhill in a narrow valley">

Training a network means changing its <Term id="parameter">parameters</Term> to make the
<Term id="loss-function">loss</Term> smaller. The <Term id="gradient">gradient</Term> (the vector of slopes of
the loss, one per parameter) points uphill, so the basic move is a step against it:
<Term id="gradient-descent">gradient descent</Term>. An <Term id="optimizer">optimizer</Term> is the rule that decides how big that step is and in which
direction, using the gradient and possibly what it saw before.

Picture a long, narrow valley. Its walls are steep and its floor slopes gently towards the lowest point. A walker
who always steps straight downhill bounces from wall to wall, because downhill points mostly across the valley,
and makes slow progress along it. Two fixes:

- Remember which way you have been going and keep some of that speed: this is
  <Term id="momentum">momentum</Term>. The bounces across the valley cancel out; the steady slope along it adds up.
- Measure how steep each direction usually is and take steps of similar length in all of them: this is what
  <Term id="adam">Adam</Term> does, on top of a momentum-like average.

This lesson races the three on two small surfaces where you can see every step.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="Three update rules">

Write the parameters as a vector $\theta$ (theta). Here $\theta = (x, y)$ has shape $(2,)$; in a network it holds
every weight and bias. Let $g = \nabla f(\theta)$ be the gradient at the current point and $\eta$ (eta) the
<Term id="learning-rate">learning rate</Term>, the step-size multiplier.

**Gradient descent.** $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\, g$. When $g$ is computed on a random
mini-batch of training data instead of the whole dataset, this is <Term id="sgd">stochastic gradient descent
(SGD)</Term>. Our surfaces are fixed formulas, so here the gradient is exact and SGD is plain gradient descent;
the noise from batches is the subject of the next lesson.

**Momentum** keeps a velocity $u$ that starts at $0$ (PyTorch calls it the momentum buffer):

$$
u \leftarrow \beta\, u + g, \qquad \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\, u
$$

With $\beta = 0.9$, a gradient that stays the same builds the velocity up to
$g\,(1 + 0.9 + 0.81 + \dots) = g / (1 - 0.9) = 10\,g$.

**Adam** keeps two running averages per parameter, $m$ (of $g$) and $v$ (of $g^2$, squared element by element),
both starting at $0$. At step $t = 1, 2, \dots$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
m &\leftarrow \beta_1 m + (1 - \beta_1)\, g, &
v &\leftarrow \beta_2 v + (1 - \beta_2)\, g^2 \\
\hat m &= \frac{m}{1 - \beta_1^t}, &
\hat v &= \frac{v}{1 - \beta_2^t} \\
\theta &\leftarrow \theta - \eta\, \frac{\hat m}{\sqrt{\hat v} + \epsilon}
\end{aligned}
$$

The division by $1 - \beta^t$ is the <Term id="bias-correction">bias correction</Term>: after one step from zero,
$m = 0.1\,g$ is only a tenth of the gradient, and dividing by $1 - 0.9^1 = 0.1$ restores it.

**Worked example** on the bowl $f(x, y) = \tfrac12(x^2 + 10y^2)$, whose gradient is $(x, 10y)$, from
$\theta = (-4, 1.5)$, so $g = (-4, 15)$:

| Optimizer                    | Step 1                                                                                                                       | Result          |
| ---------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------- |
| SGD, $\eta = 0.05$           | $(-4, 1.5) - 0.05 \cdot (-4, 15)$                                                                                            | $(-3.8, 0.75)$  |
| Momentum, $\eta = 0.05$      | $u = 0.9 \cdot (0, 0) + (-4, 15)$, then the same step                                                                        | $(-3.8, 0.75)$  |
| Adam, $\eta = 0.2$           | $m = 0.1 \cdot (-4, 15) = (-0.4, 1.5)$, $v = 0.001 \cdot (16, 225) = (0.016, 0.225)$, $\hat m = (-4, 15)$, $\hat v = (16, 225)$ | $(-3.8, 1.3)$   |

Adam's first step is $0.2 \cdot (-4/4, \; 15/15) = 0.2 \cdot (-1, 1)$: exactly $\eta$ in each coordinate,
whatever the gradient's size. Momentum differs from SGD from step 2:
$u = 0.9 \cdot (-4, 15) + (-3.8, 7.5) = (-7.4, 21)$, so
$\theta = (-3.8, 0.75) - 0.05 \cdot (-7.4, 21) = (-3.43, -0.3)$. It overshoots $y = 0$, while SGD lands on
$(-3.61, 0.375)$.

**When gradient descent diverges.** Along $y$ the gradient is $10y$, so one step maps
$y \mapsto y - 10\eta y = (1 - 10\eta)\, y$. The error shrinks only while $|1 - 10\eta| < 1$, that is for
$\eta < 0.2$. In general a quadratic with curvature $\lambda$ (its second derivative) needs $\eta < 2 / \lambda$,
and the steepest direction sets the limit for all of them. That is the trap of a narrow valley: the steep walls cap
$\eta$, and the gentle floor then takes many small steps.

<Callout kind="simplification">
  These surfaces have two parameters and one valley. A network's loss has millions of parameters, the gradient
  comes from random mini-batches, and frameworks add options we leave out: weight decay, Nesterov momentum,
  AMSGrad, learning-rate schedules. The update rules above are the ones `torch.optim.SGD` (with
  `dampening=0`, `nesterov=False`) and `torch.optim.Adam` (with `weight_decay=0`, `amsgrad=False`) apply.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section kind="mistakes" title="Learning rates that do not transfer">

<CommonMistake
  title="Reusing an SGD learning rate for Adam, or the other way round"
  wrong={'opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=0.5)  # 0.5 worked for SGD'}
  error="No error: the loss jumps around or stops improving."
  cause="SGD steps are η times the gradient, so a good η depends on the gradient's scale. Adam's steps are about η per parameter regardless of scale, so η = 0.5 moves every weight by up to 0.5 each step, far too much for most networks."
  fix={'opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3)  # PyTorch\'s default; tune from there'}
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Forgetting the bias correction in a hand-written Adam"
  wrong={'theta = theta - lr * m / (np.sqrt(v) + eps)  # m and v without the hats'}
  error="No error: the first steps have the wrong size."
  cause="At t = 1, m = 0.1·g but √v = √0.001·|g| ≈ 0.032·|g|, so the step is about 3.2·η instead of η, and the error fades only as βᵗ goes to 0."
  fix={'m_hat = m / (1 - beta1 ** t)\nv_hat = v / (1 - beta2 ** t)\ntheta = theta - lr * m_hat / (np.sqrt(v_hat) + eps)'}
/>

<Callout kind="warning">
  Divergence is not always an exploding number. At η = 0.2 on the bowl, SGD's y flips between 1.5 and −1.5
  forever without growing: the loss stays flat. A loss curve that refuses to go down can mean the learning rate
  is too high, not too low.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section kind="practice" title="Choosing an optimizer">

| Situation                                    | Common choice                                         | Why                                                         |
| -------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| A new network, little tuning time            | Adam (or AdamW), `lr=1e-3`                            | Works with little tuning because steps are scale-free        |
| Image models trained for a long time         | SGD with momentum 0.9 and a learning-rate schedule    | Often generalizes a little better once tuned                 |
| Transformers                                 | AdamW with warm-up and decay                          | AdamW is Adam with weight decay applied separately           |

Whatever you pick, try a few learning rates spaced by about 3× (1e-4, 3e-4, 1e-3, 3e-3) and compare loss
curves: the next lesson shows how to read them. These are starting points, not rules.

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

- SGD: θ ← θ − η·g. On a quadratic with curvature λ it needs η &lt; 2 / λ, and the steepest direction sets that limit.
- Momentum: u ← β·u + g, θ ← θ − η·u. Consistent directions speed up; zigzags cancel.
- Adam: running averages m and v, bias-corrected by 1 − βᵗ, and a step of η·m̂ / (√v̂ + ε): about η per parameter.
- Learning rates are optimizer-specific: Adam's default is 1e-3.
- In PyTorch: `opt.zero_grad()`, `loss.backward()`, `opt.step()`.

Next: [The training loop](#/learn/ml/deep/training-loop) puts an optimizer inside a loop over mini-batches and
epochs, where the gradient becomes noisy.

</Section>

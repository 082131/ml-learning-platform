<Section kind="intuition" title="Who is to blame, and by how much?">

The network makes a prediction, and the <Term id="loss-function">loss</Term> says how wrong it is. Training needs
to know, for every weight, which way to nudge it and how strongly that nudge would change the loss: the
<Term id="gradient">gradient</Term> of the loss with respect to that weight.

You could find it by nudging each weight and rerunning the network, but a real network has millions of weights.
<Term id="backpropagation">Backpropagation</Term> gets all of them in one backward sweep. It starts at the loss
and walks the forward computation in reverse, using the <Term id="chain-rule">chain rule</Term> at every step:
the blame that reaches a unit is split among the weights and inputs that fed it, in proportion to how much each
one affected it.

This lesson does it by hand on the smallest network that still has a hidden layer: 2 inputs, 2 hidden units, 1
output.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="The chain rule, layer by layer">

**The network and the loss.** One example $x = (0.5, -1)$ with target $y = 1$. The hidden layer uses tanh, the
output a sigmoid $\sigma$, and the loss is the <Term id="mean-squared-error">mean squared error</Term> over the one
prediction:

$$
z_1 = x W_1 + b_1, \quad a_1 = \tanh(z_1), \quad z_2 = a_1 W_2 + b_2, \quad \hat y = \sigma(z_2), \quad L = (\hat y - y)^2
$$

With $W_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 0.1 & -0.2 \\ 0.4 & 0.3 \end{bmatrix}$, $b_1 = (0, 0.1)$,
$W_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 \\ -0.6 \end{bmatrix}$ and $b_2 = 0.2$, the forward pass gives
$z_1 = (-0.35, -0.3)$, $a_1 = (-0.3364, -0.2913)$, $z_2 = 0.2066$, $\hat y = 0.5515$ and
$L = (0.5515 - 1)^2 = 0.2012$.

**Backward, one link at a time.** Each line multiplies the gradient that arrived from the right by one local
<Term id="partial-derivative">partial derivative</Term>:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat y} &= 2(\hat y - y) = 2(0.5515 - 1) = -0.8971 \\
\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_2} &= \frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat y} \cdot \sigma'(z_2) = -0.8971 \cdot \underbrace{0.5515 \cdot 0.4485}_{\hat y (1 - \hat y)} = -0.2219 \\
\frac{\partial L}{\partial W_2[i]} &= a_1[i] \cdot \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_2}: \quad (-0.3364)(-0.2219) = 0.0746, \quad (-0.2913)(-0.2219) = 0.0646 \\
\frac{\partial L}{\partial b_2} &= \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_2} = -0.2219 \\
\frac{\partial L}{\partial a_1[i]} &= \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_2} \cdot W_2[i]: \quad (-0.2219)(0.5) = -0.1109, \quad (-0.2219)(-0.6) = 0.1331 \\
\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_1[i]} &= \frac{\partial L}{\partial a_1[i]} \cdot (1 - a_1[i]^2): \quad -0.1109 \cdot 0.8869 = -0.0984, \quad 0.1331 \cdot 0.9151 = 0.1218 \\
\frac{\partial L}{\partial W_1[k][i]} &= x[k] \cdot \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_1[i]}, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b_1[i]} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_1[i]}
\end{aligned}
$$

For example $\partial L / \partial W_1[0][0] = 0.5 \cdot (-0.0984) = -0.0492$. Notice the pattern: a weight's
gradient is (the input it multiplies) × (the gradient at the unit it feeds). In matrix form, for a batch:

| Gradient | NumPy | Shape (batch $N$) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\partial L/\partial Z$ of a layer | `dA * f'(Z)` | same as $Z$: $(N, \text{out})$ |
| $\partial L/\partial W$ | `A_prev.T @ dZ` | same as $W$: $(\text{in}, \text{out})$ |
| $\partial L/\partial b$ | `dZ.sum(axis=0)` | same as $b$: $(\text{out},)$ |
| $\partial L/\partial A_\text{prev}$ (passed on) | `dZ @ W.T` | same as $A_\text{prev}$: $(N, \text{in})$ |

A gradient always has the shape of the thing it is the gradient of. That is the quickest check on backprop code.

**Checking it.** A <Term id="gradient-check">gradient check</Term> estimates each gradient without backprop, with
a central <Term id="finite-difference">finite difference</Term>: nudge one parameter by $\pm\varepsilon$ and rerun
the forward pass,

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta} \approx \frac{L(\theta + \varepsilon) - L(\theta - \varepsilon)}{2\varepsilon}, \qquad \varepsilon = 10^{-5}
$$

**Updating.** One step of <Term id="gradient-descent">gradient descent</Term> moves every parameter against its
gradient, $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \, \partial L / \partial \theta$, with
<Term id="learning-rate">learning rate</Term> $\eta$. With $\eta = 0.5$:
$W_2[0] \leftarrow 0.5 - 0.5 \cdot 0.0746 = 0.4627$, and after all 9 parameters move the loss falls from 0.2012 to
0.1493.

<Callout kind="simplification">
  We write each derivative by hand so you can see the chain rule. PyTorch and other frameworks record the forward
  pass as a <Term id="computation-graph">computation graph</Term> and run these same local rules automatically
  (autograd); you never write `dW1 = X.T @ dZ1` yourself. They also fuse sigmoid with the loss for numerical
  safety, and train on batches of many examples rather than one.
</Callout>

</Section>

Things to try:

<Section kind="mistakes" title="Backprop bugs: loud and silent">

<CommonMistake
  title="Forgetting the transpose in dW"
  wrong={'A1 = np.array([[-0.3364, -0.2913]])  # (1, 2)\ndZ2 = np.array([[-0.2219]])          # (1, 1)\ndW2 = A1 @ dZ2'}
  error="ValueError: matmul: Input operand 1 has a mismatch in its core dimension 0, with gufunc signature (n?,k),(k,m?)->(n?,m?) (size 1 is different from 2)"
  cause="dW2 must have W2's shape, (2, 1). The batch axis has to be summed away, which is what `A1.T @ dZ2` does: (2, 1) @ (1, 1)."
  fix={'dW2 = A1.T @ dZ2  # (2, 1), the shape of W2'}
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Updating integer weights in place"
  wrong={'W1 = np.array([[1, -2], [4, 3]])  # integers: dtype int64\nW1 -= 0.5 * dW1'}
  error="numpy._core._exceptions._UFuncOutputCastingError: Cannot cast ufunc 'subtract' output from dtype('float64') to dtype('int64') with casting rule 'same_kind'"
  cause="An array built from whole numbers stores integers, and an in-place update cannot write fractions into it."
  fix={'W1 = np.array([[1.0, -2.0], [4.0, 3.0]])  # floats\nW1 -= 0.5 * dW1'}
/>

<Callout kind="warning">
  The worst backprop bugs raise no error. Writing `dZ1 = dA1 * (1 - Z1 ** 2)` (the pre-activation instead of
  `A1`), or forgetting the `/ Y.size` of the mean, still produces arrays of the right shape, and training even
  makes some progress. Only a gradient check catches them: the relative error jumps far above 1e-7.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section kind="practice" title="Where you will meet it">

- Every `loss.backward()` call in PyTorch runs this algorithm over the recorded computation graph, layer by layer
  from the loss back, exactly as in the table above.
- When you write a custom layer or loss, frameworks offer a gradient check (`torch.autograd.gradcheck`) built on
  the same central difference. It expects double-precision inputs: in single precision a central difference with
  a small $\varepsilon$ loses most of its digits to rounding.
- Vanishing gradients: each layer multiplies the gradient by an activation slope, at most 0.25 for the sigmoid.
  Through many layers the product shrinks toward 0, which is why deep networks use ReLU in hidden layers.

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

- Backpropagation applies the chain rule from the loss backwards: each gradient is the next one times a local
  derivative.
- Per layer: `dZ = dA * f'(Z)`, `dW = A_prev.T @ dZ`, `db = dZ.sum(axis=0)`, `dA_prev = dZ @ W.T`.
- Every gradient has the shape of its parameter.
- Check backprop with central differences; a relative error around 1e-7 or below means it is right.
- One step $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \, \partial L/\partial \theta$ lowers the loss when $\eta$ is small enough.

</Section>

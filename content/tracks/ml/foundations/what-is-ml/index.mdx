<Section kind="intuition" title="Rules you write vs rules a program learns">

Imagine you have to sort email into spam and normal email (often called "ham"). One way is to write the rules
yourself: if the email contains "free" or "prize", call it spam. A program that follows hand-written rules like
this is a <Term id="rule-based-system">rule-based system</Term>, a classic form of
<Term id="artificial-intelligence">artificial intelligence</Term> (AI): the broad field of making computers do
tasks we associate with intelligence.

Hand-written rules break easily. "Are you free for lunch?" contains "free", and spammers simply switch to words
nobody listed. <Term id="machine-learning">Machine learning</Term> (ML) takes a different route. You collect a
<Term id="dataset">dataset</Term> of emails that people have already marked spam or ham (each mark is a
<Term id="label">label</Term>), describe each email with <Term id="feature">features</Term> (here, its words), and
let a <Term id="training">training</Term> procedure work out the rule from the examples.

| | Hand-written rules | Machine learning |
|---|---|---|
| Who writes the rule | A person | A training procedure, from labeled examples |
| What it needs | Knowledge of the problem | Many labeled examples |
| When spam changes | Someone edits the rules | Retrain on newer examples |

The learned rule is called a <Term id="model">model</Term>. Using the trained model on a new email is called
<Term id="inference">inference</Term>. <Term id="deep-learning">Deep learning</Term> is machine learning where the
model is a neural network with many layers; you will build one in the Deep Learning chapter. So deep learning is
part of machine learning, which is part of AI.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="What the spam filter learns">

The filter below learns from 10 labeled training emails, 5 spam and 5 ham. Training is plain counting:

1. Every word starts with a score of 0.
2. For each spam email, every distinct word in it gets $+1$. For each ham email, every distinct word gets $-1$.

"free" appears in 4 spam emails and 1 ham email ("are you free to talk later today"), so its score is
$4 - 1 = 3$. "the" appears in 2 ham emails and no spam, so its score is $-2$. These scores are the model's
learned numbers.

To judge an email, the model adds up the scores of its words (a word it never saw counts 0) and compares the sum
with a threshold $t$:

$$
\text{score}(\text{email}) = \sum_{\text{word} \in \text{email}} \text{score}(\text{word}),
\qquad
\text{spam if } \text{score} > t
$$

**Worked example.** "are you free for lunch today" scores
$-1 + 0 + 3 + (-1) + (-1) + (-1) = -1$. With $t = 1$ that is not above the threshold, so the learned filter says
ham. The hand-written rule sees "free" and says spam.

**The threshold is learned too.** On the training emails, the ham scores go up to $-3$ and the spam scores start
at $6$, so every whole-number threshold from $-3$ to $5$ gets all 10 right. The program takes the middle one,
$t = 1$, so that a small change in an email's score does not flip the answer.

**Judging a model.** Getting the training emails right is easy: the model was built from them. What matters is
<Term id="generalization">generalization</Term>, how it does on emails it has never seen. That is why the lesson
keeps 6 new emails out of training and measures both filters on them. Accuracy below means the share of emails a
filter labels correctly.

<Callout kind="simplification">
  Real spam filters learn from millions of emails, use many more features than single words (links, sender
  history, word pairs), and turn scores into probabilities, for example with naive Bayes or logistic regression.
  They also keep some hand-written rules for cases that must never slip through. The idea is the same: numbers
  learned from labeled examples, then a threshold.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section kind="mistakes" title="A missing word, and judging on training data">

<CommonMistake
  title="Reading a dictionary entry that does not exist yet"
  wrong={'scores = {}\nfor text, label in train:\n    for word in text.split():\n        scores[word] = scores[word] + 1'}
  error="KeyError: 'win'"
  cause="The first time a word is seen, it is not in the dictionary yet, so scores[word] on the right-hand side has nothing to read. Python raises KeyError with the missing key: 'win', the first word of the first training email."
  fix={'scores[word] = scores.get(word, 0) + 1  # 0 for a word not seen yet'}
/>

<Callout kind="warning">
  Some mistakes raise no error. Counting how many *training* emails the filter gets right prints "10 of 10",
  because the model was built from those exact emails. That number says nothing about new email: on the 6 new
  ones the same filter gets 4 right. Always measure on examples kept out of training. And "learned" does not mean
  "correct": a word the filter never saw, such as "winner", counts 0.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section kind="practice" title="Where you will meet it">

The same split between hand-written rules and learned models shows up everywhere:

| Task | A hand-written rule | What a model learns from labeled examples |
|---|---|---|
| Spam filtering | Block emails with listed words | Word and sender scores |
| Fraud detection | Flag payments over 10,000 euros | Which combinations of amount, place and time are unusual |
| Product search | Exact keyword match | Which results people click for which queries |

The learning steps are always the same: data with labels, features, training, then inference on new inputs,
judged on examples kept out of training. The rest of this track teaches each step properly, starting with
[Supervised, unsupervised and reinforcement learning](#/learn/ml/foundations/types-of-ml).

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

- AI is the broad field. Machine learning is AI that learns its behavior from data. Deep learning is machine
  learning with many-layered neural networks.
- A rule-based system follows rules a person wrote. A model's rule is learned from labeled examples.
- The learned spam filter's model is a score per word plus a threshold: spam if the summed score is above it.
- A model is judged on new examples, not the ones it was trained on.

<Section kind="intuition" title="What kind of examples do you have?">

The spam filter learned from emails that people had marked spam or ham. Those marks are
<Term id="label">labels</Term>: the answer we want the model to give. Whether you have labels, and what kind of
feedback you get, sorts almost every ML problem into one of three families.

| Family | What the program gets | What it learns | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| <Term id="supervised-learning">Supervised</Term> | Examples with the right answer (features and labels) | To predict the label of new examples | Penguin bill size → species |
| <Term id="unsupervised-learning">Unsupervised</Term> | Examples without answers (features only) | Structure: groups, or simpler descriptions | Group customers who buy similar things |
| <Term id="reinforcement-learning">Reinforcement</Term> | Rewards for actions it takes | Which actions lead to the most reward | A program learning to play a game |

Supervised problems come in two kinds. When the label is a category (spam or ham, a species), it is
**classification**. When the label is a number (a house price, a temperature), it is **regression**.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="The same penguins, with and without labels">

The visual below uses real measurements of 342 penguins from three species on islands near Palmer Station,
Antarctica. Each penguin has two <Term id="feature">features</Term>, both in millimetres: bill length and bill
depth.

**Supervised (labels known).** Training computes each species' average bill from the labeled penguins:

| Species | Penguins | Average bill length | Average bill depth |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adelie | 151 | 38.8 mm | 18.3 mm |
| Chinstrap | 68 | 48.8 mm | 18.4 mm |
| Gentoo | 123 | 47.5 mm | 15.0 mm |

The <Term id="model">model</Term> is just these three averages. To classify a new penguin, find the closest average.
For a bill of 45.0 mm by 14.5 mm, the squared distance to the Gentoo average is
$(45.0 - 47.5)^2 + (14.5 - 15.0)^2 \approx 6.5$, far smaller than to Adelie ($\approx 53$) or Chinstrap
($\approx 30$), so the prediction is Gentoo. This is called a nearest-centroid classifier.

**Unsupervised (labels hidden).** Now the program sees only the 342 bills. k-means looks for $k$ groups:

1. Pick $k$ starting centers. Here: the penguin closest to the overall average, then repeatedly the penguin
   farthest from every center chosen so far.
2. Put each penguin in the group of its nearest center.
3. Move each center to the average of its group.
4. Repeat steps 2 and 3 until no penguin changes group.

The groups it finds are called clusters. Only afterwards do we look at the species to see how they compare. With
$k = 3$, one cluster of 141 penguins holds 140 Adelies, but the other two mix Gentoo and Chinstrap penguins:
k-means groups bills that are close together, and these two species' bill lengths overlap. A cluster is a group,
not a name; a person has to decide what it means.

**Reinforcement.** There is no fixed dataset at all. An agent acts, the environment responds with a new
situation and a reward, and the agent learns which actions earn the most reward over time.

<FlowDiagram title="The reinforcement learning loop" nodes={rlNodes} edges={rlEdges} caption="The agent acts; the environment answers with its new state and a reward. Learning means adjusting the agent so its actions earn more reward over time." />

<Callout kind="simplification">
  The families blur in practice. Large language models are first trained on raw text by predicting the next
  word (the text supplies its own labels, which is called self-supervised learning), then tuned with human
  feedback using reinforcement learning. The three families are still the right first question to ask.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section kind="experiment" title="Things to try">

In the visual above:

- In the supervised view, set the bill to 44 mm by 18.5 mm: the prediction is Chinstrap. Lower the depth to 18 mm
  and it becomes Gentoo; set the length to 43.5 mm instead and it becomes Adelie. Penguins near this point are the
  ones a classifier gets wrong.
- Switch to the unsupervised view with $k = 2$. k-means splits the penguins into short-billed and long-billed
  groups: 146 of the 158 penguins in the first cluster are Adelies.
- Set $k = 4$. One cluster is now mostly Chinstrap (38 of 49). More clusters can find finer structure, but
  nothing tells k-means which $k$ is "right".

</Section>

<Section kind="mistakes" title="Calling a problem unsupervised because labels are hard to get">

<Callout kind="warning">
  If what you want the model to output is a known answer for each example (fraud or not, a price, a diagnosis),
  the problem is supervised, even if you do not have the labels yet. Running clustering instead does not produce
  those answers: the clusters it finds may not line up with fraud at all, as k-means' clusters did not line up
  with the penguin species. The usual fix is to label some examples.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="warning">
  Cluster numbers carry no meaning. "Cluster 1" is not "Adelie", and running k-means with other starting
  centers can number the same groups differently.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section kind="practice" title="Sorting real problems">

| Problem | Family | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Will this customer cancel next month? | Supervised (classification) | Past customers show who cancelled |
| How many units will sell next week? | Supervised (regression) | Past weeks show what sold |
| Which support tickets are about the same issue? | Unsupervised | Nobody has grouped them |
| Which ad to show to earn the most clicks over time | Reinforcement | Each choice gets feedback (a click or not) |

Most ML in industry is supervised, and most of this track is too:
[Linear regression](#/learn/ml/classical/linear-regression) and
[logistic regression](#/learn/ml/classical/logistic-regression) are supervised, and
[k-means and PCA](#/learn/ml/classical/clustering-dimred) are unsupervised.

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

- Supervised learning uses labeled examples to predict labels: classification for categories, regression for
  numbers.
- Unsupervised learning finds structure in unlabeled examples. Its groups need not match the categories you
  care about.
- Reinforcement learning learns from rewards for actions it takes.
- The first question about any ML problem: what examples, and what feedback, do you have?

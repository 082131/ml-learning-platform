<Section kind="intuition" title="The only fair comparison">

Your team redesigned the checkout page. Conversions went up 6% the week it launched. Did the redesign do that? Maybe.
Or maybe it was payday, or a competitor's outage, or a marketing email that went out the same week. Before-and-after
comparisons cannot separate the change you made from everything else that changed at the same time.

An <Term id="ab-test">A/B test</Term> fixes this by running both versions **at the same time**, and letting a coin
flip decide which version each user sees. Group A gets the old page, group B the new one. Payday, outages and
emails hit both groups equally, because the coin does not know about them. So if B converts better than A by more
than chance can explain, the only thing left to explain it is the page. That is why randomized experiments are the
gold standard for "did X cause Y": randomization turns a difference into a cause.

Most of the statistics for analysing such a test you already have: a difference of two proportions, its standard
error, a test and an interval. This lesson is about everything that has to be decided **before** the test starts,
because most A/B tests that go wrong were broken at the design stage, not in the analysis.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="Five decisions, made before any data">

Here is the flow: **hypothesis → metric → randomization unit → sample size → duration**, all written down before
launch. Each step depends on the one before.

### 1. The hypothesis and the primary metric

Start with one sentence: "The new checkout increases the share of visitors who complete an order." That sentence
names the **primary metric**, the single number the decision rests on: orders per visitor. Choosing one in advance
matters because, as the probability lesson showed, if you look at 20 metrics each with a 5% false alarm rate, at
least one will "win" by chance about 64% of the time.

Alongside it, choose a few <Term id="guardrail-metric">guardrail metrics</Term>: things that must not get worse,
such as page load time, refund rate or revenue per visitor. A change that raises orders while doubling refunds is
not a win, and the guardrails are how you notice.

### 2. The randomization unit

The <Term id="randomization-unit">randomization unit</Term> is what the coin is flipped for: a user, a session, a
page view, a device. Usually it should be the **user**, for two reasons:

- **Consistent experience.** A user flipped per page view would see the old checkout on one click and the new one on
  the next, which is neither version.
- **Independence.** The statistics assume independent units. Page views from one person are not independent (they
  share that person's habits), so treating each view as a unit makes the standard error too small and results look
  more certain than they are.

The rule: randomize by the unit you will analyse by, and make sure units do not influence each other.

### 3. The minimum detectable effect

Before you can size the test, you must say how big an effect you want to be able to see. The
<Term id="minimum-detectable-effect">minimum detectable effect</Term> (MDE) is the smallest lift that would change
your decision. If a +2% lift would not justify the engineering cost of the new page, there is no point paying for a
test sensitive enough to detect +2%.

The MDE is a business choice, and it is the input that drives the cost of the test more than any other.

### 4. The sample size

Now the power lesson's formula, adapted to conversion rates. With baseline rate $p_1$ and the rate we want to detect
$p_2 = p_1(1 + \text{MDE})$, the standard error of the difference between two groups of $n$ is

$$
\mathrm{SE}(\hat p_B - \hat p_A) = \sqrt{\frac{p_1(1-p_1) + p_2(1-p_2)}{n}}
$$

Each $p(1-p)/n$ is the variance of one group's conversion rate (a Bernoulli mean, from the distributions chapter), and
the variances of the two independent groups add. We need the true difference to be $z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{\text{power}}$
standard errors (1.96 + 0.84 = 2.80 for the usual choices), and solving for $n$ gives

$$
n \text{ per group} = \frac{(z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{\text{power}})^2 \,\big(p_1(1-p_1) + p_2(1-p_2)\big)}{(p_2 - p_1)^2}
$$

For a 5% baseline and a +10% relative MDE ($p_2 = 5.5\%$):
$2.80^2 \times (0.0475 + 0.051975) / 0.005^2 \approx 31{,}231$ visitors per group. Two things drive it:

- **The MDE, squared, in the denominator.** Half the lift, about four times the visitors.
- **The baseline.** Rare actions are expensive to test: at a 1% baseline the same +10% lift needs 163,092 per group.

### 5. The duration

Divide the total, $2n$, by daily traffic. At 4,000 visitors a day, 62,462 visitors take 16 days. Then round **up to
whole weeks**: weekday and weekend users often behave differently, and a test that covers Monday to Thursday only
measures weekday users. Decide the end date now, and do not stop early because the numbers look good. The next
lesson shows exactly how much damage early stopping does.

### Assigning users

In practice the coin flip is a hash: `hash(experiment name + user id)`, even or odd. A good hash behaves like a fair
coin but is reproducible, so a returning user always sees the same version, and no assignment table needs to be
stored. Including the experiment name (a "salt") gives each experiment an independent split, so the same users are
not always in group B.

<Section kind="mistakes" title="Tests that were broken before they started">

<CommonMistake
  title="Comparing opt-in users with everyone else"
  wrong={'lift = engagement[users.opted_in].mean() / engagement[~users.opted_in].mean() - 1'}
  error="(no error) a large 'lift' that is mostly the difference between keen and casual users"
  cause="Users chose their group, so the groups differed before the treatment. No amount of data fixes self-selection."
  fix="Randomize who gets the feature. If you cannot, say clearly that the comparison is not causal."
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="No sample size, no end date"
  wrong={'# run it and stop when it is significant'}
  error="(no error) false positive rates of 20 to 30% instead of 5% (next lesson)"
  cause="The test's error rates assume one look at a sample size fixed in advance."
  fix="Compute n from the MDE, α and power, convert it to whole weeks, and analyse once at the end (or use a sequential method designed for repeated looks)."
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Randomizing by request, analysing by user"
  wrong={'group = "B" if random.random() < 0.5 else "A"   # per request'}
  error="(no error) users see both versions, and the SE is computed as if requests were independent"
  cause="The unit of randomization does not match the experience or the analysis."
  fix="Hash the user id (salted with the experiment name) so each user gets one version, and analyse per user."
/>

<Section kind="practice" title="Where you will use this">

- **Product and growth teams** run every change this way: write the hypothesis, metric, guardrails, MDE and duration
  in a short pre-registration document before launch.
- **ML model launches**: an online A/B test of a new ranking or recommendation model is the final check after offline
  evaluation, because offline metrics do not always move business metrics. The randomization unit is usually the
  user; for marketplaces, sometimes a whole region.
- **Feature flags** use exactly the salted-hash assignment above to roll features out to a percentage of users.

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

A before-and-after comparison cannot separate a change from everything else that happened at the same time; running
both versions at once and letting a coin decide who sees which makes the change the only systematic difference, so a
difference becomes a cause. The design then flows in order: a hypothesis names one primary metric and a few
guardrails; the randomization unit (usually the user) keeps the experience consistent and the units independent; the
minimum detectable effect says what is worth finding; the power formula turns MDE, baseline, α and power into
visitors per group, where small lifts and rare actions are expensive; and traffic turns visitors into whole weeks. A
salted hash flips the coin reproducibly.

With the test designed and run, the data arrive. Next we analyse them, and look closely at the most common way a
well-designed test is ruined at the last minute:
[Analysing an A/B test: lift, intervals and the peeking trap](#/learn/stats/experiments/ab-test-analysis).

</Section>

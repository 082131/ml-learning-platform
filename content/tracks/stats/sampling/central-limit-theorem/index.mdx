<Section kind="intuition" title="Averaging cancels extremes">

Support wait times are lopsided: most customers wait a few minutes, a few wait over an hour. Their histogram looks
nothing like a bell. Now take the **average** of 30 waits, many times over, and plot those averages. They form a
near-perfect bell. The same happens with heights, with yes/no conversions, with almost anything. That is the
**central limit theorem** (CLT), and it is one of the most useful facts in statistics.

Why would averaging produce a bell? Think about what it takes for an average of 30 waits to be extreme. A very high
average needs most of the 30 customers to have waited a long time, all at once. That is rare, because long waits
are rare and they would have to coincide. A middling average, on the other hand, can happen in a huge number of ways:
some long, some short, mixed in any order. So averages pile up in the middle and thin out quickly toward the edges.
The more values you average, the stronger this effect, and the shape that results is the normal curve.

Why it matters: almost every method in the rest of this track (confidence intervals, z-tests, A/B tests) needs to
know how a sample mean is distributed. The CLT says we do not need to know the shape of the data to answer that. We
only need $\mu$, $\sigma$ and $n$.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="What the theorem says, and when to trust it">

### The statement

Take $n$ independent values from any population with mean $\mu$ and a finite standard deviation $\sigma$. As $n$
grows, the distribution of the sample mean $\bar x$ gets closer and closer to a normal distribution:

$$
\bar x \;\approx\; N\!\left(\mu,\; \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\right)
$$

Each part comes from somewhere we have already been:

- **The centre, $\mu$**: the sample mean is unbiased (last lesson).
- **The width, $\sigma / \sqrt n$**: the standard error, from the variance rules (last lesson).
- **The shape, normal**: this is the new part, and the theorem's real content. The previous lesson told us the
  centre and the spread of the means; the CLT tells us the shape too.

The same holds for sums (a sum is just $n$ times the mean): the total of $n$ values is approximately
$N(n\mu, \sigma\sqrt n)$.

### What it does not say

The CLT is about the **distribution of the average across repeated samples**, never about the data. Collecting more
wait times will not make wait times normal; it will show their skewed shape more clearly. People often write "by
the CLT, the data are normal", and it is always wrong.

### How large is "large enough"?

The theorem says "as $n$ grows". How fast the bell appears depends on the population:

- **Symmetric populations** (heights, uniform values, even two humps): the means look normal already at $n \approx 5$
  to 10, because there is no lopsidedness to wash out.
- **Skewed populations** (wait times, incomes, latencies): it takes longer. For exponential waits the skewness of
  the mean is $2 / \sqrt n$: 2 at $n = 1$, 0.37 at $n = 30$, 0.14 at $n = 200$. The famous "n ≥ 30" rule is for
  moderate skew; heavy tails need more.
- **Rare yes/no events** (conversion rates of 1%): the mean of 0/1 values is a proportion, and it only looks normal
  once you expect about 10 or more successes and 10 or more failures ($np \ge 10$ and $n(1-p) \ge 10$). At a 0.5%
  rate that is 2,000 visitors.

### Using it: how unusual is this sample mean?

The CLT turns questions about averages into normal-curve questions, using the z-score idea from the normal lesson
but with the standard error as the unit:

$$
z = \frac{\bar x - \mu}{\sigma / \sqrt n}
$$

Heights have $\mu = 170$ cm and $\sigma = 10$ cm. One 174 cm person is $z = 0.4$, ordinary. A random group of 25
averaging 174 cm is $z = (174 - 170) / (10/5) = 2$: unusual, because averages of 25 rarely stray that far. This exact
calculation, "how many standard errors away is what we saw?", is the heart of every test in the next chapter.

</Section>

<Section kind="mistakes" title="Misusing the theorem">

<CommonMistake
  title="'By the CLT, my data are normal'"
  wrong={'# 5,000 latencies, so the CLT applies\nthreshold = lat.mean() + 3 * lat.std()'}
  error="(no error) far more than 0.13% of requests exceed the threshold"
  cause="The CLT is about the distribution of an average, not of single values. Latencies stay skewed however many you collect."
  fix="Use the CLT for the mean (for example a confidence interval of the average latency). For single requests, use empirical percentiles such as p99."
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Normal approximations for rare events"
  wrong={'p_hat = 1 / 200             # one conversion among 200 visitors\nse = (p_hat * (1 - p_hat) / 200) ** 0.5\nlow = p_hat - 1.96 * se       # -0.0048: a negative conversion rate'}
  error="(no error) an interval that includes impossible negative rates"
  cause="With about one expected success the sampling distribution is lumpy and skewed; the normal curve does not describe it."
  fix="Collect more data (aim for 10 or more expected successes), or use methods made for small counts: an exact binomial interval, a Wilson interval, or the Bayesian approach in the last chapter."
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Values that are not independent"
  wrong={'# 30 measurements from the same user session\nse = s / 30 ** 0.5'}
  error="(no error) the SE is far too small, so results look more certain than they are"
  cause="Repeated measurements of one unit are correlated: they share that user's habits. The CLT and σ/√n both assume independent values."
  fix="Average per user first and use the number of users as n, or use methods that account for clustering."
/>

</Section>

<Section kind="practice" title="Where you will use this">

- **Conversion rates** are means of 0/1 values. The CLT is why an A/B test can use a normal curve for the difference
  in rates once each group has enough conversions.
- **Average latency, revenue per user, session length** are skewed, so check the expected sample size: means of
  revenue per user (heavy tails from big spenders) may need thousands of users before the normal approximation is
  good.
- **Mini-batch gradients** in training are averages over a batch, which is one reason their noise is roughly normal
  and shrinks like $1/\sqrt{\text{batch size}}$.

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

The last lesson gave us the centre ($\mu$) and the spread ($\sigma/\sqrt n$) of a sample mean. The central limit
theorem gives its shape: approximately normal, whatever the shape of the data, because extreme averages need many
extreme values at once while middling averages can be reached in countless ways. How large $n$ must be depends on
how skewed or rare the data are, and the theorem always describes averages and sums, never the data themselves.
Putting the pieces together, "how far is this sample mean from $\mu$?" becomes a z-score measured in standard errors,
answered with the normal CDF.

There is a catch we have quietly skipped: those calculations needed $\mu$, the very number we are trying to learn.
The next lesson turns the reasoning around, from "where will the sample mean land, given $\mu$" to "where could
$\mu$ be, given the sample mean": [Confidence intervals](#/learn/stats/inference/confidence-intervals).

</Section>

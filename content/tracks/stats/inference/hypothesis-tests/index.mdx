<Section kind="intuition" title="Innocent until the data say otherwise">

Your ticket-routing model labels support tickets correctly 70% of the time. A colleague trains a new one and runs it
on 100 fresh tickets: 78 correct. Is the new model better? It did score higher, but even a model that is exactly as
good as the old one would sometimes score 78 on a lucky set of tickets, just as a fair coin sometimes lands heads 7
times out of 10. The question is whether 78 is a result that luck alone **plausibly** produces.

A hypothesis test answers that with a clever reversal. Instead of trying to prove the new model is better, we
**assume it is not**, and ask: in that world, how often would we see a score as high as 78? If the answer is "all the
time", the score is no evidence of improvement. If it is "hardly ever", then either we were very unlucky or the
assumption is wrong, and we start to believe the new model really is better.

It works like a trial: the defendant (no improvement) is presumed innocent, and we only convict if the evidence would
be very unlikely under innocence. This lesson builds that procedure step by step, names its parts, and spends a
good while on what its result, the p-value, does and does not mean, because it is the most misread number in data
work.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="The four steps of a test">

Here is the whole flow before the details: **state the "nothing happened" hypothesis → work out what results would
look like if it were true → see where our result falls → if it falls somewhere very unusual, reject the
hypothesis.**

### Step 1: two hypotheses

- The <Term id="null-hypothesis">null hypothesis</Term>, $H_0$, is the boring explanation: no effect, no difference,
  nothing new. Here: the new model's true accuracy is 70%, the same as the old one.
- The <Term id="alternative-hypothesis">alternative hypothesis</Term>, $H_1$, is what we suspect instead. Here: the
  true accuracy is **more** than 70% (a one-sided alternative). If we only suspected it was **different**, better or
  worse, the alternative would be two-sided.

We test $H_0$, not $H_1$, because $H_0$ is specific enough to compute with. "Accuracy is 70%" pins down a whole
distribution; "accuracy is more than 70%" does not.

### Step 2: what would results look like under $H_0$?

If the true accuracy were 70%, the number correct out of 100 independent tickets would follow a binomial
distribution with $n = 100$, $p = 0.7$ (from the distributions chapter). Its mean is 70 and its SD is
$\sqrt{100 \cdot 0.7 \cdot 0.3} \approx 4.58$. That distribution is the **null distribution**: the full range of
scores a no-better model would produce, and how often each.

The number we compare against it is the <Term id="test-statistic">test statistic</Term>, a single number that
summarises the data in the direction we care about. Here it is simply the count correct, 78. Often it is a z-score:
$(78 - 70)/4.58 = 1.75$, "1.75 SDs above what the null predicts".

### Step 3: the p-value

The <Term id="p-value">p-value</Term> is **the probability, if $H_0$ is true, of a result at least as extreme as the
one we got.** For our one-sided alternative, "at least as extreme" means 78 or more correct:

$$
p = P(X \ge 78 \mid p = 0.7) = \sum_{i=78}^{100} \binom{100}{i} 0.7^i\, 0.3^{100-i} \approx 0.048
$$

So a model that is truly no better would score 78 or more on about 4.8% of test sets. That is the bars highlighted in
the explorer below, added up. For a two-sided alternative we also count results just as unusual on the low side,
which roughly doubles it here, to 0.082.

### Step 4: decide, using a threshold chosen in advance

We reject $H_0$ when $p$ is below a threshold called the <Term id="significance-level">significance level</Term>,
$\alpha$ (alpha), usually 0.05. Here $0.048 < 0.05$, so with a one-sided test we reject the null and say the
improvement is statistically significant. With a two-sided test ($p = 0.082$) we would not.

What does $\alpha$ buy us? If we always reject when $p < 0.05$, then in all the situations where $H_0$ is really true
we wrongly reject it 5% of the time. $\alpha$ is the false alarm rate we agree to tolerate. The next lesson looks at
this error, and the opposite one, in detail.

### What a p-value is not

Each of these misreadings is common, and each is wrong:

1. **"$p$ is the probability that $H_0$ is true."** No. The p-value was computed by *assuming* $H_0$ is true. It is
   $P(\text{data this extreme} \mid H_0)$, not $P(H_0 \mid \text{data})$, the same reversal as fraud flags in the
   conditional probability lesson. Getting $P(H_0 \mid \text{data})$ needs a prior: the Bayesian chapter.
2. **"Not significant means no effect."** No. A large p-value means the data are compatible with no effect, which is
   not the same as showing there is none. A real 4-point improvement on only 100 tickets often gives $p > 0.05$.
3. **"A smaller p means a bigger effect."** No. The p-value mixes effect size with sample size. 78% on 400 tickets
   gives $p \approx 0.0002$; 78% on 100 gives 0.048. Same effect, different evidence.

### Tests and intervals are two views of one thing

A 95% confidence interval contains exactly the values that a two-sided test at $\alpha = 0.05$ would **not** reject.
So "is 70% inside the 95% interval for the new model's accuracy?" and "is the two-sided p-value above 0.05?" are the
same question. The interval is usually more informative: it also shows how big the effect could plausibly be.

<Section kind="mistakes" title="How tests go wrong">

<CommonMistake
  title="Reading p as the probability the null is true"
  wrong={'"p = 0.03, so there is only a 3% chance the new model is no better."'}
  error="(no error) a statement the p-value cannot support"
  cause="p = P(data at least this extreme | H0). The claim needs P(H0 | data), which also depends on how likely improvement was to begin with (a prior)."
  fix="Say: 'if the model were no better, a result this good would happen 3% of the time'. For P(H0 | data), use a Bayesian analysis."
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Choosing one-sided after seeing the data"
  wrong={'p_two = 0.08   # not significant...\np_one = p_two / 2   # "the effect is obviously positive", so 0.04'}
  error="(no error) the real false positive rate of this habit is 10%, not 5%"
  cause="Picking the side the data went is a second chance to win. The test's error rate assumes the hypothesis was fixed in advance."
  fix="Write down the hypotheses, the test and α before collecting data. Use one-sided only when the other direction would not change any decision."
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Treating 'not significant' as 'no difference'"
  wrong={'if p > 0.05:\n    print("The new model is the same as the old one.")'}
  error="(no error) a real but small improvement gets thrown away because the test set was small"
  cause="A large p-value means the data cannot rule out no effect. With little data it cannot rule out a sizeable effect either."
  fix="Report the confidence interval: '74% (95% CI 64% to 82%)' shows how big the improvement might still be. Plan the test size in advance (next lesson)."
/>

<Section kind="practice" title="Where you will use this">

- **Model evaluation**: "is the new model's accuracy better than the baseline's on this test set?" is exactly this
  test. For two models on the **same** examples, a paired test (McNemar's) is the right version.
- **Data validation**: "is today's share of null emails higher than the usual 2%?" is a one-sample test of a
  proportion; drift detectors run tests like this every day.
- **A/B tests**: the two-proportion z-test in the A/B chapter is this lesson with two groups instead of one fixed
  baseline.

<Section kind="summary">

To decide whether 78 of 100 is a real improvement over 70%, we assumed it was not (the null hypothesis) and worked out
the full distribution of scores a no-better model would produce. The p-value measured how far into the tail of that
distribution our result fell: the probability, under the null, of a result at least as extreme. Rejecting when it is
below a pre-chosen α means accepting a false alarm rate of α. Along the way we kept the p-value in its place: it is
not the probability the null is true, a large one is not proof of no effect, and it mixes effect size with sample
size. Confidence intervals and tests are two views of one calculation, and the interval usually tells you more.

Every decision rule can go wrong in two ways: crying wolf when nothing changed, and missing a real change. The next
lesson measures both and shows how sample size trades one against the other:
[Type I and II errors, power and sample size](#/learn/stats/inference/errors-power).

</Section>

<Section kind="intuition" title="When values form a smooth hill">

Suppose a shop records its number of online orders every day for three years. Most days land near 500, some days
are a bit busier or quieter, and very few are wildly off. If you draw a histogram of those 1,000 days, you get a
hill: tall in the middle, sloping down on both sides, roughly symmetric. Heights of adults, measurement errors,
daily totals of many small independent purchases: the same hill keeps appearing.

That hill has a name, the **normal distribution** (or bell curve), and two numbers describe it completely: where its
centre is (the mean) and how wide it is (the standard deviation). Knowing just those two numbers lets you answer
questions like "how often do we get more than 600 orders?" or "how many orders do we need to handle on all but the
worst 1% of days?".

There is a second reason to learn it, and it is the bigger one. Next chapter we will see that **averages** of almost
any kind of data follow this curve, even when the data themselves do not. That fact, the central limit theorem, is
why the normal distribution sits underneath confidence intervals, hypothesis tests and A/B tests.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="Densities, the bell curve and z-scores">

### Why continuous values need a new idea

For counts we drew a bar for each value and the bar's height was its probability. Order counts are whole numbers,
but think about something truly continuous, such as a delivery time: 31.2 minutes, 31.27, 31.2748... There are
infinitely many possible values, so each exact one must have probability 0 (otherwise they would add up to more than
1). "Exactly 31.2748... minutes" never happens; "between 31 and 32 minutes" does.

So for continuous values we describe probability with a curve called a
<Term id="pdf">probability density function</Term> (PDF), and we read it by **area**: the probability that the value
lands between $a$ and $b$ is the area under the curve between $a$ and $b$. The total area is 1. The curve's height
is a density, "probability per unit of x", which is why it can be bigger than 1 for a narrow distribution. Height
tells you where values are crowded; only areas are probabilities.

### The normal curve

The <Term id="normal-distribution">normal distribution</Term> with mean $\mu$ (mu) and standard deviation $\sigma$
(sigma) has the density

$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\; e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\right)^2}
$$

You will never compute this by hand, but each part has a meaning worth seeing once:

- $\frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$ is the distance from the centre, measured in standard deviations.
- Squaring it and putting it in $e^{-\frac12(\cdot)^2}$ makes the curve highest at the centre and fall off fast and
  symmetrically on both sides. The fall-off is so fast that values more than 3 SDs away are rare, and more than 5
  are almost unheard of.
- $\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}$ just scales the height so the total area is exactly 1. A wider curve ($\sigma$ big)
  must be lower.

### The 68-95-99.7 rule

Because every normal curve is the same shape slid by $\mu$ and stretched by $\sigma$, the same shares always fall
within the same number of SDs of the mean:

| Within | Share of values | For orders ~ N(500, 40) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 SD | 68.3% | 460 to 540 |
| 2 SD | 95.4% | 420 to 580 |
| 3 SD | 99.7% | 380 to 620 |

So a day with more than 580 orders (beyond 2 SD on the high side) happens on about $(100 - 95.4) / 2 \approx 2.3\%$
of days.

### z-scores: one scale for every normal

To use one table (or one function) for every normal distribution, we convert a value to a
<Term id="z-score">z-score</Term>: how many standard deviations it lies from the mean.

$$
z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}
$$

600 orders is $z = (600 - 500) / 40 = 2.5$. A z-score of 2.5 means the same rarity whatever the units, which is how
you compare a busy day at a big store with a busy day at a small one.

### From value to probability, and back

Two questions come up constantly, and they go in opposite directions:

1. **Value → probability.** "How likely are more than 600 orders?" Use the CDF, $\Phi$ (capital phi), which gives
   the area to the left: $P(X > 600) = 1 - \Phi(2.5) = 1 - 0.9938 = 0.0062$.
2. **Probability → value.** "How many orders do the busiest 5% of days exceed?" Use the inverse CDF: the z with 95%
   of the area to its left is 1.645, so the value is $500 + 1.645 \times 40 \approx 565.8$.

In Python both live in `statistics.NormalDist`: `.cdf(x)` and `.inv_cdf(p)`.

### When the bell does not fit

The normal is a model, and many quantities do not follow it. Wait times, incomes and request latencies cannot go
below zero and have long right tails (a few very large values). A normal model of them predicts impossible negative
values and underestimates the extremes. Always look at a histogram before using a normal curve on raw data. The good
news, next chapter, is that **averages** of such data are close to normal anyway.

</Section>

<Section kind="mistakes" title="Reading the curve wrongly">

<CommonMistake
  title="Treating the density as a probability"
  wrong={'p_exactly_500 = NormalDist(500, 40).pdf(500)    # 0.00997...'}
  error="(no error) the number is a density per order, not the chance of 500 orders"
  cause="For a continuous model, P(X = 500 exactly) is 0. The pdf is probability per unit."
  fix="Ask about a range: cdf(500.5) - cdf(499.5) for 'about 500', which here is close to pdf(500) × 1 because the window is 1 order wide."
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Using cdf when you need the inverse"
  wrong={'capacity = NormalDist(500, 40).cdf(0.99)'}
  error="(no error) returns about 0.0, a probability, where a number of orders was wanted"
  cause="cdf maps a value to a probability. A capacity question gives the probability and asks for the value."
  fix="capacity = NormalDist(500, 40).inv_cdf(0.99)  # about 593 orders"
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Assuming raw data are normal"
  wrong={'threshold = waits.mean() + 3 * waits.std()     # "3-sigma" alert on wait times'}
  error="(no error) the alert fires far more often than the 0.13% a normal model promises"
  cause="Wait times are skewed with a long right tail; 3 SDs above the mean is not rare for them."
  fix="Use an empirical percentile (np.quantile(waits, 0.999)) or a distribution that fits the data."
/>

<Section kind="practice" title="Where you will use this">

- **Standardising features.** `StandardScaler` computes z-scores for each column, so features measured in different
  units are on the same scale for a model.
- **Outlier and anomaly rules** such as "flag beyond 3 SD" assume roughly normal data; check the histogram first.
- **Every z-test and confidence interval** in the next chapters ends with a normal CDF or inverse CDF: 1.96 is just
  `NormalDist().inv_cdf(0.975)`.

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

Continuous values cannot have a probability for each exact value, so we described them with a density curve and
read probabilities as areas under it. The normal curve is the most important such shape: two numbers, the mean and
the standard deviation, fix it completely, and because every normal curve is the same shape slid and stretched, the
same shares always lie within 1, 2 and 3 SDs (68%, 95%, 99.7%). The z-score turns any value into "SDs from the mean",
so one CDF answers value-to-probability questions, and its inverse answers probability-to-value questions. And the
model only fits data that are actually hill-shaped, which skewed quantities like wait times are not.

So far we have assumed we know the distribution. In real work we only see a sample from it. The next chapter asks
how much a sample's average can be trusted, and finds the normal curve waiting there:
[Samples, populations and the standard error](#/learn/stats/sampling/sampling-distributions).

</Section>

<Section kind="intuition" title="Named shapes for counting things">

In the last lesson we described a random variable by writing out its whole table. That works for a spinner with
three outcomes, but think about questions like these:

- 20 people visit a product page today, and each buys with chance 10%. How likely is it that 3 or more buy?
- A support team gets 3 tickets an hour on average. How likely is an hour with more than 6?

Writing the table by hand would be painful. Luckily, counts like these come from processes that repeat over and over
in data work, and the same few shapes keep appearing. Each shape has a name and a formula, and once you recognise the
process you get the whole table for free. This lesson covers the three you will meet most often: the Bernoulli (one
yes/no), the binomial (how many yes answers out of a fixed number of tries) and the Poisson (how many events in a
window of time).

The real skill is not the formulas, which a library computes for you. It is **recognising which process produced the
data, and checking that its assumptions hold**, because when they do not, the formula answers a question about a
different world.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="From one coin flip to many">

### Bernoulli: a single yes/no

A <Term id="bernoulli-trial">Bernoulli trial</Term> is one try with two outcomes: success (written 1) with
probability $p$, failure (written 0) with probability $1 - p$. One visitor buys or does not. One email is opened or
not. One prediction is right or wrong.

Using the previous lesson's formulas on this two-row table: $E[X] = 1 \cdot p + 0 \cdot (1 - p) = p$, and
$\mathrm{Var}(X) = p(1 - p)$. The variance is largest at $p = 0.5$ (a coin flip is as uncertain as it gets) and zero
at $p = 0$ or $1$ (nothing is uncertain).

### Binomial: how many successes in n tries

Now repeat the trial $n$ times, independently, with the same $p$, and count the successes. That count follows the
<Term id="binomial-distribution">binomial distribution</Term>. To build its formula, take the question "exactly 2
buyers among 20 visitors" and split it into two smaller questions.

1. **What is the chance of one specific arrangement?** Say visitors 1 and 2 buy and the other 18 do not. Because the
   visitors are independent, we multiply: $p \cdot p \cdot (1-p)^{18} = 0.1^2 \cdot 0.9^{18} \approx 0.0015$.
2. **How many arrangements are there?** The two buyers could be any 2 of the 20 visitors. The number of ways to pick
   2 from 20 is "20 choose 2" $= \binom{20}{2} = 190$. Every arrangement has the same chance from step 1.

Multiply the two answers, and you have the binomial formula:

$$
P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k}\, p^k\, (1 - p)^{n - k}
$$

Reading it part by part: $\binom{n}{k}$ counts the arrangements, $p^k$ is the chance that the chosen $k$ succeed, and
$(1-p)^{n-k}$ is the chance that the rest fail. For our example, $190 \times 0.0015 \approx 0.285$.

Because the count is a sum of $n$ independent Bernoulli trials, the "variances add" rule gives its spread directly:

$$
E[X] = np \qquad \mathrm{Var}(X) = np(1 - p)
$$

20 visitors at 10% average 2 buyers, with SD $\sqrt{20 \cdot 0.1 \cdot 0.9} \approx 1.34$.

### Poisson: events in a window of time

Tickets arriving, errors logged, requests per second. Here there is no fixed number of tries; events just happen at
some average rate. The <Term id="poisson-distribution">Poisson distribution</Term> describes the count of events in
a window when they happen independently, at a steady average rate $\lambda$ (lambda) per window:

$$
P(X = k) = \frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!}
$$

Where does it come from? Chop the hour into 3,600 seconds. In each second a ticket arrives with a tiny chance,
$\lambda / 3{,}600$. That is a binomial with huge $n$ and tiny $p$, and as you chop finer and finer, the binomial
formula turns into this one. So the Poisson is not a new idea: it is the binomial for "many tries, each very
unlikely".

Its mean and variance are both $\lambda$. That gives a quick reality check on data: if hourly counts have a mean of 3
and a variance of 12, the events are not independent (they come in clumps, such as an outage creating fifty tickets
at once), and the Poisson will underestimate the busy hours.

### Exactly, at most, more than

A PMF answers "exactly k". Most practical questions are "at most k" or "more than k". Because "exactly 0", "exactly
1", ... are mutually exclusive, we add the bars:

$$
P(X \le k) = \sum_{i=0}^{k} P(X = i) \qquad P(X > k) = 1 - P(X \le k)
$$

The running total $P(X \le k)$ is the <Term id="cdf">cumulative distribution function</Term> (CDF). It is how
libraries answer "how likely is a value this low or lower", and next lesson it becomes the main tool for continuous
values.

<Section kind="mistakes" title="Formulas used outside their assumptions">

<CommonMistake
  title="Trials that are not independent"
  wrong={'# 30 users from the same 3 companies, each converts with p = 0.2\nP_none = 0.8 ** 30'}
  error="(no error) the formula gives 0.0012, but whole companies decide together, so 'no conversions at all' is far more likely"
  cause="Users in one company share a decision. The binomial assumes each trial is independent."
  fix="Count the units that decide independently (companies, not users), or use a model for clustered data."
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="A rate that is not constant"
  wrong={'lam = tickets_per_day / 24     # average per hour, used for the 9 am hour'}
  error="(no error) the 9 am hour is badly under-staffed"
  cause="Tickets arrive mostly in working hours. A Poisson needs a steady rate over the window you apply it to."
  fix="Estimate λ for each hour of the day (or day of the week) separately."
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="An off-by-one in the tail"
  wrong={'P_3_or_more = 1 - sum(binom_pmf(k, 20, 0.1) for k in range(3 + 1))'}
  error="(no error) this computes P(X > 3) = P(X ≥ 4), not P(X ≥ 3)"
  cause="range(4) includes k = 3, so subtracting it from 1 removes the k = 3 bar too."
  fix="P(X ≥ 3) = 1 - P(X ≤ 2) = 1 - sum(... for k in range(3)). Say the inequality in words before coding it."
/>

<Section kind="practice" title="Where you will use this">

- **Conversion and click counts** in A/B tests are binomial. Its variance $p(1-p)$ is where the standard error of a
  conversion rate comes from in the A/B chapter.
- **Model accuracy on a test set** is binomial too: 400 labelled examples at 90% accuracy give a count with SD 6, so
  88% vs 90% on 400 examples may be noise. The hypothesis testing lesson makes this precise.
- **Capacity and alerting**: requests per second, errors per minute and tickets per hour are modelled as Poisson to
  set thresholds such as "page someone if errors exceed the 99.9th percentile of the expected count".

<Section kind="summary">

We started from the smallest random event, a single yes/no (Bernoulli) with mean $p$ and variance $p(1-p)$. Repeating
it $n$ times independently and counting gave the binomial: count the arrangements, multiply by the chance of each,
and the "variances add" rule hands us its mean $np$ and variance $np(1-p)$. Pushing the binomial to many tiny chances
gave the Poisson for events in time, with mean and variance both $\lambda$. Adding bars turned "exactly k" into "at
most k", the CDF. Each formula only describes the world its assumptions describe: independent trials, a fixed $n$, a
steady rate.

Counts take separate values you can put bars on. Measurements such as times, heights and amounts can take any value
in a range, and that needs a different idea, a curve whose area is probability:
[The normal distribution and z-scores](#/learn/stats/distributions/normal-distribution).

</Section>

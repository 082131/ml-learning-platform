<Section kind="intuition" title="A probability is a long-run share">

Imagine you roll two dice a million times and keep a tally. Some totals turn up a lot (7 is the most common) and
some rarely (2 and 12). If 7 shows up in about 167,000 of the million rolls, we say "the probability of a 7 is
about 1/6". That is the simplest mental model of probability, and the one this whole track builds on: **a
probability is the share of times something happens if you could repeat the situation over and over.**

Why do we need this? Because almost every question in data work is a question about outcomes we cannot predict one
by one. Will this user click? Is this transaction fraud? Did the new model really get better, or did it get lucky
on this test set? We cannot answer "what will happen next time", but we can answer "how often would it happen",
and that turns out to be enough to make good decisions. Statistics, A/B testing and machine learning are all built
on top of that idea.

A probability is always a number from 0 to 1. Zero means "never happens", 1 means "always happens", and 0.3 means
"happens in about 30% of repetitions". It is not a promise about the next single trial: a 30% event can happen
three times in a row, and a 90% event can fail. It is a promise about the long run.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="Outcomes, events and three rules">

### Step 1: list everything that could happen

To compute a probability we first need a complete list of possible outcomes. That list is called the
<Term id="sample-space">sample space</Term>. For two dice it is every pair (first die, second die): (1, 1), (1, 2),
and so on up to (6, 6), which makes 6 × 6 = 36 outcomes.

Notice that (3, 4) and (4, 3) are listed separately. That matters, because the next step only works if every
outcome in the list is equally likely, and each specific pair has the same chance, 1 in 36. If you lumped "a 3 and
a 4" into one outcome, it would be twice as likely as "two 3s", and counting would give wrong answers.

### Step 2: ask a yes/no question about the outcome

An <Term id="probability-event">event</Term> is any yes/no question about the outcome: "is the sum 7?", "is the
first die a 6?". Each event picks out some of the 36 outcomes. "The sum is 7" picks out six of them: (1, 6),
(2, 5), (3, 4), (4, 3), (5, 2), (6, 1).

### Step 3: count and divide

When all outcomes are equally likely, the probability of an event is just a share:

$$
P(A) = \frac{\text{number of outcomes in } A}{\text{number of outcomes in the sample space}}
$$

So $P(\text{sum is 7}) = 6/36 = 1/6$. This is the "long-run share" idea from the intuition, computed without
rolling anything: if every one of the 36 pairs comes up equally often in the long run, then sum-7 pairs make up
6/36 of all rolls.

### Three rules that save you from counting

Counting works for dice, but most real problems have too many outcomes to list. Three rules let you combine
probabilities you already know.

**1. The complement rule ("not").** Either A happens or it does not, so the two chances add up to 1:

$$
P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A)
$$

This is most useful for "at least one" questions. "At least one 6 in four rolls" has many cases (one 6, two 6s,
...), but its <Term id="complement-rule">complement</Term>, "no 6 at all", has just one. We compute the easy side
and subtract from 1.

**2. The addition rule ("or").** To get the chance that A or B (or both) happens, add the two chances, then
subtract the overlap:

$$
P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \text{ and } B)
$$

Why subtract? Outcomes that are in both A and B were counted once in $P(A)$ and again in $P(B)$. Subtracting
$P(A \text{ and } B)$ removes the double count. When the events cannot happen together (they are
<Term id="mutually-exclusive">mutually exclusive</Term>), the overlap is 0 and you simply add.

**3. The multiplication rule ("and").** If knowing that A happened does not change the chance of B, the events are
<Term id="independent-events">independent</Term>, and

$$
P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \cdot P(B)
$$

Separate rolls of a die are independent (the die has no memory), so the chance of two 6s in two rolls is
$\tfrac16 \cdot \tfrac16 = \tfrac{1}{36}$. If the events are not independent, you cannot just multiply. The next
lesson shows what to do instead.

### Independent is not the same as mutually exclusive

These two words sound related and are constantly mixed up, so it is worth being precise.

- **Mutually exclusive** means A and B **cannot both happen**: "the sum is 2" and "the sum is 12". The overlap is
  empty.
- **Independent** means knowing A **tells you nothing** about B: "the first die is odd" and "the second die is 6".
  They overlap (in 3 outcomes), and that overlap is exactly as large as the product rule predicts.

In fact, two mutually exclusive events with positive probabilities are never independent. If you learn that the sum
was 2, you know for certain the sum was not 12. That is the strongest information one event can give about another.

</Section>

Things to try, and what to look for:

- **"the sum is 7" and "the first die shows 6"**: one cell in both. $1/36 = (6/36) \cdot (6/36)$, so they are
  independent. Surprising at first: learning the first die is a 6 leaves exactly one way to reach 7 out of six
  possible second dice, which is still 1/6.
- **"the sum is 10 or more" and "the first die shows 6"**: three cells in both, but the product rule predicts only
  one. A 6 on the first die makes a high sum much more likely, so the events are dependent.
- **"both dice show the same number" and "the sum is 7"**: no overlap at all. They are mutually exclusive, and the
  readout shows that this makes them dependent.

  title="Outcomes that are not equally likely"

  fix="Count in the sample space of equally likely outcomes (the 36 ordered pairs), then group them into sums."
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Adding probabilities of events that overlap"
  wrong={'P_mobile_or_ad = 0.5 + 0.4      # 0.9'}
  error="(no error) 0.9 overstates it; with a 0.2 overlap the true value is 0.7"
  cause="Users who are both on mobile and from an ad are counted twice."
  fix="Subtract the overlap: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A and B). Only add directly when the events cannot happen together."

<Section kind="practice" title="Where you will use this">

- **Data checks.** "What share of rows have a missing email or a missing phone?" is the addition rule. Computing it
  as the sum of the two shares double counts rows missing both.
- **System reliability.** If three independent services each have 99.9% uptime and a request needs all three, it
  succeeds with probability $0.999^3 \approx 0.997$. If they share a database, they are not independent, and the
  real number can be very different.
- **"At least one" in experiments.** Testing 20 metrics, each with a 5% false alarm chance, gives a
  $1 - 0.95^{20} \approx 64\%$ chance of at least one false alarm. The A/B testing chapter comes back to this.

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

We started with the idea that a probability is a long-run share. To compute that share without repeating anything,
we listed the equally likely outcomes (the sample space), asked a yes/no question about them (an event), and divided
the favourable count by the total. Because listing is not always possible, we picked up three rules that combine
known probabilities: "not" subtracts from 1, "or" adds and removes the double counted overlap, and "and" multiplies,
but only when the events are independent, meaning one tells you nothing about the other. Mutually exclusive events
are the opposite case: knowing one rules the other out.

The open question is what to do when events are **not** independent, which is most of real life: a transaction
that is flagged is more likely to be fraud, a user who clicked once is more likely to click again. That is
conditional probability, next: [Conditional probability and Bayes' rule](#/learn/stats/probability/conditional-probability).

</Section>

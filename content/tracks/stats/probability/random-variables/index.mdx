<Section kind="intuition" title="Putting numbers on outcomes">

So far our outcomes were yes/no: fraud or not, a 7 or not. Most data are numbers: dollars spent, minutes waited,
orders per day. A <Term id="random-variable">random variable</Term> is simply **a number whose value is decided by
chance**. The roll of a die is one. So is the payout of a prize spinner, the size of tomorrow's insurance claims,
or the number of users who sign up today.

Once outcomes are numbers, two questions come up every time, and this lesson answers both:

1. **What value should I expect, on average?** If you play the spinner all evening, will you end up ahead? If an
   insurer sells 10,000 policies, how much will it pay out?
2. **How far from that average does a single result usually land?** Is the payout reliably close to the average, or
   could one evening be a big win and the next a big loss?

The first answer is the expected value. The second is the variance and its square root, the standard deviation.
Everything later in this track, from sampling to A/B tests, is built on these two numbers.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="Expected value and spread">

### The distribution is a table

A random variable is described by its distribution: every value it can take and the probability of each. For a
variable with a list of separate values (a discrete variable), that table is called its
<Term id="pmf">probability mass function</Term>, or PMF. Our running example is a prize spinner:

| Outcome | Value $x$ | Probability $p$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| lose | −$1 | 0.5 |
| small win | +$1 | 0.3 |
| big win | +$4 | 0.2 |

The probabilities add up to 1, because one of the outcomes always happens.

### Expected value: the long-run average

Imagine spinning 1,000 times. About 500 spins lose $1, about 300 win $1 and about 200 win $4. Your total is about
$500 \cdot (-1) + 300 \cdot 1 + 200 \cdot 4 = 600$ dollars, so your average per spin is about $0.60.

Notice what we did: we multiplied each value by how often it happens and added up. Dividing the counts by 1,000
turns them into probabilities, and that gives the definition of the
<Term id="expected-value">expected value</Term>:

$$
E[X] = \sum_x x \cdot P(X = x) = (-1)(0.5) + (1)(0.3) + (4)(0.2) = 0.6
$$

It is a **weighted average**: each value counts in proportion to how often it occurs. The law of large numbers
(from the first lesson) is the promise that makes it meaningful: the average of many independent spins really does
settle near 0.6.

Two things the expected value is **not**. It need not be a possible outcome (no single spin pays $0.60). And it is
not "the typical result": half the spins lose money.

### Variance: how far results usually land from the average

Two games can have the same expected value and feel completely different. "Always pay $0.60" and our spinner both
average 0.6, but the spinner swings between −$1 and +$4. We need a number for that spread.

The natural first idea is "the average distance from the mean". Distances above and below cancel if we keep their
signs, so we square them first. The <Term id="variance">variance</Term> is the probability-weighted average of the
squared distances:

$$
\mathrm{Var}(X) = \sum_x (x - E[X])^2 \cdot P(X = x)
$$

For the spinner: $(−1 − 0.6)^2 (0.5) + (1 − 0.6)^2 (0.3) + (4 − 0.6)^2 (0.2) = 1.28 + 0.048 + 2.312 = 3.64$.

Squaring has a side effect: the units are squared too (dollars²), which means nothing to a person. Taking the square
root brings us back to dollars. That is the <Term id="standard-deviation">standard deviation</Term>:

$$
\mathrm{SD}(X) = \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X)} = \sqrt{3.64} \approx 1.91 \text{ dollars}
$$

Read it as: a single spin typically lands about $1.91 away from the $0.60 average. Look at where the 3.64 came from:
the big win contributes 2.312 of it. Rare values far from the mean dominate the variance, because the distance is
squared.

### Rules you will use again and again

These rules let you get the mean and spread of new quantities without redoing the table. Each has a plain meaning.

- **Shift and stretch:** $E[aX + b] = a\,E[X] + b$ and $\mathrm{SD}(aX + b) = |a|\,\mathrm{SD}(X)$. Converting
  hours to minutes ($a = 60$) stretches every distance by 60. Adding a fixed fee ($b$) moves every value by the same
  amount, so the average moves but the spread does not.
- **Sums:** $E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]$ always. If $X$ and $Y$ are independent,
  $\mathrm{Var}(X + Y) = \mathrm{Var}(X) + \mathrm{Var}(Y)$. Variances add, **standard deviations do not**: two
  independent spins have SD $\sqrt{3.64 + 3.64} \approx 2.70$, not $2 \times 1.91 = 3.82$, because a good spin and a bad
  spin partly cancel.

The second rule is the seed of the most important result in the sampling chapter. The total of $n$ independent spins
has variance $n \cdot 3.64$, so its SD grows like $\sqrt{n}$. The average is the total divided by $n$, so its SD is
$\sqrt{n} \cdot 1.91 / n = 1.91 / \sqrt{n}$: averages of more spins are less spread out. Keep that in mind; it comes
back as the standard error.

</Section>

Those are two different spreads, and confusing them is one of the most common mistakes in statistics. The SD of

player to player (here roughly 0.5 to 0.63) does shrink as each player spins more. The sampling chapter gives that
second spread its name.

<CommonMistake
  title="Reading the expected value as the typical outcome"
  wrong={'"The expected claim is $140, so a typical customer costs us about $140."'}

  cause="The expected value is a weighted average, pulled up by rare large values. It says what the total of many will be, not what one will be."
  fix="Report the expected value for totals and budgets, and the median or the full distribution for 'a typical customer'."

<CommonMistake
  title="Adding standard deviations"
  wrong={'sd_total = sd_a + sd_b        # two independent sources of delay'}
  error="(no error) overstates the spread: 3 + 3 = 6 instead of about 4.24"
  cause="For independent variables, variances add. Standard deviations are square roots of variances, and square roots do not add."
  fix="sd_total = (sd_a**2 + sd_b**2) ** 0.5. If the variables are correlated, add the covariance term as well."
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Probabilities that do not add up to 1"
  wrong={'values = [0, 500, 5000]\nprobs  = [0.9, 0.08, 0.01]'}
  error="(no error) E[X] comes out as $90 instead of $140, because 1% of the probability is missing"
  cause="An outcome was left out or a probability was mistyped; the weighted average silently ignores the missing share."
  fix="Assert abs(sum(probs) - 1) < 1e-9 before computing anything, as the exercise does."

<Section kind="practice" title="Where you will use this">

- **Cost and capacity planning.** Expected requests per user times the number of users gives expected load; the SD
  tells you how much headroom to keep.
- **Decisions under uncertainty.** "Should we ship this?" is often an expected value: the chance it works times the
  gain, minus the chance it fails times the loss. The Bayesian A/B lesson uses exactly this as "expected loss".
- **Loss functions.** Training a model minimises the expected loss over the data, approximated by the average loss on
  a batch. Mean squared error is a variance-like quantity: the average squared distance from the truth.

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

We gave outcomes numbers and called the result a random variable, described by a table of values and probabilities.
Asking "what do I get on average over many plays?" led to the expected value, a probability-weighted average that the
law of large numbers makes real. Asking "how far does one play usually land from that?" led to the variance, the
weighted average of squared distances, and its square root, the standard deviation, which is back in the original
units. Two rules connect them to everything that follows: shifting and stretching a variable moves and scales these
numbers in predictable ways, and for independent variables the variances add, which is why an average of many draws
is less spread out than a single draw.

Writing out a table by hand works for three outcomes. Many real quantities follow a few standard shapes, and knowing
them saves the work: [Bernoulli, binomial and Poisson](#/learn/stats/distributions/binomial-poisson).

</Section>

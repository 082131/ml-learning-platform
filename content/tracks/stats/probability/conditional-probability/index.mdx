<Section kind="intuition" title="New information shrinks the world">

You run a fraud model on card payments. It is good: it flags 95% of fraudulent payments and only 5% of legitimate
ones. A payment just got flagged. How likely is it to be fraud?

Most people answer "about 95%". The real answer, with 1 fraudulent payment in every 100, is about 16%. Six out of
seven flagged payments are false alarms, from a model that sounds excellent. This lesson is about why, and the idea
behind it is simple once you see it: **when you learn something, you stop looking at everything that could have
happened and look only at the cases where what you learned is true.** "The payment was flagged" means: forget the
payments that were not flagged, and ask what share of the flagged ones are fraud.

That shrinking of the world is called conditioning, and it is what the previous lesson's multiplication rule was
missing. It is also the most practical idea in this chapter. Every time a model, a test or an alert says
"positive", someone has to decide how much to trust it, and that trust depends on how common the real thing was to
begin with.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="From counts to Bayes' rule">

### Conditional probability: the share inside the smaller world

The <Term id="conditional-probability">conditional probability</Term> $P(A \mid B)$, read "the probability of A
given B", is the share of B-cases in which A also happens:

$$
P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \text{ and } B)}{P(B)}
$$

Read the formula as the "shrink the world" idea. The denominator, $P(B)$, is the new world: only the cases where B
happened. The numerator, $P(A \text{ and } B)$, is the part of that world where A happens too. Dividing gives the
share.

A tiny example with counts. In a class of 100, 40 students did a practice exam and 24 of those passed. Then
$P(\text{passed} \mid \text{practised}) = 24/40 = 0.6$. We ignored the 60 who did not practise, because the
condition says we only care about those who did.

### The order inside the bar matters

$P(A \mid B)$ and $P(B \mid A)$ have the same "and" on top but different worlds underneath:

- $P(\text{flagged} \mid \text{fraud})$: among fraudulent payments, the share the model flags. This is the model's
  <Term id="sensitivity">sensitivity</Term> (also called recall or true positive rate), here 95%.
- $P(\text{fraud} \mid \text{flagged})$: among flagged payments, the share that are fraud. This is what the person
  reviewing the alert needs to know (it is also the model's precision), and it is only 16%.

They differ because the worlds have different sizes. There are 1,000 fraudulent payments but 5,900 flagged ones, so
the same 950 "fraud and flagged" payments are a big share of the first world and a small share of the second.

### Natural frequencies: the tree

The clearest way to get from one to the other is to imagine a concrete crowd. Take 100,000 payments:

1. **Split by the truth.** The <Term id="base-rate">base rate</Term> of fraud is 1%, so 1,000 are fraud and 99,000
   are legitimate.
2. **Split each group by the model.** It flags 95% of the 1,000 fraud (950 caught, 50 missed) and, through its
   <Term id="false-positive-rate">false positive rate</Term> of 5%, 5% of the 99,000 legitimate payments (4,950
   false alarms).
3. **Condition on what you saw.** Everything flagged: 950 + 4,950 = 5,900. The fraudulent share: 950 / 5,900 ≈
   16.1%.

Why so low? Because the 5% false alarm rate is applied to a huge group (99,000) while the 95% catch rate is applied
to a small one (1,000). A small rate times a big group beats a big rate times a small group.

### Bayes' rule is the tree written as a formula

If you write the three steps with probabilities instead of counts, you get <Term id="bayes-rule">Bayes' rule</Term>:

$$
P(\text{fraud} \mid \text{flag}) =
\frac{P(\text{flag} \mid \text{fraud}) \cdot P(\text{fraud})}
{P(\text{flag} \mid \text{fraud}) \cdot P(\text{fraud}) + P(\text{flag} \mid \text{legit}) \cdot P(\text{legit})}
$$

Each part maps to the tree:

- $P(\text{flag} \mid \text{fraud}) \cdot P(\text{fraud}) = 0.95 \cdot 0.01 = 0.0095$: the "fraud and flagged" branch
  (950 out of 100,000). This is the multiplication rule for events that are **not** independent:
  $P(A \text{ and } B) = P(B \mid A) \cdot P(A)$.
- $P(\text{flag} \mid \text{legit}) \cdot P(\text{legit}) = 0.05 \cdot 0.99 = 0.0495$: the false alarm branch (4,950).
- The denominator adds every way to get flagged: $0.0095 + 0.0495 = 0.059$, the 5,900.
- The ratio $0.0095 / 0.059 \approx 0.161$.

So Bayes' rule is not a new law to memorise. It is "of everything that matches what I saw, what share is the thing I
care about", written with probabilities.

### Prior, evidence, posterior

The rule also describes how beliefs should change. Before the flag, the chance of fraud was the base rate, 1%: the
**prior**. The flag is **evidence**. After seeing it, the chance is 16.1%: the **posterior**. The flag made fraud
sixteen times more likely, but not likely. If a second, independent check also flags the payment, today's posterior
becomes the next prior, and the chance climbs to about 79% (the knowledge check works it out). The Bayesian chapter
at the end of this track builds a whole way of doing statistics on this update step.

<Section kind="mistakes" title="Reading conditional probabilities backwards">

<CommonMistake
  title="Confusing P(A | B) with P(B | A)"
  wrong={'"The model catches 95% of fraud, so a flagged payment is 95% likely to be fraud."'}
  error="(no error) the real P(fraud | flagged) at a 1% base rate is 16%"
  cause="95% is P(flagged | fraud). The question is P(fraud | flagged). They share a numerator but divide by different groups: all fraud (1,000) versus all flagged (5,900)."
  fix="Say both in words before computing: 'among fraud, the share flagged' and 'among flagged, the share fraud'. Then build the tree."
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Ignoring the base rate"
  wrong={'precision = recall   # assumed equal when choosing an alert threshold'}
  error="(no error) the alert queue is mostly false alarms and reviewers start ignoring it"
  cause="Precision depends on how common positives are; recall does not. A model evaluated on a balanced test set (50% fraud) looks far more precise than it will be in production (1% fraud)."
  fix="Report precision at the production base rate, or test on data with realistic class balance. Bayes' rule converts between them."
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Multiplying as if the events were independent"
  wrong={'P_fraud_and_flag = 0.01 * (5900 / 100_000)   # P(fraud) * P(flag)'}
  error="(no error) gives 0.00059, but the true value is 950 / 100,000 = 0.0095, 16 times larger"
  cause="Fraud and flags are strongly dependent: that is the whole point of a fraud model. The product rule only holds for independent events."
  fix="Use the general rule: P(A and B) = P(B | A) * P(A) = 0.95 * 0.01."
/>

</Section>

<Section kind="practice" title="Where you will use this">

- **Alert thresholds.** Before shipping a classifier that pages someone, compute P(real problem | alert) at the real
  base rate. If it is 5%, people will learn to ignore the pager.
- **Evaluation on resampled data.** If you balanced the classes for training or testing, precision on that data is
  not production precision. Re-weight with the real base rate.
- **Naive Bayes and spam filters** apply Bayes' rule once per word, treating the words as independent given the
  class. The update-the-prior idea is the same as here.
- **A/B testing.** "The test says significant" is a positive result too. How much to trust it depends on how many of
  the ideas you test really work, which is a base rate. The hypothesis testing lesson returns to this.

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

We began with a model that sounds 95% accurate yet is right only 16% of the time it raises an alert. To see why, we
treated new information as shrinking the world: given a flag, look only at the flagged cases. That gave the
conditional probability $P(A \mid B)$, the share of B-cases where A also holds, and showed it is not the same as
$P(B \mid A)$ because the two divide by different groups. Building a tree of 100,000 payments made the difference
visible: a small false alarm rate applied to the many legitimate payments outweighs a high catch rate applied to the
few fraudulent ones. Writing that tree with probabilities is Bayes' rule, and reading it as prior, evidence and
posterior shows how a belief should move when evidence arrives.

So far our events have been yes/no. Next we attach numbers to outcomes (a payout, a count, a time) and ask what
value to expect on average and how far a single result usually lands from it:
[Random variables, expected value and variance](#/learn/stats/probability/random-variables).

</Section>

<Section kind="intuition" title="The electricity bill of a house where nobody switches anything off">

Imagine a shared house where the lights, the heating and the oven are never switched off, the bill arrives a month
later as one number, and nobody knows whose room used what. The bill is high, everyone suspects everyone else, and
the usual suggestion is to buy cheaper light bulbs. The real fix is boring: meters per room, a warning when the month
is heading over budget, and switches that turn things off when nobody is home.

Cloud bills for ML teams look exactly like that house. Training GPUs get the attention, but the bill is often
dominated by things nobody is using: notebooks running all night, an endpoint sized for a launch that never came,
checkpoints from last spring, data copied across regions every day. And the bill arrives as "EC2: \$4,000" rather than
"the search experiment: \$4,000".

This lesson builds the habits that fix it, in the order that matters: make the bill readable, get warned in time,
then remove the leaks, largest first, and only then haggle over the price per hour.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="From one number to named lines, and from lines to fixes">

**The big picture.** You cannot cut what you cannot see → so first give every cost an owner with tags → then get
warned before the month is spent, with budgets and anomaly alerts → then read the bill as lines with formulas, hours ×
price, and find the largest → most large lines are idle time, so remove the idle hours → and only for the usage that
remains, lower the price with spot or commitments.

### Step 1: make the bill readable with tags

Every cloud resource can carry labels: a <Term id="cost-allocation-tag">cost allocation tag</Term> such as
`project=search` or `team=ranking`. Billing tools can then group costs by them, which turns "SageMaker: \$3,100" into
"the ranking team's nightly retraining: \$2,400, the search notebooks: \$700". Two details trip teams up:

- **Activation.** On AWS, a tag you add to resources only appears in Cost Explorer after you activate it as a cost
  allocation tag in the Billing console. On Google Cloud, labels appear in the billing export (to BigQuery). Do it on
  day one, before the costs you will want to explain.
- **Enforcement.** Tags only help if they are always there. Put them in infrastructure code (the next lesson), pass
  them on every training job, and enforce them with a tag policy or a guardrail that denies creating untagged
  resources. The size of the "untagged" row in your report is the measure of how well this works.

The goal is **unit cost**: not "we spent \$7,000", but "a retraining run costs \$40", "a thousand predictions cost
\$0.12". Unit costs tell you whether spending grew because the business grew or because something leaked.

### Step 2: get warned in time, and know that a warning is not a stop

A <Term id="cloud-budget">budget</Term> (AWS Budgets, Google Cloud budgets) sets a monthly amount and sends alerts
when actual spend, or the forecast for the month, crosses a percentage of it. Two facts shape how to use it:

1. **A budget notifies; it does not cap.** Nothing stops when the alert fires, unless you add an action: AWS Budgets
   can apply an IAM or guardrail policy or stop chosen EC2 and RDS instances, and on Google Cloud you can connect the
   budget's Pub/Sub messages to a function of your own. Real hard limits come from elsewhere: quotas (the GPU quota
   from the GPU lesson is a cost control too), guardrail policies that forbid the largest instance types, and
   auto-stop rules.
2. **Billing data arrives late.** Cost data is updated several times a day, not instantly. A runaway loop that starts
   on Friday evening can run for many hours before any alert based on actual spend fires. That is why you alert on
   the **forecast** (for example at 80%) and use anomaly detection (AWS Cost Anomaly Detection), which flags a service
   or tag whose daily spend jumps far above its usual pattern.

### Step 3: read the bill as lines, hours × price

Here is a typical month for a small ML team, with illustrative prices. Each line is a formula, and writing it out is
what reveals the fix:

| Line | Formula | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Training GPUs | 300 GPU-hours × \$4 | \$1,200 |
| Notebooks | 4 notebooks × 730 h × \$1.20 | \$3,504 |
| Model endpoint | 2 instances × 730 h × \$1 | \$1,460 |
| Object storage | 40,000 GB × \$0.023 | \$920 |
| Data transfer out | 2,000 GB × \$0.09 | \$180 |
| **Total** | | **\$7,264** |

The surprise is in the second line. Training, which everyone worries about, is a sixth of the bill. The notebooks are
almost half, and the formula says why: **730 hours** is every hour of the month, while people use them about 174
(8 hours on 21.75 working days). Three quarters of the line is paid for nobody.

### Step 4: remove idle time, which is most of the leaks

Most large lines in an ML bill are hours × price where many of the hours are idle. The usual suspects:

- **Notebooks and development machines.** Turn on <Term id="idle-shutdown">idle shutdown</Term> (SageMaker Studio
  and Vertex AI Workbench both offer it). In the example, 730 → 174 hours saves \$2,669, 37% of the whole bill.
- **Forgotten instances, volumes and addresses.** A GPU VM nobody stopped (the managed-training lesson's 14 idle
  hours), block storage volumes left behind when instances were terminated, snapshots, and public IPv4 addresses,
  which AWS bills by the hour. A weekly report of resources without recent activity catches them.
- **Endpoints sized for the peak.** The model-APIs lesson showed that a fleet sized for the busiest hour sits mostly
  idle. Autoscaling (or a serverless endpoint for occasional traffic) brings the average from 2 instances to about
  1.1 in the example, saving \$657.
- **Storage that only grows.** Every spot-safe run writes checkpoints, and with versioning each overwrite keeps the
  old copy. <Term id="lifecycle-rule">Lifecycle rules</Term> expire old checkpoints and noncurrent versions, move
  archives to cheaper <Term id="storage-class">storage classes</Term>, and abort incomplete multipart uploads, whose
  parts are billed but invisible in a normal listing. Halving the stored data saves \$460 here.
- **Data on the move.** <Term id="egress">Egress</Term> and cross-region copies, from the moving-data lesson, and NAT
  gateway processing charges, from the networking lesson (an S3 gateway endpoint removes them for S3 traffic).

### Step 5: only then, lower the price of what remains

Once the idle hours are gone, the remaining usage is real, and two price levers apply to it:

- **Spot** for work that can resume, as in the spot lesson. Moving all training to spot at 60% off saves \$720 here:
  worthwhile, but the second-largest saving, not the first.
- **A <Term id="commitment-discount">commitment</Term>** (AWS Savings Plans or Reserved Instances, Google Cloud
  committed use discounts): a lower rate for promising one or three years of a certain usage. The catch is in the
  word promise: you pay for the commitment whether or not you use it. So the order matters. **Scale down first, then
  commit to the steady floor** you are certain to use. Committing to 2 always-on endpoint instances and then
  autoscaling to an average of 1.1 leaves almost half of the commitment paid for and unused.

Applied together, the controls in the example bring \$7,264 down to about \$2,517, 65% less, without making a single
training run slower. That ordering, visibility, then warnings, then idle time, then price, is the core of what the
industry calls FinOps.

<Callout kind="simplification">
The bill is illustrative: instance and notebook prices are round numbers, the spot discount is a fixed 60%, the
commitment a flat 30% on the endpoint, autoscaling a fixed 55% of the instances on average, and lifecycle rules a
fixed 50% of the stored data. Storage and egress use Amazon S3 Standard and internet data transfer list prices in US
East (first tiers). Real bills add request charges, taxes, support plans and many smaller lines, and real discounts
vary by instance type, region and term.
</Callout>

<Section kind="practice" title="Cost habits for an ML team">

- **Tag from day one** (project, team, environment, owner), activate the tags for billing, and enforce them in code.
- **Set a budget per team or project** with a forecast alert and anomaly detection, routed to people who can act.
- **Turn on idle shutdown** for every notebook and development instance, and review unused resources weekly.
- **Autoscale or go serverless** for endpoints, and stop non-production endpoints outside working hours.
- **Put lifecycle rules on every bucket** that holds checkpoints, logs or intermediate data.
- **Use spot for resumable work**, and commit only after usage is stable, to the floor.
- **Track unit costs** (per training run, per thousand predictions) next to the total.

<Section kind="summary">

A cloud bill becomes manageable when it stops being one number. Tags give each cost an owner, budgets with forecast
and anomaly alerts warn while there is still time (they warn, they do not stop), and writing each line as hours ×
price shows where the money goes. In ML bills, the largest lines are usually idle hours: notebooks running all night,
endpoints sized for the peak, forgotten machines, and storage that only grows.

So the order of work follows from the formulas: remove idle hours first (idle shutdown, autoscaling, lifecycle
rules), then lower the price of the usage that remains, with spot for resumable work and commitments only for the
steady floor. In the example that order cuts the month by about two thirds without slowing any training run.

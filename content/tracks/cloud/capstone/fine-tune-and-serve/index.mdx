<Section kind="intuition" title="One plan, every lesson">

Each lesson in this track answered one question: where machines live, who may do what, how traffic flows, how data
reaches the GPUs, which GPU fits, how to survive spot interruptions, when to let a managed service run the job, when a
hosted API beats your own GPUs, where the money goes, and how to build all of it from code. Real projects ask all of
those questions at once, and the answers constrain each other: the cheapest GPU may be too small, the cheapest
capacity may miss the deadline, the quickest network setup may be open to the internet.

This capstone gives you one realistic request and asks for a plan that satisfies all of it, then for the plan built
and run. The request: **fine-tune an 8-billion-parameter open-weight model with LoRA four times a month, each run
finished within 30 hours, serve it to an internal application, and keep the whole thing under \$2,500 a month, with no
long-lived keys and nothing reachable from the internet.**

</Section>

the decisions interact (instance → hours → spot → deadline → cost) → so you settle them in order of how hard they
constrain → and the plan passes when every check passes with a figure you can show.

### 1. Memory decides which instances are even candidates

Start with the hardest constraint, because nothing else matters if the model does not fit. LoRA keeps the base
weights frozen in 16-bit precision, about 2 bytes per parameter, and adds small trainable adapters; with the GPU
lesson's 20% headroom that is 8 × 2 × 1.2 = **19.2 GB**. The 16 GB T4 (g4dn.xlarge) is out; every 24 GB card or larger
is in. (4-bit QLoRA would fit the T4, at some cost in speed and a small one in quality: a valid alternative if you
write down why.)

### 2. Measured hours, then capacity, decide the deadline

You cannot plan a deadline from a guess. Run the real script for 30 minutes on the candidate instance, read the steps
per second, and extrapolate to the full run: here, **20 hours on g6.xlarge** (one L4). Hours belong to an instance, so
switching instances means measuring again.

Twenty hours fits in thirty on demand. On spot, the spot lesson's formula decides. With 0.05 interruptions per hour
and **no checkpoints**, the run needs 20 uninterrupted hours, and the expected wall-clock time is 34.7 hours: the
deadline fails. With a checkpoint every **60 minutes**, the expected time is 21.8 hours: it passes, at 35% of the
price. Young's rule puts the best interval at √(2 × 0.05 h × 20 h) ≈ 1.4 hours; because the total is flat near the
best value, an hour costs almost nothing extra and keeps each loss small.

On a managed job this is two settings plus a script that resumes: `use_spot_instances`, `checkpoint_s3_uri`, and a
`max_wait` with room for capacity waits (the managed-training lesson).

### 3. The month, line by line

Now the bill. Training: 21.8 hours × \$0.28 (the L4 at 65% off) × 4 runs ≈ **\$24**. Serving: an endpoint with one 24 GB
GPU at \$0.80 an hour. Two always-on instances cost \$1,168 a month; autoscaling between 1 and 3, averaging 1.4,
costs **\$818**. Data in the same region costs nothing to read. Total: about **\$842**, a third of the budget.

The shape of that total is the lesson of the whole track in one line: the part people worry about (GPU training) is
3% of the bill, and the part that runs all month (serving) is 97%. Effort goes where the money is. The model-APIs
lesson's question is worth asking here too: at your expected traffic, would a hosted model API serving a similar
model cost less than \$818 a month? If traffic is low, it may, and the fine-tune would then need a provider that hosts
custom adapters.

### 4. Security and placement are not optional extras

Three checks have no price but fail the plan just the same:

- **Credentials.** The training job and the endpoint run as IAM roles with exactly the bucket prefixes they need.
  Access keys in a config file never expire and leak with the image or the repository.
- **Network.** Training and serving live in private subnets, S3 is reached through a gateway endpoint, and the
  endpoint is reachable only from inside the VPC. A public subnet with port 8888 open is reachable from anywhere.
- **Data placement.** The 200 GB dataset sits in the GPUs' region. In another region each of the 12 monthly reads
  costs transfer, runs slower, and may move data where it is not allowed.

### 5. Build it from code, and prove it

The plan is then built as Terraform (network, roles, buckets, endpoint, budget), run as a managed spot job, and
proved: one deliberate interruption that resumes from the last checkpoint, a smoke test of the endpoint, a budget with
forecast alerts, a bill compared with the estimate, and a clean `terraform destroy`.

<Callout kind="simplification">
The checker uses the track's illustrative prices and assumptions: a fixed interruption rate of 0.05 per hour, 3-minute
checkpoints, 10-minute restarts, a 65% spot discount, a serving instance at \$0.80 an hour and an autoscaled average
of 1.4 instances. Training hours are an input you would measure. Real interruption rates, discounts and prices vary
by instance type, region and hour, so the numbers in your DESIGN.md should come from your own benchmark, the current
price pages and your provider's spot advisor.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

A cloud plan for ML is a chain of checks, and each link comes from one lesson. Memory decides which GPUs are
candidates; a benchmark on the chosen instance gives the hours; the spot formula with checkpoints turns those hours
into an expected wall-clock time against the deadline; the bill, written as named lines, shows that serving rather
than training usually dominates; and roles, private networking and data placement are checks that cost nothing and
fail the plan just as surely as a missed deadline.

Built from code, run as a managed spot job, served behind an autoscaling private endpoint and watched by a budget,
the plan becomes something a reviewer can verify with the same arithmetic you used, and something you can tear
down and rebuild in an afternoon. That is what "cloud for ML" means in practice: not knowing every service, but
knowing which question each decision answers, and checking it with a number.

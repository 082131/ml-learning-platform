<Section kind="intuition" title="Renting someone else's computers, by the second">

Training a model needs a lot of computing for a short time. A team might need eight GPUs for three days this week
and none next week. Buying those GPUs would cost hundreds of thousands of dollars, and they would sit idle most of
the year. "The cloud" is the alternative: a company that owns enormous buildings full of computers rents them out,
and you pay only for the minutes you use.

So the simplest mental model is a **utility**, like electricity. You don't build a power station to boil a kettle;
you plug into the grid and pay for what you draw. In the cloud you ask for a machine with a GPU, it is ready in a
couple of minutes, and the meter runs until you give it back.

That model immediately raises the questions this lesson answers. *Where* are those computers? What happens when one
of them, or a whole building, fails? And if you are renting, which problems are the landlord's and which are yours?

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="Regions, zones, and who is responsible for what">

**The big picture first.** Every decision in this lesson follows one chain: you pick a **region** (which city your
work runs in) → inside it you place copies of your work in one or more **zones** (separate buildings) → that layout
decides how often your service is down, how fast it answers, and what it costs.

### Regions: which city

A <Term id="cloud-region">region</Term> is a geographic area where the provider runs data centres, such as AWS
`us-east-1` (Northern Virginia) or Google Cloud `europe-west4` (Netherlands). Regions are deliberately independent:
a disaster or a software fault in one should not spread to another.

You choose a region for four reasons, and it helps to know which one is driving the choice:

1. **Where the data already is.** Training reads the dataset many times. If the data sits in a bucket in one region
   and the GPUs in another, you pay to move it every time (the storage chapter puts numbers on this). This is
   usually the strongest reason for ML work.
2. **Where the users are.** For a model that answers requests, every extra thousand kilometres adds round-trip time
   (light in fibre covers roughly 200 km per millisecond, and real routes are longer).
3. **Rules about data.** Laws or contracts may require that personal data stays in a country or the EU.
4. **What is available and what it costs.** The newest GPU types appear in a few regions first, and prices differ by
   region.

### Zones: which building

Inside a region are several <Term id="availability-zone">availability zones</Term> (AWS) or *zones* (Google Cloud):
groups of data centres with their own power, cooling and network, a few kilometres to tens of kilometres apart.
They are close enough that traffic between them takes about a millisecond, and far enough apart that a fire, a
flood or a power failure usually takes out only one.

Why both levels? Because they protect against different things. Zones protect you from the *common* failures (one
building has a problem) while keeping your copies close together and fast. Regions protect you from the *rare*
failures (a whole area has a problem), at the cost of distance and of copying your data across.

### What "99.9% available" means

<Term id="availability-sla">Availability</Term> is the fraction of time something works. Before calculating
anything, translate it into hours, because that is what you will feel: a year has 24 × 365 = 8,760 hours, so

- 99% up means 1% down: 87.6 hours a year, more than three and a half days;
- 99.9% up means 8.76 hours a year;
- 99.99% up means 53 minutes a year.

Each extra "nine" divides the downtime by ten. Now, how do the parts combine into the whole? There are two rules,
and every layout is built from them.

**Parts in series multiply.** If *A and B must both* work (the zone must have power *and* the VM inside it must be
healthy), the chance of success is the product: 0.999 × 0.99 = 0.98901. A chain is weaker than its weakest link.

**Copies in parallel fail only together.** If *any one* of n copies is enough, the service fails only when all n
are down at the same moment. One copy is down with chance (1 − p), so all n are down with chance (1 − p)ⁿ, and

$$
P(\text{at least one up}) = 1 - (1 - p)^n .
$$

With p = 0.99 and two copies: 1 − 0.01² = 1 − 0.0001 = 0.9999. One extra copy turned "down 1% of the time" into
"down 0.01% of the time", because both copies now have to fail at once.

### Putting the rules together: why zones matter

Here is the result that surprises most people. Take two VMs. Put both in **one zone**: they protect you against VM
failures, but the zone itself is still a single point of failure, so the zone's 99.9% caps everything:
0.999 × (1 − 0.01²) ≈ 0.9989. Put them in **two zones**, one each: now a zone failure takes out only one copy,
and the arithmetic gives about 1.9 hours down a year instead of 10.5. Same two VMs, same price, five times less
downtime. You will reproduce these numbers in the experiment and in the code below.

<Callout kind="simplification">
The formulas assume failures are **independent**: one copy failing tells you nothing about the others. Real failures
are often correlated. A bad deployment pushed to every copy, an expired certificate or a full disk breaks all copies
at once, whatever zone they are in. Redundancy protects against hardware and building failures, not against your
own mistakes; that is what staged rollouts are for (see the
[Deploy track's rollouts lesson](#/learn/deploy/kubernetes/autoscaling-rollouts)).
</Callout>

### Who is responsible for what

Renting changes who has to worry about each failure. Providers describe this as the
<Term id="shared-responsibility">shared responsibility model</Term>: the provider is responsible for security and
reliability *of* the cloud (buildings, hardware, the network, the hypervisor), and you are responsible for what you
put *in* it (your data, who can access it, how your machines and networks are configured, your code).

How much is yours depends on the service model, often summarised as <Term id="iaas-paas-saas">IaaS, PaaS and SaaS</Term>:

| Model | You rent | You still manage | ML example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Infrastructure as a service | a virtual machine, a disk | the OS, drivers, CUDA, your code, scaling | an EC2 or Compute Engine GPU VM |
| Platform as a service | a service that runs your code | your code, data and configuration | a SageMaker or Vertex AI training job |
| Software as a service | a finished product | only your inputs and access | a hosted model API |

The further down the table, the less you manage and the less you control. Most ML teams use all three, and the
compute and managed-services chapters are about choosing between them.

<Section kind="practice" title="How teams choose a region and a layout">

- **Start from the data.** Train where the data lives; serve close to the users. These can be different regions.
- **Check capacity early.** Confirm the GPU instance types you need are offered in your region and zones, and
  request quota before you need it (the GPU lesson covers quotas).
- **Spread across zones by default** for anything that serves traffic. Single-zone is fine for a training job that
  can simply be restarted.
- **Translate nines into hours** when you talk about reliability, and agree on a number before designing.
- **Know your half of the shared responsibility model:** access, data, network configuration and patching your own
  machines are yours.

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

The cloud is rented computing, paid by use. Those computers live in **regions** (cities), and each region has
several **zones** (separate buildings). You pick the region mainly for where the data is, then for users, rules and
available GPUs.

Availability follows from the layout through two rules: parts that must *all* work multiply their chances, and
copies where *any one* is enough fail only together, 1 − (1 − p)ⁿ. Putting those together explains the key result:
copies in the same zone are capped by that zone, so spreading the same copies across zones cuts downtime far more
than adding copies in one place. The math assumes independent failures, so it protects against hardware and
buildings, not against a bad deploy.

Finally, renting splits the work: the provider runs the buildings and hardware; your data, access and configuration
are yours. The next lesson starts with the most important of those: who is allowed to do what.

</Section>

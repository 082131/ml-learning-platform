<Section kind="intuition" title="A cheap seat that you might have to give up">

Airlines sell standby tickets: much cheaper, but if the flight fills up you stay behind. Cloud providers do the same
with spare machines. At any moment some of their GPUs are unused, so they rent them out at a large discount, as
<Term id="spot-instance">spot capacity</Term>, on the condition that they can take them back when a full-price
customer needs them.

For training, that deal is very attractive and slightly dangerous. Attractive, because GPU hours are the biggest
line on an ML bill, and spot can cut it by more than half. Dangerous, because if the machine disappears at hour 19
of a 20-hour job and nothing was saved, you start again from zero.

The tool that turns the danger into a small cost is the **checkpoint**: saving the training state regularly so an
interruption loses only the work since the last save. This lesson works out how much an interruption really costs,
how often to save, and how to write a training loop that shrugs interruptions off.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="Interruptions, checkpoints and the best interval">

**The big picture.** An interruption rate tells you how often you lose the machine → each loss costs the work since
the last checkpoint plus a restart → checkpoints reduce that loss but cost time to write → the best interval balances
the two → and the expected time × the spot price, compared with the on-demand cost, decides whether spot is worth it.

### What spot is, exactly

On AWS, Spot Instances are the same EC2 instance types at a price that moves with supply and demand; AWS says the
discount can reach 90%, and for GPU families it is often around half. On Google Cloud, Spot VMs work the same way at
a fixed discount. When the provider needs the capacity back, you get an <Term id="interruption-notice">interruption
notice</Term>: **two minutes** on AWS (visible in the instance metadata and as an EventBridge event) and **30
seconds** on Google Cloud. Then the machine is stopped or terminated.

Interruptions are not evenly spread: some instance types and zones are interrupted rarely, the newest GPUs in busy
regions more often. AWS publishes an interruption-frequency range per type (the Spot Instance Advisor). For
planning, we describe it with one number: λ (lambda), the average number of interruptions per hour of running. With
λ = 0.1, the *mean time between interruptions* is M = 1/λ = 10 hours.

### Without checkpoints: why long jobs never finish

Start with the worst case so the rest has something to fix. With no checkpoints, a W-hour job must run W hours in
one go. For short jobs that is likely; for long ones it becomes almost impossible, because the chance of no
interruption in W hours falls exponentially (it is e^(−λW)). The expected wall-clock time, including a restart of R
hours after each interruption, is

$$
E[T] = \left(\tfrac{1}{\lambda} + R\right)\left(e^{\lambda W} - 1\right).
$$

With λ = 0.1, R = 10 minutes and W = 20 hours: (10 + 0.17)(e² − 1) ≈ **65 hours**. At 35% of the on-demand price,
65 hours costs more than just paying for 20 hours on demand. For a 40-hour job the expectation is over 500 hours.
Without checkpoints, spot is a trap for long training runs.

### With checkpoints: two small costs instead of one huge one

A <Term id="checkpoint">checkpoint</Term> here means everything needed to continue: model weights, optimizer state,
the step number, and the data loader's position. Saving every τ hours of work changes the arithmetic into two
simple costs:

1. **The cost of saving.** Every τ hours you pause C hours to write the checkpoint, so even with no interruptions the
   job takes W(1 + C/τ). Save every 30 minutes with a 3-minute save: 10% extra.
2. **The cost of losing work.** An interruption lands, on average, halfway through a segment, so it loses
   (τ + C)/2 plus the restart R. At λ interruptions per hour, the fraction of time wasted is λ((τ + C)/2 + R).

Putting the two together, the expected time is

$$
T = \frac{W\,(1 + C/\tau)}{1 - \lambda\left(\frac{\tau + C}{2} + R\right)} .
$$

The numerator is "work plus saving"; the denominator shrinks the useful share of each hour by the waste. With
τ = 30 min, C = 3 min, R = 10 min, λ = 0.1 and W = 20 h: 22 / (1 − 0.1 × (0.275 + 0.167)) ≈ **23 hours**. At 35% of
an on-demand price of \$1/hour, that is about \$8 instead of \$20.

### Choosing the interval

The two costs pull in opposite directions. Save very often and the first term dominates (you spend the run saving);
save rarely and the second dominates (each interruption throws away hours). The best interval balances them. A
classic result from 1974, **Young's rule**, gives it in one line:

$$
\tau^{*} \approx \sqrt{2\,C\,M} ,
$$

with C the time to save one checkpoint and M the mean time between interruptions. With C = 3 minutes and M = 10
hours: √(2 × 0.05 × 10) = 1 hour. The useful insight is in the shape: a slow save (large C) or rare interruptions
(large M) both mean save less often, and the total cost is flat near the best value, so "roughly right" is fine.

<Callout kind="simplification">
The model assumes interruptions arrive at a constant random rate and that the restart is instant once a machine is
available. In reality capacity can be unavailable for a while after an interruption (the job waits, unbilled but
late), and interruption rates change by hour and zone. Managed services help: SageMaker managed spot training
copies `/opt/ml/checkpoints` to S3 and restarts the job for you, with a maximum wait time you set.
</Callout>

<Section kind="practice" title="Spot training that teams rely on">

- **Use spot for work that can resume**: training with checkpoints, hyperparameter sweeps, batch inference. Keep
  serving and deadline-critical jobs on on-demand or reserved capacity.
- **Checkpoint everything** needed to resume, atomically, to object storage.
- **Pick the interval with Young's rule** from your measured save time and the interruption rate, then round.
- **Handle the warning**: save on SIGTERM; two minutes on AWS is usually enough for one more save.
- **Spread across instance types and zones** (capacity-optimized allocation on AWS) so one busy pool does not stop
  you, and let managed spot training retry for you.

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

Spot capacity is spare cloud hardware at a deep discount that the provider can reclaim with a short warning: two
minutes on AWS, 30 seconds on Google Cloud. Without checkpoints, long jobs on spot rarely finish, because the chance
of an uninterrupted run falls exponentially with its length.

Checkpoints replace that one huge risk with two small, predictable costs: the pause to save every τ hours, and,
per interruption, half a segment of lost work plus a restart. The expected time is "work plus saving" divided by
the useful share of each hour, and Young's rule, τ* ≈ √(2CM), balances the two costs. Multiply that time by the spot
price and compare with on-demand to decide.

<Section kind="intuition" title="Renting a car, a taxi, or a ride by the minute">

There are three ways to get across town. You can rent a car for the month: it is always there, you pay whether you
drive or not, and you maintain it. You can take taxis: you pay per trip, nothing when you stay home, but each trip
costs more per kilometre and you wait for one to arrive. Or something in between: a car-share that a service keeps
fuelled and parked for you.

Cloud compute comes in the same three shapes. A **virtual machine** is the monthly rental. A **serverless
function** is the taxi: it runs only when a request arrives and you pay per request. **Container services** sit in
between: you hand over a packaged application and the platform runs and scales it.

None of them is "best". The right one depends on how steady your work is, how long each piece of work takes, and
whether it needs a GPU. This lesson turns that into numbers you can compare.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="The options, what they manage, and what they cost">

**The big picture.** Describe the workload (how many requests or jobs, how long each runs, how much memory, GPU or
not) → see which options can run it at all → price the ones that can for a month → and choose, checking limits
such as cold starts and time limits.

### The four shapes

| Option | You hand over | The platform manages | You pay for | AWS / Google Cloud |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <Term id="virtual-machine">Virtual machine</Term> | an image and a size | hardware | every hour it runs | EC2 / Compute Engine |
| Container service | a <Term id="container-image">container image</Term> | machines, scaling, restarts | running containers (per second) | ECS and Fargate / Cloud Run |
| Managed <Term id="kubernetes">Kubernetes</Term> | Kubernetes manifests | the control plane | the cluster's machines | EKS / GKE |
| <Term id="serverless-function">Serverless function</Term> | a function | everything, including scaling to zero | each request and its run time | Lambda / Cloud Run functions |

Reading down the table, you manage less and control less. A VM lets you install any driver and keep a model in
memory for days. A function hides the machine entirely, which is wonderful until you need something the platform
does not offer. Kubernetes for models has its own track: see
[Kubernetes for models](#/learn/deploy/kubernetes/k8s-for-models).

### Why the billing shapes differ

Each option charges for something different, and that is what decides the cost:

- A **VM** charges per hour whether it is busy or idle. Its monthly cost is a staircase: flat until traffic needs
  another machine, then a step up. You also keep a minimum (usually two, in two zones, for availability).
- A **serverless function** charges per **GB-second**: memory × run time, summed over every request, plus a small
  charge per request. Its cost is a straight line through zero: no traffic, no bill; double the traffic, double the
  bill.

With AWS Lambda's x86 list prices in US East ($0.0000166667 per GB-second, $0.20 per million requests), a million
requests a month of 200 ms at 1 GB costs

$$
10^6 \times 0.2\,\text{s} \times 1\,\text{GB} \times \$0.0000166667 + 1 \times \$0.20 \approx \$3.53 .
$$

Two small always-on VMs at $0.10 an hour cost 2 × 730 × $0.10 = $146 for the same month (730 is the number of
hours cloud price pages use for a month). The function wins by a factor of 40.

### The break-even point

Now the natural next question: does the function always win? No, because a line through zero eventually crosses a
staircase. At 100 million requests a month the function costs about $353, while three VMs handle the peak for
$219. The **break-even volume** is where the two costs meet, around 40 million requests a month in this example.
Below it, pay per use; above it, keep machines busy. The lab finds it for any inputs.

The deeper reason is **utilization**: how busy the VMs are on average. At a million requests a month, two VMs are
busy well under 1% of the time, so you are paying for idle hours. At very high, steady traffic they are busy most of
the time, and per-hour pricing becomes the cheaper way to buy the same work.

### Limits that decide before price does

Cost only matters among options that can run the workload. Serverless functions have hard limits that often rule
them out for ML:

- **No GPUs** on Lambda, and a maximum of 10,240 MB of memory and 15 minutes per invocation. (Cloud Run *services*
  can attach NVIDIA L4 GPUs, a notable exception.)
- <Term id="cold-start">Cold starts</Term>: when no warm instance exists, the platform must start one, download your
  code and load your model before answering. For a 2 GB model this can take many seconds. Provisioned concurrency
  or minimum instances remove cold starts, but then you pay for idle capacity again.
- **Packages and model size**: deployment packages and images have size limits, and a large model loaded on every
  cold start is slow.

So a practical map for ML:

| Workload | Usually best | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Small preprocessing triggered by uploads | serverless function | spiky, short, CPU-only |
| A light CPU model API with uneven traffic | container service that scales to zero | no idle cost, no 15-minute limit |
| Nightly batch scoring | a batch job on VMs or a managed job | runs for an hour, then nothing |
| LLM or GPU inference with steady traffic | GPU VMs or Kubernetes, or a managed endpoint | needs GPUs and warm models |
| Training | GPU VMs or managed training jobs | long, GPU-heavy; covered next |

<Callout kind="simplification">
The model ignores free tiers, Lambda's ARM pricing (about 20% cheaper), load balancer and NAT costs, and request
duration changing with memory size (more memory also means more CPU, so functions often run faster). VM capacity
per instance is an input you should measure with a load test (see the
[Inference track's load testing lesson](#/learn/inference/ops/monitoring-load-testing)).
</Callout>

<Section kind="mistakes" title="Choosing compute by habit">

<CommonMistake
  title="An always-on GPU VM for a demo nobody uses at night"
  wrong={'# g5.xlarge running 24/7 for an internal demo used ~2 hours a day'}
  error="(no error) about $735 a month for roughly 60 hours of real use"
  cause="Per-hour billing charges idle hours exactly like busy ones."
  fix="Schedule it off outside working hours, use a service that scales to zero, or start it on demand."
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Loading the model inside the handler"
  wrong={'def handler(event, context):\n    model = joblib.load("/opt/model.pkl")   # every request\n    return model.predict(event["rows"])'}
  error="(no error) every request takes 3 s and the GB-second bill is many times higher than it should be"
  cause="The model is loaded on every invocation instead of once per warm instance."
  fix="Load it at module level, outside the handler, so warm invocations reuse it; keep the model small or move to a container service."
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Putting a long job in a function"
  wrong={'# Lambda function that retrains a model on new data'}
  error="Task timed out after 900.00 seconds"
  cause="Lambda invocations stop after 15 minutes, and training rarely fits."
  fix="Use a batch job, a container task, or a managed training job, triggered by the function if you like."
/>

<Section kind="practice" title="How teams pick compute">

- **Describe the workload first**: requests or jobs per month, duration, memory, GPU, latency target.
- **Rule out options by their limits** (GPU, time limit, memory, cold starts) before comparing prices.
- **Price both shapes** for your real traffic, including the minimum instances you need for availability.
- **Prefer scale-to-zero for spiky, low-volume work**; prefer busy machines for steady, heavy work.
- **Revisit as traffic grows**: the right answer at launch is often wrong a year later.

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

Cloud compute comes in shapes that trade control for convenience: VMs, container services, managed Kubernetes and
serverless functions. They also bill differently, and that difference decides cost. VMs charge per hour, busy or
idle, as a staircase of whole machines with a minimum for availability; functions charge per GB-second, a straight
line through zero.

So at low or spiky volume the line sits far below the staircase and serverless wins; as steady volume grows the line
crosses the staircase at the break-even point, and busy VMs win, because utilization is what you are really buying.
Before price, though, limits decide: no GPUs on Lambda, 15-minute invocations and cold starts rule functions out of
most model serving and all training.

Training needs GPUs, so the next lesson chooses among GPU instances.

</Section>

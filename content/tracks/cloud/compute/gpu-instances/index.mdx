<Section kind="intuition" title="The question is not 'how fast?' but 'does it fit?'">

When people pick a GPU, they usually ask which one is fastest. For most ML jobs that is the second question. The
first is simpler and stricter: **does the job fit in the GPU's memory?** A GPU can only work on what is in its own
memory. If the model and everything that goes with it do not fit, the job does not run slowly; it crashes with
`CUDA out of memory`.

So choosing a GPU instance is like choosing a moving van: first you measure how much you have to carry, then you
rule out every van that is too small, and only then do you compare the prices of the vans that are left. This
lesson gives you the measuring rule, the names of the vans on AWS and Google Cloud, and the surprisingly important
detail of whether you are even allowed to rent one.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="Measure, rule out, then compare">

**The big picture.** Model size (billions of parameters) × bytes per parameter for the job → GB of GPU memory needed
→ rule out instances with less → among the rest compare price per hour, and then price per *job* → check that your
account has quota for it.

### Step 1: memory needed

The rule of thumb is one multiplication. A parameter is one number in the model; a billion parameters stored at one
byte each take one GB. So

$$
\text{GB needed} \approx \text{billions of parameters} \times \text{bytes per parameter} \times \text{headroom}.
$$

The bytes per parameter depend on the **job**, not the model, and this is the part people get wrong:

| Job | Bytes per parameter | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Serve in 16-bit | 2 | each weight is a 16-bit number |
| Serve 4-bit quantized | 0.5 | each weight packed into 4 bits ([Deploy track](#/learn/deploy/quantization/llm-quantization)) |
| <Term id="fine-tuning">Fine-tune</Term> with LoRA (16-bit frozen base) | 2 | the base model is frozen; only small adapter matrices train |
| Fine-tune with QLoRA (4-bit frozen base) | 0.5 | as LoRA, with the frozen base quantized |
| Full fine-tune, mixed precision with Adam | 16 | weights 2 + gradients 2 + fp32 master copy 4 + Adam's two averages 4 + 4 |

The headroom (we use 1.2, i.e. 20% extra) covers what lives beside the weights: activations, the KV cache when
serving, the CUDA context and fragmentation. The [Inference track's memory lesson](#/learn/inference/memory/memory-estimation)
breaks that down properly; here a rough figure is enough to rule instances out.

Work it through for an 8B model: serving in 16-bit needs 8 × 2 × 1.2 = **19.2 GB**; full fine-tuning needs
8 × 16 × 1.2 = **153.6 GB**. Same model, eight times the memory, because training keeps gradients and optimizer
state for every parameter. That single table explains why LoRA and QLoRA are so popular: they make fine-tuning fit
on the hardware used for serving.

### Step 2: reading instance names

Cloud GPU instances bundle a number of GPUs with CPUs, RAM and network. The names encode what you get:

| Instance | GPUs | GPU memory | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| AWS `g4dn.xlarge` | 1 × T4 | 16 GB | small models, cheap inference |
| AWS `g6.xlarge` / Google `g2-standard-4` | 1 × L4 | 24 GB | 7B-8B inference, LoRA on small models |
| AWS `g5.xlarge` | 1 × A10G | 24 GB | inference and light fine-tuning |
| AWS `g6e.xlarge` | 1 × L40S | 48 GB | larger inference, LoRA on 8B-13B |
| AWS `p4d.24xlarge` | 8 × A100 40 GB | 320 GB | multi-GPU training |
| Google `a2-ultragpu-1g` | 1 × A100 80 GB | 80 GB | single-GPU training of mid-size models |
| AWS `p5.48xlarge` / Google `a3-highgpu-8g` | 8 × H100 | 640 GB | large training jobs |

On AWS the letter is the family (g for graphics-and-inference GPUs, p for the large training GPUs), the digit its
generation, and the size after the dot (`xlarge`, `12xlarge`, `48xlarge`) the share of the machine. On Google Cloud
the GPU type is fixed by the machine series (G2 has L4s, A2 has A100s, A3 has H100s).

When a job needs more memory than one GPU has, it must be **split across GPUs** with
<Term id="tensor-parallelism">tensor parallelism</Term> or sharded data parallelism (FSDP, DeepSpeed ZeRO). That
works best inside one multi-GPU instance, where the GPUs are linked by NVLink, much faster than the network between
separate machines.

### Step 3: price per hour, then price per job

Among the instances that fit, the cheapest per hour is the obvious choice for serving, where the machine runs all
month. For *training*, the bill is price per hour × hours, and faster GPUs finish sooner. An instance that costs
twice as much per hour but finishes in a third of the time is cheaper per job. The honest way to compare is to run
a short benchmark (a few hundred steps) on two candidates and extrapolate; the capstone uses exactly that number.

### Step 4: are you allowed to rent it?

New cloud accounts usually have a <Term id="gpu-quota">quota</Term> of zero, or close to it, for GPU instances. On
AWS the limit is counted in vCPUs per family group (a `p5.48xlarge` uses 192 of them); on Google Cloud it is
counted in GPUs per model per region. Increases are requests that a person reviews, which can take days. Even with
quota, the newest GPUs are sometimes simply unavailable in a zone; reservations and AWS Capacity Blocks for ML let
you book capacity ahead for a fixed window.

<Callout kind="simplification">
The 1.2 headroom is a planning figure. Training activations grow with batch size and sequence length and can exceed
it by a lot (activation checkpointing trades compute to shrink them). Prices in the lab are illustrative round
numbers, not quotes: GPU prices change often, differ by region, and dropped notably for some families in 2025.
Check the pricing pages before you budget.
</Callout>

<Section kind="practice" title="How teams pick GPU instances">

- **Write down the memory estimate** (params × bytes per job × headroom) before looking at instances.
- **Shrink the job first** if it does not fit: quantize for serving, LoRA or QLoRA for fine-tuning.
- **Keep multi-GPU jobs inside one instance** when you can; cross-machine training needs fast networking (EFA on
  AWS, GPUDirect on Google Cloud).
- **Benchmark, then extrapolate** cost per job for training; use the cheapest that fits for always-on serving.
- **Secure quota and capacity early**, in the region where the data is.

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

Choosing a GPU starts with memory, because a job that does not fit does not run. The estimate is one line:
billions of parameters × bytes per parameter for the job × headroom, and the job changes the bytes dramatically,
from 0.5 for 4-bit serving to 16 for full fine-tuning with Adam. That is why LoRA and QLoRA let fine-tuning fit
where only serving fitted before.

With the number in hand, you rule out every instance with less GPU memory, splitting across the GPUs of one
multi-GPU instance when no single GPU is enough. Among what is left, the cheapest per hour wins for serving, while
for training the right comparison is price × hours from a short benchmark. And none of it matters until your account
has quota for the instance in the region where your data lives.

GPUs are expensive by the hour. The next lesson cuts that price by more than half, at the cost of being interrupted.

</Section>

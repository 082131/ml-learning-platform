<Section kind="intuition" title="A taxi or a car of your own">

If you drive twice a month, owning a car is absurd: you pay for it every day, park it, insure it and service it, so
each trip costs a fortune. A taxi charges only for the trips. If you drive four hours every day, the sums flip: the
car's fixed costs are spread over so many trips that each one becomes cheap, and the taxi meter becomes the expensive
choice.

Language models on the cloud offer the same choice. A <Term id="model-api">model API</Term>, such as Amazon Bedrock
or Vertex AI, is the taxi: you send a request and pay for the tokens in and out, nothing when nobody calls. Running
an <Term id="open-weight-model">open-weight model</Term> on your own GPUs is the car: you pay for the GPUs every hour
of the month, plus the people who keep the service running, whether the requests come or not.

The previous lesson showed how a managed job removes idle hours from training. This lesson asks the same question
for serving: at what volume does owning the GPUs start to pay off, why that volume is usually much higher than
people expect, and what lies between the two extremes.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="Two cost shapes, and where they cross">

**The big picture.** An API's cost grows in proportion to traffic → self-hosting starts from a floor (at least one
GPU all month, plus the people) and then grows in steps sized for the busiest hour → so self-hosted GPUs sit mostly
idle → the two lines cross at a break-even volume → and the services in between (batch, provisioned throughput,
managed endpoints) move that crossing, as do data, latency and control requirements.

### Three ways to get an answer from a model on the cloud

| Option | You provide | You pay | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Hosted model API** | Requests | Per input and output token | Amazon Bedrock, Gemini and partner models on Vertex AI |
| **<Term id="managed-endpoint">Managed endpoint</Term>** | The model (weights + serving container) | Per instance-hour, or per use for serverless | SageMaker real-time and serverless endpoints, Vertex AI endpoints |
| **Your own servers** | Everything: GPUs, serving engine, scaling, monitoring | Per GPU-hour + engineering time | GPU VMs, or Kubernetes with vLLM ([Deploy track](#/learn/deploy/llm-serving/vllm-kserve)) |

Moving down the table, you gain control (any model, your own fine-tune, your own batching and quantization) and you
take on fixed costs and work. The cost comparison below sets the top row against the bottom one, because that is where
the difference is largest; a managed endpoint has the bottom row's cost shape with less of the work.

### The API: cost proportional to traffic

Per request you pay input tokens × the input price plus output tokens × the output price, quoted per million tokens.
The [AI Engineer track](#/learn/ai/ops/latency-cost-security) works through the per-request levers (prompt caching,
shorter outputs, smaller models); here we only need the monthly total:

$$
\text{API} = 30 \times \text{requests per day} \times \frac{t_{in}\,p_{in} + t_{out}\,p_{out}}{10^6}.
$$

With 1,000 input and 300 output tokens at illustrative prices of \$0.50 and \$1.50 per million, a request costs
\$0.00095. At 10,000 requests a day that is **\$285 a month**. Halve the traffic and the bill halves; stop it and the
bill is zero. That proportionality is the whole appeal.

### Self-hosting: a floor, then steps sized for the peak

On your own GPUs the question is not "how many tokens" but "how many GPUs must be running". Work it through in a
chain:

1. **Average load.** 10,000 requests × 300 output tokens ÷ 86,400 seconds = **34.7 output tokens per second** on
   average.
2. **Peak load.** Traffic is not flat: it rises during working hours and falls at night. The
   <Term id="peak-to-average-ratio">peak-to-average ratio</Term> (here 3) says the busiest hour needs about
   104 tokens per second, and users at the busiest hour must still be served.
3. **GPUs.** One GPU with a batching engine sustains, say, 400 output tokens per second for this model. 104 ÷ 400
   rounds up to **1 GPU**, and you can never run fewer than one.
4. **Cost.** 1 GPU × 730 hours × \$1 = \$730, plus 10 engineer-hours at \$100 for upgrades, monitoring and on-call:
   **\$1,730 a month**, six times the API.

Now look at how busy that GPU is: 34.7 ÷ 400 = **9%**. You pay for 100% of the hours and use 9% of them. Even at very
high traffic, where the fleet is large and the one-GPU floor no longer matters, a fleet sized for a peak three times
the average is busy at most a third of the time on average. Autoscaling down at night raises that share (the
[autoscaling lesson](#/learn/deploy/kubernetes/autoscaling-rollouts) shows how), but GPUs with large models start
slowly, so most teams keep a buffer. An API provider faces the same physics but pools thousands of customers whose
peaks fall at different times, and that pooling is what you buy with each token.

### The break-even: where the lines cross

Put the two together. The API line rises smoothly from zero. The self-hosted line starts at \$1,730 and climbs in
\$730 steps each time the peak needs another GPU. They cross when the API bill catches up with the step you are on.
With these numbers, a request costs \$0.0285 a month on the API (\$0.00095 × 30), and at 3 GPUs self-hosting costs
\$3,190, so the API stops winning near 3,190 ÷ 0.0285 ≈ **112,000 requests a day**.

The three inputs that move the crossing most are worth knowing by heart:

- **The people cost.** It is fixed and the API has none. Raise it from 10 to 40 hours a month and the break-even
  quadruples to about 457,000 a day. It is the most underestimated term in real plans.
- **The token price.** A larger model at \$10 per million output tokens costs the API \$1,050 a month at the default
  volume, and self-hosting wins from about 17,000 requests a day. Expensive models make owning GPUs attractive sooner.
- **Serving speed.** A faster engine (1,500 instead of 400 tokens per second) changes nothing at low volume, where
  you pay for the one-GPU floor anyway, but it delays the second GPU and pulls the crossing down to about 62,000 a day.

### Between the extremes

Hosted services offer more than one price shape, and each matches a traffic pattern:

- **<Term id="batch-inference">Batch inference</Term>** (Bedrock batch inference, Vertex AI batch prediction): you
  upload a file of requests and get results within hours. For supported models both providers list it at around half
  the on-demand price. Nightly jobs, evaluations and backfills belong here.
- **<Term id="provisioned-throughput">Provisioned throughput</Term>**: you buy capacity on a hosted model by the hour
  or month. It guarantees a rate and is sometimes required for custom (fine-tuned or imported) models, but like your
  own GPUs it bills during quiet hours too.
- **Managed endpoints**: your own model, served and scaled by the platform. SageMaker serverless and asynchronous
  endpoints can scale to zero for spiky or occasional traffic, at the cost of cold starts (the compute-options
  lesson's trade-off again).

### It is not only cost

The cheapest line on the chart is not always the right answer. Four other questions often decide:

1. **Data and networking.** Bedrock and Vertex AI process requests in the region you call, authenticate with the
   same IAM roles and service accounts as the rest of your account (no API keys to leak), and can be reached through
   private endpoints (AWS PrivateLink, Private Service Connect) so prompts never cross the public internet. Read the
   provider's data-use terms, but strict data rules do not by themselves force self-hosting.
2. **Limits.** Hosted models have quotas in requests and tokens per minute, per model and region. A launch that
   needs ten times the default quota must request it early, exactly like the GPU quotas in the GPU lesson.
3. **Latency and control.** Your own servers let you pick the model, the quantization and the batching, and keep a
   fine-tuned model; an API gives you the provider's choices and its shared capacity.
4. **Lock-in.** Bedrock's Converse API and OpenAI-compatible endpoints give many models one request shape, so
   switching models (or moving to self-hosting later) is a configuration change rather than a rewrite.

<Callout kind="simplification">
All prices in this lesson are illustrative, not quotes: token prices differ by model, region and provider, and GPU
prices by instance type. The self-hosting model assumes a fixed throughput per GPU, a fleet sized for the peak hour
with no autoscaling, and 730 hours a month; the API side ignores caching and batch discounts. Real throughput
depends on prompt lengths, batch sizes and the serving engine, so measure it with a load test (the
[Inference track](#/learn/inference/serving/apis-containers) shows how) before trusting a break-even figure.
</Callout>

<Section kind="practice" title="Choosing how to serve a model">

- **Start on an API** for a new feature: there is no fixed cost, so it is the cheapest way to learn the real traffic.
- **Log tokens per request and requests per hour** from day one. They are the inputs of the break-even, measured.
- **Price self-hosting as a whole month**: peak-sized fleet × 730 hours + the people, and a load-tested throughput.
- **Move steady overnight work to batch inference** before thinking about owning GPUs.
- **Keep the request shape portable** (Converse or an OpenAI-compatible endpoint) so changing model or host later is
  configuration.
- **Self-host when volume, data rules, a fine-tuned model or latency control clearly justify it**, and review the
  sums each quarter as prices fall.

<Section kind="summary">

A model API and your own GPUs have different cost shapes. The API grows in proportion to traffic and is zero when
nobody calls. Self-hosting starts from a floor, at least one GPU all month plus the people who run it, and then grows
in GPU-sized steps chosen for the busiest hour. Because the fleet is sized for the peak, it is busy on average only
about average ÷ peak of the time, which is why the break-even volume is usually much higher than a naive "price per
token" comparison suggests.

The crossing moves with three inputs above all: the people cost (fixed, and often decisive), the token price of the
model you would replace, and serving speed (which matters only once you need more than one GPU). Between the
extremes, batch inference trades latency for price, provisioned throughput and managed endpoints trade flexibility
for capacity, and data location, quotas, control and lock-in can outweigh the cost line.

</Section>

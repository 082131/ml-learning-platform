<Section kind="intuition" title="If two people disagree, which one taught the model?">

Every supervised model learns from people's decisions. When annotators label a comment "toxic" or "fine", they
apply a definition that lives in their heads unless someone writes it down. If two annotators hold different
definitions, the dataset holds both, and the model learns a blur of the two.

You cannot check labels against the truth for every item; if you could, you would not need annotators. What you can
check cheaply is whether independent annotators agree. Low agreement means the task is unclear, the annotators are
careless, or the task is genuinely ambiguous. All three must be fixed or accounted for before the labels are worth
training on.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="Guidelines, agreement and chance">

**Guidelines.** <Term id="annotation-guidelines">Annotation guidelines</Term> define each label with examples, and add
a rule for each edge case that came up. They are versioned: labels made under v1 and v2 can mean different things.

**Agreement.** <Term id="inter-annotator-agreement">Inter-annotator agreement</Term> is measured on items that two or
more people label independently. Write the counts in a 2 × 2 matrix: rows are annotator A's label, columns are B's.
The diagonal holds agreements. Observed agreement is

$$
p_o = \frac{\text{both toxic} + \text{both fine}}{n}
$$

**Chance agreement.** If A calls a share $p_A$ of comments toxic and B a share $p_B$, two people labeling at random at
those rates would still agree on

$$
p_e = p_A\,p_B + (1 - p_A)(1 - p_B)
$$

When toxic comments are rare, both $p_A$ and $p_B$ are small and $p_e$ is close to 1: agreeing on "fine" is nearly
free.

**Cohen's kappa** measures agreement beyond chance, as a share of the improvement that was possible:

$$
\kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e}
$$

<Term id="cohens-kappa">Cohen's kappa</Term> is 1 for perfect agreement, 0 for chance level and negative for worse
than chance. A common reading (Landis and Koch, 1977) calls 0.2 to 0.4 fair, 0.4 to 0.6 moderate, 0.6 to 0.8
substantial and above 0.8 almost perfect. These bands are a convention; what is acceptable depends on the task.

**Worked example** (the default setting below): A says toxic on 11 of 20 comments, B on 9; they agree on 12.
$p_o = 12/20 = 0.60$, $p_e = 0.55 \cdot 0.45 + 0.45 \cdot 0.55 = 0.495$, so
$\kappa = (0.60 - 0.495)/(1 - 0.495) = 0.105/0.505 = 0.21$.

**Quality controls in a labeling job.** <Term id="gold-standard">Gold questions</Term> with known answers are mixed into
the work to measure each annotator's accuracy. Each item is labeled by several people, unanimous items take the
shared label, and split items go to <Term id="adjudication">adjudication</Term> by an expert. Managed services such as
SageMaker Ground Truth and tools such as Label Studio implement these steps.

</Section>

<Section kind="mistakes" title="Agreement numbers that mislead">

<CommonMistake
  title="Reporting raw percent agreement on imbalanced labels"
  wrong={"agreement = sum(a == b for a, b in zip(A, B)) / len(A)   # 0.85, looks great"}
  error="(no error: an annotator who always answers the majority class scores the same 0.85)"
  cause="When one label dominates, agreeing on it is nearly automatic, so raw agreement mostly measures the class balance."
  fix={"from sklearn.metrics import cohen_kappa_score\nkappa = cohen_kappa_score(A, B)   # 0.00 for the constant annotator"}
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Measuring agreement only on a balanced pilot sample"
  wrong={"pilot = sample_balanced(comments, per_class=10)   # kappa 0.90, ship it"}
  error="(no error: on the real stream, where toxic is rare, agreement on the toxic class is much worse than the pilot suggests)"
  cause="Agreement depends on the label distribution. A balanced pilot hides how annotators behave on the distribution they will actually label."
  fix="measure on a random sample of the real stream as well, and report per-class agreement next to kappa"
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Resolving every split by majority vote"
  wrong={"final = [Counter(votes).most_common(1)[0][0] for votes in item_votes]"}
  error="(no error: a careless annotator who clicks one label decides every split where the other two disagree)"
  cause="A vote counts every annotator equally, whatever their accuracy."
  fix="weight or filter annotators by gold accuracy, and send splits to adjudication instead of voting them away"
/>

</Section>

<Section kind="practice" title="Running a labeling project">

- **Pilot first.** Label 100 to 200 items with two or three annotators, compute kappa, read every disagreement, and
  update the guidelines. Repeat until agreement is stable.
- **Version the guidelines** and store the version with each label, along with the annotator ID and timestamp.
- **Keep overlap in production.** Have a share of items (often 5 to 10%) labeled twice for the whole job, so agreement
  is monitored rather than measured once.
- **Mix in gold questions** and track each annotator's accuracy over time; retrain or remove people who fall below
  the bar.
- **Adjudicate and feed back.** Every adjudicated case is a candidate example for the next guideline version.
- **Accept real ambiguity.** If experts also disagree on a kind of item, record it (a soft label, or an "unsure"
  class) instead of forcing a label.

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

- Guidelines define what labels mean; version them and grow them from disagreements.
- Observed agreement $p_o$ is inflated when one class dominates. Cohen's kappa subtracts chance agreement $p_e$.
- In the experiment, three edge-case rules raised kappa from 0.21 to 0.90; a lazy annotator matched a careful one on
  raw agreement (0.85) but scored kappa 0.
- Gold questions measure accuracy, overlap measures consistency, and adjudication resolves splits.

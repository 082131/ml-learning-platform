<Section kind="intuition" title="Write the rules down, then let people handle the rest">

A support team wants a model that spots refund requests among thousands of tickets a day. Labeling 50,000 old
tickets by hand would take weeks. But the team already knows a lot: tickets that say "refund" or "charged twice" are
usually refund requests, thank-you notes are not, how-to questions rarely are.

Weak supervision turns that knowledge into labels: write each hunch as a small function, let the functions vote, and
accept that the result is noisy. Active learning handles the other end: when a person must label, let the model pick
the tickets where a human answer teaches it the most. Together they make expert time go much further than labeling
a random pile.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="Labeling functions, coverage and uncertainty">

**Labeling functions.** In <Term id="weak-supervision">weak supervision</Term> each source of labels is a
<Term id="labeling-function">labeling function</Term>: a function that returns a label, or abstains when its rule does
not apply. Applying $m$ functions to $n$ examples gives an $n \times m$ **label matrix** of votes.

**Four numbers per function**, measured against a small gold set:

- <Term id="label-coverage">Coverage</Term>: the share of examples where it votes.
- Overlap: the share where it votes together with another function.
- Conflict: the share where another function votes differently.
- Accuracy: of its votes on gold examples, the share that are right.

**Combining votes.** The simplest combiner is a majority vote over the non-abstaining functions; a tie or no vote
leaves the example unlabeled. A **label model** (as in Snorkel) goes further: it estimates each function's accuracy
from the pattern of agreements and conflicts and outputs a probability per label. Either way, the weak labels then
train an ordinary classifier, which can generalize to examples no rule covers.

**Active learning.** <Term id="active-learning">Active learning</Term> is a loop: train on the labels you have, score
the unlabeled pool, ask a person to label the most useful examples, retrain. The most common strategy is
<Term id="uncertainty-sampling">uncertainty sampling</Term>: pick the examples whose predicted probability is closest
to 0.5, where the model is least sure. In one dimension this is binary search for the decision boundary: each label
halves the interval where the boundary can be.

**Other ways to save labeling time.** Model-assisted pre-labeling (annotators correct a model's guess instead of
starting from scratch), and managed "automated labeling" services that label confident items with a model and send
the rest to people. They all trade some label quality for speed, so all of them need a gold set to measure the cost.

</Section>

- **Add how_question.** 11 tickets at 0.91. It labels two how-to questions correctly, but it also votes "no" on "How

- **Add cancel.** 12 tickets, but accuracy drops to 0.83: its only new ticket is "Cancel my subscription please",
  which it gets wrong. More coverage is not always better.

**Uncertainty sampling against random sampling.** An unknown threshold separates positives from negatives on a score
between 0 and 1. With a budget of 8 labels, which items should a person label? The experiment repeats on 20 random
pools of 1,000 items:

<PythonRunner example={activeRunner} title="active.py" />

Random labels land anywhere, so after 8 of them the threshold is known to about ±0.06. Uncertainty sampling always asks
about the item closest to its current guess, halving the interval each time: about 1,000 items fit in
$2^{10}$, so 8 halvings narrow it to a handful of items.

<CommonMistake

  wrong={"lfs = sorted(lfs, key=coverage, reverse=True)[:5]   # the five broadest rules"}
  error="(no error: the weak labels cover most tickets and are wrong on many of them)"
  cause="Broad rules fire often because they are vague. Coverage says how often a function speaks, not whether it is right."
  fix="report coverage and accuracy on a gold set together; keep precise narrow rules, fix or drop broad inaccurate ones"

<Section kind="practice" title="Programmatic labeling in production">

- **Start from errors.** Read 100 unlabeled examples, write functions for patterns you see, then measure them on a
  gold set of a few hundred.
- **Version the functions** with the dataset: the weak labels are a build product of code plus data, and must be
  reproducible.
- **Keep a human queue.** Unlabeled and conflicting examples go to annotators, ordered by the model's uncertainty.
- **Watch the boundary.** Uncertainty sampling concentrates labels on hard cases; check annotator agreement on them,
  and mix in random samples so the labeled set still represents the data.
- **Retire rules.** When the trained classifier beats a rule on the gold set, the rule has done its job.

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

- Labeling functions encode heuristics; each votes or abstains, and the votes form a label matrix.
- Judge every function on coverage and accuracy together; a broad rule can add coverage and lower accuracy, as cancel
  did.
- A label model weighs functions by estimated accuracy; a classifier trained on the weak labels generalizes past the
  rules.
- Active learning spends human labels where the model is least sure; in active.py it found the boundary about 50
  times more precisely than random sampling with the same 8 labels.

<Section kind="intuition" title="Removing names is not anonymizing">

Training data is often about people: their purchases, messages, health, location. Teams usually start by deleting
names and emails and call the result anonymous. It rarely is. Age, postcode and sex together single out most people,
and anyone who knows those three facts about a neighbour can find their row and read the rest.

ML data operations has to answer three questions for every dataset: may we use this data for this purpose, how do we
reduce what it reveals about individuals, and can we delete a person's data everywhere it was copied, including old
versions. This lesson measures re-identification risk on a small table, shows how to pseudonymize identifiers
correctly, and connects deletion to the dataset versioning of the previous lessons.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="Identifiers, pseudonyms, k-anonymity and governance">

**Three kinds of fields.**

- Direct identifiers name a person: name, email, phone, account number. Remove them or replace them with tokens.
- <Term id="quasi-identifier">Quasi-identifiers</Term> do not name anyone alone but do in combination: age, postcode,
  sex, job title, signup date.
- Sensitive attributes are what must not leak: a diagnosis, a salary, a religion.

**Pseudonymization.** <Term id="pseudonymization">Pseudonymization</Term> replaces an identifier with a token that can
only be linked back with separately kept information. A keyed hash, $\text{HMAC}_K(\text{email})$, gives the same token
for the same person in every table (so joins still work) and cannot be computed without the key $K$. A plain hash can
be computed by anyone, so a list of candidate emails reveals who is in the data. Pseudonymized data is still personal
data under the GDPR, because the key holder can re-identify it.

**k-anonymity.** Group the rows by their (generalized) quasi-identifiers. A table is
<Term id="k-anonymity">k-anonymous</Term> if every group has at least $k$ rows: someone who knows a person's
quasi-identifiers can narrow them down only to $k$ rows. You raise $k$ by generalizing (age 58 → 50–59, postcode
10407 → 104\*\*) or suppressing fields.

**l-diversity.** If every row in a group shares one sensitive value, the group reveals it whatever $k$ is.
<Term id="l-diversity">l-diversity</Term> requires at least $l$ distinct sensitive values in every group.

Both are measures on a released table, not guarantees against every attack: an attacker with extra knowledge can
still learn something. Stronger, mathematical guarantees come from differential privacy, which adds calibrated noise
to statistics or training (for example DP-SGD).

**Governance around the data.** Beyond the table itself:

- **Purpose and basis:** each dataset records why it may be used (consent, contract, legitimate interest) and for what.
- **Documentation:** a <Term id="datasheet-for-datasets">datasheet</Term> (or data card) records collection, labeling,
  personal data, known gaps, intended and unsuitable uses, and retention.
- **Erasure:** a deletion request must reach the main table, old snapshots (until
  <Term id="snapshot-retention">retention</Term> expires them), branches, exports and backups, and a policy must say what
  happens to models trained on the data.
- **Access:** the people and jobs that can read raw personal data should be few, logged and separate from those that
  use pseudonymized training sets.

</Section>

The attacker knows that Lena is 58, lives in postcode 10407 and is female. Things to try:

- **Default** (exact values): 16 groups of one. k = 1, so Lena's row, and her diagnosis, are found directly.
- **10-year bands, sex kept, full postcode:** still 16 groups of one. Generalizing one field is rarely enough.
- **10-year bands and 3 postcode digits:** k = 2, but Lena's group of two both have diabetes.
- **Now remove sex:** k = 4, the table is 4-anonymous, and the diagnosis is still revealed: all four people in their
  fifties in district 104 have diabetes (l = 1).
- **20-year bands, postcode removed, sex removed:** k = 4 and l = 3. Lena hides among 8 rows with 4 diagnoses, but the
  table has lost almost everything a model could use about location.

<Section kind="mistakes" title="Data that was never as private as it looked">

<CommonMistake
  title="Hashing identifiers without a key"
  wrong={"df[\"user\"] = df[\"email\"].map(lambda e: hashlib.sha256(e.encode()).hexdigest())"}
  error="(no error: anyone with a list of emails can find those users in the export)"
  cause="A plain hash is a public function; it hides nothing from someone who can guess the input."
  fix={"df[\"user\"] = df[\"email\"].map(lambda e: hmac.new(KEY, e.encode(), hashlib.sha256).hexdigest())"}
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Calling a table anonymous after dropping names"
  wrong={"release = patients.drop(columns=[\"name\", \"email\"])   # age, postcode, sex, diagnosis remain"}
  error="(no error: most rows are unique on age, postcode and sex, so they can be linked to a voter list or a social profile)"
  cause="Quasi-identifiers in combination identify people."
  fix="measure k and l on the quasi-identifiers, generalize or suppress until both meet your policy, or release only aggregates"
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Deleting a person from the latest version only"
  wrong={"DELETE FROM training_examples WHERE user_token = :token;   -- done?"}
  error="(no error: time travel, branches, exports and backups still contain the rows)"
  cause="Versioned data keeps the past on purpose."
  fix="track every copy, expire or rewrite old snapshots on a deadline, and log that the request was completed"
/>

<Section kind="practice" title="Governance in a data pipeline">

- **Classify fields at ingestion** (direct identifier, quasi-identifier, sensitive) and store the classification with
  the schema.
- **Pseudonymize early** with a keyed hash, so most pipelines never see raw identifiers.
- **Measure before release:** compute k and l for any table leaving the team; scrub personal data from free text before
  it reaches annotators.
- **Write the datasheet with the first version** and update it with every major change (new source, new guideline,
  new known gap).
- **Make erasure a pipeline:** one request ID, every copy listed, deadlines, and a completion log that an auditor can
  read.
- **Minimize:** keep only the fields and history the model needs; data you never stored cannot leak.

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

- Quasi-identifiers in combination identify people; dropping names is not anonymization.
- k-anonymity bounds how far an attacker can narrow down a row; l-diversity stops a group from revealing its sensitive
  value. In the experiment a 4-anonymous table still revealed Lena's diagnosis.
- Pseudonymize with a keyed hash; a plain hash is reversible by guessing.
- Purpose, datasheets, retention and erasure across versions are part of the data pipeline.

<Section kind="intuition" title="A library index, and a test you grade yourself">

A library doesn't find a book by walking past every shelf. It sends you to the right section, and you look there. You
might miss a book shelved in the wrong section, but you save hours. An
<Term id="ann-index">approximate nearest-neighbor index</Term> does the same for embeddings: it searches the few
regions most likely to hold the nearest vectors and accepts that it will occasionally miss one.

That raises a question the [RAG lesson](#/learn/ai/rag/rag-pipeline) left open: how do you know whether retrieval is
good enough, and whether a change made it better? You write a test. Collect questions, note which passage answers each,
and count how often that passage comes back. Every retrieval decision in this lesson, from index settings to rerankers,
is judged with that count.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="Indexes, metadata and retrieval metrics">

**Exact search** compares the query with every stored vector: $n$ dot products of $d$ numbers each. A million
384-dimensional vectors means $3.84 \times 10^8$ multiply-adds per query, too slow for many applications.

**IVF.** An <Term id="ivf-index">IVF index</Term> runs k-means once to split the vectors into `nlist` clusters. A
query first compares itself with the `nlist` cluster centers, then scans only the vectors in the `nprobe` closest
clusters. With 32 clusters and `nprobe = 4`, it scans about 4/32 of the vectors. Neighbors that sit just across a
cluster boundary are missed; raising `nprobe` finds them and costs time.

**HNSW.** An <Term id="hnsw">HNSW</Term> index links each vector to a few near neighbors, in layers that get sparser
toward the top. A search starts at the top, walks greedily toward the query, and drops down a layer whenever it can't
get closer, keeping `ef_search` candidates. It reaches high recall with few comparisons, uses extra memory for the links
and needs no training. It is the default index in many <Term id="vector-database">vector databases</Term>.

**What to store with each vector:** the chunk text, the document it came from, any field you will filter on, and the
name and version of the embedding model. A <Term id="metadata-filter">metadata filter</Term> restricts a search to
what a user may see or what the question is about.

**Measuring retrieval.** For each evaluation question, find the rank of the passage that answers it.

- <Term id="recall-at-k">Recall@k</Term> is the share of questions whose answer is in the top $k$. Only the top $k$
  reach the model, so this is the number that matters for RAG.
- <Term id="mean-reciprocal-rank">MRR</Term> averages $1/\text{rank}$, counting a miss as 0. It rewards putting the
  answer first.

Ranks 1, 2 and a miss give recall@1 $= 1/3$, recall@3 $= 2/3$ and MRR $= (1 + 1/2 + 0)/3 = 0.5$.

The same word has a second use: for an ANN index, recall@k is the share of the exact top $k$ that the index also
returned. The first experiment uses that meaning, the second the RAG meaning.

<Callout kind="simplification">
  The index experiment uses 3,000 synthetic unit vectors in 16 dimensions, grouped around 40 random topic directions,
  and 60 queries near stored vectors, all generated from a fixed seed. Real embeddings have hundreds of dimensions and
  millions of rows, where the speed-up matters far more. The evaluation set has six Lumen questions: enough to show the
  method, far too few to trust. Real evaluation sets start at 50 to 100 questions.
</Callout>

</Section>

- Raise the keyword weight to 0.5. Recall@1 becomes 1.00: <Term id="hybrid-search">hybrid search</Term> fixed the

- Back at weight 0, switch to 20-word chunks. Recall@1 drops to 0.67 and the offline-conflict answer falls to rank 4,
  outside a top 3: small chunks cut the explanation apart.
- In the index experiment, keep `nlist = 32` and move `nprobe` from 1 to 4: recall@10 climbs from 0.73 to 0.96 while the

- Compare `nlist = 64` with `nprobe = 4` (0.90 recall, 6.3% scanned) and `nlist = 16` with `nprobe = 4` (0.96, 25.7%).
  More, smaller clusters scan less per probe but need more probes for the same recall.

<Section kind="mistakes" title="Mixing models and trusting a small sample">

<CommonMistake
  title="Querying old vectors with a new embedding model"
  wrong={'# documents were embedded with model A last year\nquery_vec = model_b.encode(question)\nresults = db.search(query_vec, k=5)'}
  error="(no error: results look random, and retrieval quality collapses)"
  cause="Each embedding model has its own vector space. The cosine between a model-B query and model-A documents carries no meaning."
  fix={'results = db.search(query_vec, k=5, filter={"model": "model-b"})\n# and re-embed all documents with model B before switching over'}

<Section kind="practice" title="Building a retrieval evaluation set">

1. Collect real questions: search logs, support tickets, or questions written by people who know the content. Avoid
   only writing questions while looking at the passage; they end up sharing its exact words.
2. For each, record the passage (or document) that answers it, and mark questions with no answer.
3. Report recall@k at the k you actually use, and MRR. Break results down by question type (codes, how-to, policy).
4. Re-run it for every change to chunking, the embedding model, the index settings, hybrid weights or the reranker, and
   record latency next to quality.

A reranker or hybrid search earns its place when it lifts recall@k at your k by more than the noise of your set, at a
latency your users accept. Then measure the end result, the answers, in the [evaluation
lesson](#/learn/ai/reliability/evals-guardrails).

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

- Exact search compares every vector; ANN indexes (IVF, HNSW) compare a fraction and miss a few neighbors.
- IVF's `nprobe` and HNSW's `ef_search` move along the recall-speed trade-off at query time.
- Store each vector with its text, metadata and embedding model; never mix vectors from different models.
- Measure retrieval with recall@k and MRR on questions with known answer passages.
- Keep a reranker or hybrid search only when it beats the noise of your evaluation set at an acceptable latency.

</Section>

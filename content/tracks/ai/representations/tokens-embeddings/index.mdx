<Section kind="intuition" title="Text has to become numbers">

A neural network can only do arithmetic. Before a language model can read "Tokenizers split unbelievably long
words into pieces.", two things happen:

1. A <Term id="tokenizer">tokenizer</Term> cuts the text into <Term id="token">tokens</Term>, small pieces from a
   fixed list called the vocabulary, and replaces each piece with its number, the <Term id="token-id">token ID</Term>.
2. The model turns those IDs into vectors (lists of numbers) and computes with them. An embedding model goes one
   step further and squeezes a whole text into one vector, a <Term id="sentence-embedding">sentence embedding</Term>,
   built so that texts about similar things get vectors that point in similar directions.

Think of a library. Tokenizing is spelling the request out in the library's alphabet; embedding is giving every book
a position on a huge map, so that books on the same subject sit near each other. A search then means "find the books
nearest to where my question lands", and <Term id="cosine-similarity">cosine similarity</Term> is the ruler.

Everything in this lesson runs on a real, small, open model, `sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2`. The tokenizer
below runs in your browser with the model's own vocabulary. The model itself is too big to load here, so its vectors
for 27 texts were computed ahead of time and bundled with the lesson; the similarities between them are computed live
as you click.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="Subword tokens, IDs and one vector per text">

**Why subwords.** A vocabulary of whole words can't cover every name, typo or new word, and a vocabulary of single
characters makes texts very long. Subword tokenizers sit in between. all-MiniLM-L6-v2 uses
<Term id="wordpiece">WordPiece</Term>, the tokenizer of BERT, with 30,522 entries. It works in three steps:

1. **Normalize**: lowercase, remove accents ("Café" → "cafe"), and put spaces around Chinese characters.
2. **Pre-tokenize**: split on whitespace and make every punctuation character its own word, so "E-402:" becomes
   `e`, `-`, `402`, `:`.
3. **WordPiece**: for each word, take the longest beginning that is in the vocabulary, then repeat on the rest. Pieces
   after the first get a `##` prefix. "unbelievably" is not in the vocabulary, but `un` is, then `##bel`, `##ie`,
   `##va` and `##bly`. If no piece fits, the whole word becomes `[UNK]` (unknown), which happens to emoji such as 🙂.

Finally the tokenizer adds `[CLS]` (ID 101) at the start and `[SEP]` (ID 102) at the end, markers the model was
trained with. The ID of a token is simply its line number in the vocabulary file, counting from 0.

**From IDs to one vector.** The model looks up a learned vector for each ID, runs the sequence through 6 transformer
layers, and outputs one 384-number vector per token: shape $(\text{tokens}, 384)$. To get one vector for the whole
text, sentence-transformers averages the token vectors (mean pooling) and scales the result to length 1:

$$
e = \frac{m}{\lVert m \rVert}, \qquad m = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} h_t, \qquad e \in \mathbb{R}^{384}
$$

where $h_t$ is the vector of token $t$ and $T$ the number of tokens. Long or short, every text ends up as 384 numbers.

**Comparing vectors.** The cosine similarity of two vectors $a$ and $b$ is

$$
\cos\theta = \frac{a \cdot b}{\lVert a \rVert \, \lVert b \rVert} = \frac{\sum_i a_i b_i}{\sqrt{\sum_i a_i^2}\,\sqrt{\sum_i b_i^2}}
$$

It is 1 when they point the same way, 0 when they are perpendicular and −1 when they are opposite. Because these
embeddings have length 1, the denominator is 1 and the cosine is just the dot product. Two real values from the
bundled table: "A cat was sitting on the mat." and "The kitten slept on the rug all afternoon." share almost no words
but score 0.574, while "The movie was good." and "The movie was not good." score 0.80, even though they mean opposite
things.

<Callout kind="simplification">
  The vectors here are stored as 16-bit floats to keep the download small (about 30 KB for 27 texts). Compared with
  the full 32-bit vectors, that changes no cosine similarity in this lesson by more than 0.0001 (measured by the
  generation script). Production vector stores often compress further, with similar small trade-offs.
</Callout>

<Section kind="mistakes" title="Mixing models, and silent truncation">

<CommonMistake
  title="Comparing embeddings from two different models"
  wrong={'a = small_model.encode("refund policy")   # shape (384,)\nb = large_model.encode("refund policy")   # shape (768,)\nprint(a @ b)'}
  error="ValueError: matmul: Input operand 1 has a mismatch in its core dimension 0, with gufunc signature (n?,k),(k,m?)->(n?,m?) (size 768 is different from 384)"
  cause="Each model has its own vector space. Here the sizes differ, so NumPy refuses. Worse: two models with the same size give a number without any error, but the number means nothing, because dimension 17 of one model has no relation to dimension 17 of the other."
  fix={'# Embed the documents and every query with the same model, and store the model name with the vectors.\nb = small_model.encode("refund policy")'}
/>

<Callout kind="warning">
  Upgrading the embedding model means re-embedding every stored document. Keep the model name and revision next to
  your vectors, as the bundled table for this lesson does.
</Callout>

<CommonMistake
  title="Embedding a whole document at once"
  wrong={'vector = model.encode(open("handbook.txt").read())   # 20 pages'}
  error="(no error: the text after the first 256 word pieces is silently ignored)"
  cause="all-MiniLM-L6-v2 truncates input to 256 word pieces, as its model card states. A 20-page handbook is embedded from its first page or so, and questions about the rest never match."
  fix={'# Split long documents into chunks, embed each chunk, and search the chunks.\nvectors = model.encode(chunks)'}
/>

<Section kind="practice" title="Where you will meet tokens and embeddings">

| Task                                   | What you use                       | Why it matters                                                        |
| -------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Estimating API cost and context limits | Token counts                       | Prices and limits are per token, and each model counts differently    |
| Semantic search and RAG                | Embeddings + cosine similarity     | Finds text by meaning, not exact words                                |
| Deduplicating support tickets          | Embeddings + a similarity cutoff   | Near-duplicates score close to 1                                      |
| Exact codes, IDs and names             | Keyword search alongside embeddings | "E-402" and "B-402" look alike to an embedding model                  |
| Checking a model on your data          | Pairs you know the answer for      | Negation and rare terms are where small embedding models go wrong     |

A token count from this tokenizer is not the count a large language model will charge for: GPT-style and Claude
models use their own tokenizers with different vocabularies. Always count with the tokenizer of the model you call.

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

- A tokenizer turns text into tokens from a fixed vocabulary and each token into an ID. WordPiece splits unknown
  words into the longest known pieces, marking continuations with `##`.
- A model only understands its own tokenizer's IDs, and token counts differ between models.
- A sentence embedding is one fixed-length vector per text (384 numbers for all-MiniLM-L6-v2), made by averaging the
  token vectors and scaling to length 1.
- Cosine similarity compares directions; for unit vectors it is the dot product. Ranking by it finds text by topic and
  wording, but it can miss negation and exact codes.
- Compare only vectors from the same model, and split long texts before embedding them.

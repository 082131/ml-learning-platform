<Section kind="intuition" title="Let the computer do the boring part">

Every engineer has chores: download the export, rename the files, rerun the notebook, paste the chart into the
slides, check that last night's training run finished. Each one takes a few minutes. Together they take hours every
month, and they are exactly the kind of work people do badly when tired: a skipped step, the wrong file, a check
forgotten on a Friday.

Automation means writing down the procedure precisely enough that a computer can do it, then letting it. Done well,
it gives you the time back and does the job the same way every time. Done badly, it breaks quietly, costs more to
maintain than it saves, or does the wrong thing very fast. This lesson is about telling the two apart before you
write any code.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="Toil, payback and the automation ladder">

**Toil.** Google's Site Reliability Engineering book calls this kind of work <Term id="toil">toil</Term>: manual,
repetitive, automatable, without lasting value, and growing with the system. Two tests separate toil from real work:

- **Does it need judgment?** Choosing which model ships, deciding how to label an ambiguous ticket, or reading an
  incident is not toil. Automate the steps around a decision (gather the evidence, prepare the report), not the
  decision.
- **Does it repeat on a rule?** "Every weekday at 7, copy yesterday's export" is a rule. "Whenever it seems useful"
  is not, yet.

**Payback.** An automation costs time to build and time to maintain, and saves time on every run. The monthly
saving is

$$
\text{saved per month} = \frac{(\text{manual minutes} - \text{minutes still spent}) \times \text{runs per week} \times \tfrac{52}{12}}{60}\ \text{hours.}
$$

Subtract the maintenance hours per month to get the net. The <Term id="break-even-point">break-even point</Term> is
the month in which the cumulative net reaches the build cost. Two inputs are routinely forgotten: the minutes a
person *still* spends per run (reading the report, handling the odd failure), and maintenance (APIs change, paths
move, credentials expire).

**The ladder.** Automation is rarely all or nothing. Most good automations climb these rungs one at a time:

| Rung | What exists | You can now |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. <Term id="runbook">Runbook</Term> | Written steps | hand the task to anyone |
| 2. Script | Code that does the steps when someone runs it | do it in seconds, the same way every time |
| 3. Scheduled job | The script runs on a timer, and alerts when it fails | stop remembering to do it |
| 4. Pipeline | Steps with dependencies, retries, checks and gates | trust it with important work |
| 5. Self-service | Others trigger it safely, with guardrails | stop being the bottleneck |

**Properties every automation needs.** Whatever the rung, an automation should be
<Term id="idempotent">idempotent</Term> (running it twice is the same as running it once, so retries are safe),
**observable** (it logs what it did and says loudly when it fails), and **safe to stop** halfway (a crash does not
leave half-written output that looks complete). The scheduling and pipeline chapters build these in.

</Section>

<Section kind="mistakes" title="Automation that costs more than it saves">

<CommonMistake
  title="Automating a process nobody wrote down"
  wrong={'# "I know the steps, I\'ll just script them"\nsubprocess.run(["bash", "-c", "cp exports/* data/ && python clean.py && ..."])'}
  error="(two weeks later) nobody, including the author, knows why step 3 exists or what to do when it fails"
  cause="The script encodes one person's memory of the procedure, including its accidents."
  fix="Write the runbook first: steps, inputs, what 'done' looks like, what to do on failure. Then script the runbook, one step at a time."
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Counting the savings, forgetting the upkeep"
  wrong={'saved = minutes_per_run * runs_per_month / 60   # "6.5 hours a month!"'}
  error="(no error) the scraper breaks whenever the site changes; fixing it takes 8 hours a month"
  cause="Maintenance and the minutes a person still spends per run were left out of the estimate."
  fix="Estimate maintenance honestly, and prefer stable interfaces (official APIs, exports) over brittle ones (scraping, clicking through UIs)."
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Automating the judgment call"
  wrong={'if new_auc > old_auc:\n    deploy(new_model)   # any improvement ships, automatically'}
  error="(no error) a model that is 0.001 better on average and much worse for one customer segment ships at 3 a.m."
  cause="'Should this ship?' is a decision with trade-offs, not a rule."
  fix="Automate the evidence and the gates (see the model delivery lesson) and keep a person's approval for production."
/>

<Section kind="practice" title="How teams decide">

- **Log before you automate.** A week of real chores, with minutes and frequency, beats intuition.
- **Climb the ladder.** Runbook, then script, then schedule. Each rung is useful on its own and makes the next one
  cheaper.
- **Budget for upkeep.** Count maintenance as part of the cost, and retire automations that lose time.
- **Make it boringly safe:** idempotent, logged, alerting on failure, and with a dry-run mode for anything that
  moves, deletes or sends.
- **Keep decisions human** until the rule behind them is written down and agreed.

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

- Automate toil: repetitive, rule-based, judgment-free work. Automate the evidence around decisions, not the decisions.
- Payback = build cost against (saved − maintenance) per month. Forgetting upkeep is the classic error.
- Climb the ladder: runbook, script, scheduled job, pipeline, self-service.
- Every automation should be idempotent, observable and safe to stop.

<Section kind="intuition" title="Three tools for three different problems">

When a model's answers are not good enough, there are three levers you can pull, and they fix different things.
Picking the wrong one is the most common and most expensive mistake in this whole field, so this lesson comes
before any training code.

Think of the model as a capable new colleague:

- **Prompting** is telling them clearly what you want, with a few examples. It costs minutes and you can change it
  any time. It works when the colleague already *can* do the job and just needs to know your expectations.
- **Retrieval** (<Term id="retrieval-augmented-generation">RAG</Term>) is handing them the right documents before
  they answer. It works when they *could* answer but lack the facts, especially facts that change.
- **Fine-tuning** is training them until a behaviour becomes habit. It works when you need the same behaviour every
  time (a format, a tone, a narrow skill) and explaining it in every request is too long, too costly or not reliable
  enough.

The chain to remember: first find out *what kind of problem you have* (missing facts or wrong behaviour), then
pick the tool that changes that thing, and only then think about cost.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="What each tool can and cannot change">

**What fine-tuning changes.** The [previous lesson](#/learn/finetune/foundations/how-llms-learn) showed that
fine-tuning runs the same next-token training on your own examples. So the model gets better at producing text
*that looks like your examples*. That makes it strong at:

- **Format and style**: always JSON with these fields, always three sentences, always your brand's tone.
- **Narrow skills**: a classification scheme with 40 labels, your company's internal jargon, a domain language the
  base model rarely saw.
- **Cost and speed**: a small fine-tuned model can often match a large model that needs a long prompt, and every
  token you no longer send is money and latency saved.

**What fine-tuning is weak at: new facts.** This surprises people. If the training examples contain facts the model
did not already know, the model learns them slowly and unreliably. Worse, a study by Gekhman et al. (2024) found that
the model learns the *confident answering style* of those examples faster than the facts themselves, so it starts
answering confidently even when it does not know: hallucinations go up. Facts the model already absorbed in
pretraining are its <Term id="parametric-knowledge">parametric knowledge</Term>; adding to that store through a small
fine-tune is the job it does worst.

**Why retrieval handles facts better.** With retrieval, the facts are in the prompt, right in front of the model, at
the moment it answers. Change a document and the next answer changes. You can also show the source, so people can
check it. A comparison by Ovadia et al. (2024) found retrieval more reliable than fine-tuning for injecting new
knowledge. The AI track covers how to build it: [the RAG pipeline](#/learn/ai/rag/rag-pipeline).

**Why prompting comes first, always.** A careful prompt with a few examples
(<Term id="few-shot-prompting">few-shot prompting</Term>) is cheap and fast to change, and it gives you two things
every later step needs:

1. A **baseline**: a measured score on an <Term id="eval-set">evaluation set</Term>. Without it you cannot tell
   whether a fine-tune helped.
2. **Data**: the best prompted outputs, reviewed by people, often become the first fine-tuning examples.

**How much data does fine-tuning need?** For style and format, a few hundred to a few thousand clean examples are
often enough (the LIMA study fine-tuned a strong assistant on 1,000 carefully chosen examples). For narrow skills,
thousands. Below about a hundred, put your examples in the prompt instead. Quality matters more than quantity, which
is why there is a whole [lesson on data quality](#/learn/finetune/data/dataset-quality).

**Putting it together as a decision.** Ask these questions in order:

1. Have we tried a careful prompt and measured it? If not, do that first.
2. Is the problem missing or outdated facts? Use retrieval.
3. Is it behaviour (format, style, skill) or cost? Do we have at least a few hundred good examples? Fine-tune.
   If not, collect examples first.
4. Is it both? Combine them: fine-tune the behaviour, retrieve the facts. This is common in production.

</Section>

  error="(no error) the model quotes last quarter's prices with confidence, and invents specs for new products"

  fix="Retrieve the catalogue entries at question time; fine-tune, if at all, only the answer style."

<CommonMistake
  title="Skipping the baseline"
  wrong={'week 1: collect data\nweek 2: fine-tune\nweek 3: "it seems better?"'}
  error="(no error) no way to know if three weeks of work helped"
  cause="No measured prompt baseline and no fixed evaluation set."
  fix="Before any training, build the evaluation set and score the best prompt on it. Every candidate is compared with that number."
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Fine-tuning with 50 examples"
  wrong={'50 examples, 10 epochs'}
  error="(no error) the model parrots the 50 answers and gets worse on everything else"
  cause="Too little data to learn a general behaviour, so the model memorizes; many epochs make it worse."
  fix="Put the 50 best examples in the prompt, log real traffic, and fine-tune once you have hundreds of reviewed examples."
/>

<Section kind="practice" title="Before you start a fine-tune">

- **Write down the problem in one sentence**, and whether it is facts or behaviour.
- **Have an evaluation set** of a few hundred real inputs with reference answers or a grading rubric.
- **Score your best prompt on it.** That is the number to beat.
- **Estimate the payback** if cost is the motive, including people time.
- **Plan the data**: where examples come from, who reviews them, how you keep test items out of training.

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

The decision runs in one direction. First name the problem: missing facts or wrong behaviour. Facts, especially
changing ones, belong in the prompt through retrieval, because fine-tuning learns facts slowly and can teach the model
to answer confidently beyond what it knows. Behaviour (format, style, narrow skills) is what fine-tuning changes well,
and it can also cut cost by replacing a long prompt, which pays off at high volume. Whatever you choose, a measured
prompt baseline comes first: it is the number every fine-tune must beat, and its best outputs often become the first
training data.

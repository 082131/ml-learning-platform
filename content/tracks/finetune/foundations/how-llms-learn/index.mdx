<Section kind="intuition" title="One skill, learned three times">

Every large language model you have used was built by teaching one simple skill: **guess the next piece of text**.
Give the model "The capital of France is" and it should put a high probability on "Paris". That is the whole task.
The surprising part is how much a model has to learn to get good at it. To guess the next word of a physics
textbook it has to pick up some physics; to guess the next line of a Python file it has to pick up some Python.

The skill is learned in three stages, and each stage starts from the model the previous one produced. Here is the
big picture before any detail:

1. **Pretraining.** The model reads trillions of tokens of general text (web pages, books, code) and practises
   guessing the next token. Out comes a <Term id="base-model">base model</Term>: it knows a lot about language and the
   world, but it only *continues* text. Ask it a question and it may simply write more questions, because lists of
   questions are common on the web.
2. **Supervised fine-tuning (SFT).** We show the base model tens of thousands of example conversations written the way
   we want: a question, then a good answer. It practises the same next-token guessing, but only on this curated data.
   Out comes a model that has the *habit* of answering.
3. **Preference tuning.** We show it pairs of answers to the same question, one that people preferred and one they
   did not, and nudge it towards the preferred kind. Out comes an <Term id="instruct-model">instruct model</Term>: the
   helpful, polite assistant you chat with.

Stages 2 and 3 together are called <Term id="post-training">post-training</Term>. An analogy that holds up well:
pretraining is reading a whole library, SFT is an apprenticeship where someone shows you how answers should look,
and preference tuning is a reviewer telling you which of your two drafts is better.

This track follows that chain. This lesson is about the one number all of it is built on: the loss.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="The loss: how wrong was the guess?">

**What problem does the loss solve?** Training needs a single number that says how badly the model is doing, so that
gradient descent can change the weights to make that number smaller (the AI track's
[transformer lesson](#/learn/ai/llm/transformers-llms) shows how a model turns its scores into probabilities with a
softmax). The question the number must answer is: *how much probability did the model put on what really came next?*

**One prediction.** Say the model gave the correct next token probability $p$. The
<Term id="cross-entropy-loss">cross-entropy loss</Term> of that prediction is

$$
\text{loss} = -\ln p
$$

Read it piece by piece. $p$ is between 0 and 1. The natural log of a number below 1 is negative, so the minus sign
turns it into a positive cost. The result behaves exactly as a "wrongness" score should:

| p (probability on the truth) | loss = −ln p | in words |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1.0 | 0 | certain and right: no cost |
| 0.5 | 0.69 | a coin flip between two options |
| 0.05 | 3.00 | the truth was a long shot |
| 0.001 | 6.91 | confidently wrong: very expensive |

**Why a logarithm and not just 1 − p?** Two reasons, and both matter later in the track.

- *Sentences multiply, logs add.* The probability of a whole sentence is the product of the probabilities of its
  tokens. The log of a product is the sum of the logs, so the loss of a sentence is simply the sum of its per-token
  losses. That is why training can score every position of every sequence independently and add them up.
- *Confident mistakes must hurt most.* With 1 − p, going from p = 0.01 to p = 0.001 would barely change the cost (0.99
  to 0.999). With −ln p it jumps from 4.6 to 6.9. A model that puts almost no probability on the truth is pushed hard
  to fix that.

**A whole sentence.** Take "The capital of France is Paris". The model reads "The" and predicts "capital", reads "The
capital" and predicts "of", and so on: five predictions. Suppose it gave the correct tokens these probabilities:

| context | next token | p | −ln p |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The | capital | 0.05 | 2.996 |
| The capital | of | 0.60 | 0.511 |
| The capital of | France | 0.20 | 1.609 |
| The capital of France | is | 0.70 | 0.357 |
| The capital of France is | Paris | 0.80 | 0.223 |

The sum is $2.996 + 0.511 + 1.609 + 0.357 + 0.223 = 5.696$, and the **average loss** is $5.696 / 5 = 1.139$ nats
("nats" is the unit you get with the natural log; with log base 2 you would get bits). We average rather than sum so
that long and short texts can be compared.

**Perplexity: the same number, easier to picture.** A loss of 1.139 nats is hard to imagine. Undo the log and you get
the <Term id="perplexity">perplexity</Term>:

$$
\text{perplexity} = e^{\text{average loss}} = e^{1.139} = 3.12
$$

It means: *on average, the model was as unsure as if it were choosing uniformly among about 3 tokens.* You can check
that reading on a clean case. If the model gave every correct token p = 0.25, the loss would be −ln 0.25 = 1.386 and
the perplexity e^1.386 = 4, exactly "one of four equally likely choices".

**Where does training start?** A model with random weights spreads probability roughly evenly over its vocabulary.
With 50,000 tokens, every token gets p = 1/50,000, so the loss is ln 50,000 = 10.82 and the perplexity is 50,000.
Pretraining pushes that down to single digits on ordinary text. That drop, from "any of 50,000" to "one of a
handful", is what learning a language looks like in numbers.

**Loss and perplexity are not two ideas.** They carry the same information in different units: loss is what the
optimizer works with, perplexity is what people report. When one goes down, so does the other.

</Section>

<Section kind="experiment" title="Three stages, side by side">

The loss formula never changes, but what the model is shown, and which tokens count, does. This table is the map of
the rest of the track:

| | Pretraining | Supervised fine-tuning | Preference tuning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Starts from | random weights | the base model | the SFT model |
| Data | trillions of tokens of general text | thousands to millions of example conversations | thousands to millions of answer pairs (better, worse) |
| Training signal | next-token loss on every token | next-token loss, usually only on the answer tokens | "make the preferred answer more likely than the other one" |
| What it adds | knowledge of language, facts, code | the habit and format of answering | the style people prefer: helpful, honest, safe |
| Typical cost | months on thousands of GPUs | hours to days on a few GPUs | hours to days on a few GPUs |
| Lessons | [scaling](#/learn/finetune/pretraining/scaling-compute), [training runs](#/learn/finetune/pretraining/training-runs) | [data](#/learn/finetune/data/chat-templates-masking), [SFT](#/learn/finetune/sft/supervised-fine-tuning), [LoRA](#/learn/finetune/peft/lora) | [RLHF](#/learn/finetune/alignment/rlhf-reward-models), [DPO](#/learn/finetune/alignment/dpo) |

Two distinctions cause most of the confusion around these words:

- **Fine-tuning versus prompting.** <Term id="fine-tuning">Fine-tuning</Term> changes the model's weights, so the change
  is permanent and applies to every request. Prompting changes only the input of one request; the weights stay as
  they were. A prompt is like telling a colleague what you want today; fine-tuning is like training them until it
  becomes habit.
- **Base model versus instruct model.** Both contain the same knowledge from pretraining. The instruct model has had
  post-training, so it treats your text as a request. When you fine-tune for your own product, you usually start from
  the instruct model, unless you want to teach a completely different format.

The natural next question is "so when should *I* fine-tune instead of prompting?". That is the
[next lesson](#/learn/finetune/foundations/when-to-fine-tune).

<Section kind="mistakes" title="Misreading the loss">

<CommonMistake
  title="Comparing perplexity across tokenizers"
  wrong={'model A (32k vocabulary): perplexity 8.1\nmodel B (128k vocabulary): perplexity 10.9\n=> "A is the better model"'}
  error="(no error) a conclusion the numbers do not support"
  cause="Perplexity is per token. A tokenizer with a bigger vocabulary packs more text into each token, so each token is harder to guess even if the model is better."
  fix="Compare models with the same tokenizer, or normalize by words or bytes (bits per byte), or compare on a task you care about."
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Celebrating a low training loss"
  wrong={'epoch 8: train loss 0.21  (was 1.40)\n=> ship it'}
  error="(no error) the model has memorized its training examples and answers new questions worse"
  cause="Training loss measures examples the model has already seen. It can keep falling while the model gets worse on anything new."
  fix="Always track the loss on held-out examples the model never trains on, and stop when that one stops improving (see the SFT lesson)."
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Expecting a base model to chat"
  wrong={'base_model.generate("Summarize this email: ...")'}
  error="(no error) it continues the email, or writes another instruction, instead of a summary"
  cause="A base model has never been trained to treat text as an instruction. That habit comes from supervised fine-tuning."
  fix="Use the instruct version of the model, or prompt the base model with a pattern it can continue (an email followed by the word Summary and a colon)."
/>

<Section kind="practice" title="Habits that carry through the track">

- **Read loss as "probability on the truth".** A loss of 0.1 means p ≈ 0.9 on average; 2.3 means p ≈ 0.1.
  Converting in your head (p ≈ e^−loss) makes loss curves meaningful.
- **Report perplexity with its tokenizer and dataset**, never on its own.
- **Keep a held-out set from day one.** Every later lesson compares models on data they did not train on.
- **Know which stage you are changing.** Most teams never pretrain; they fine-tune an instruct model. That decides
  the data, cost and risks of the project.

<Section kind="summary">

The whole chain in a few sentences: an LLM learns one skill, guessing the next token, and we score each guess with
−ln p, the probability it put on the truth. Averaging that over tokens gives the loss that training pushes down;
raising e to it gives the perplexity, "how many tokens the model is hesitating between". Pretraining applies this
loss to trillions of tokens of general text and produces a base model that knows things but only continues text.
Supervised fine-tuning applies the same loss to curated conversations so the model learns the habit of answering,
and preference tuning then uses comparisons between answers to shape which answers it prefers. Fine-tuning changes
weights permanently; prompting changes only one request.

Next: [When to fine-tune, and when prompting or retrieval is enough](#/learn/finetune/foundations/when-to-fine-tune).

</Section>

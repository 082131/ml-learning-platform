<Section kind="intuition" title="Driving a training run">

The [previous lesson](#/learn/finetune/pretraining/scaling-compute) decided *how much* training to do. This one is
about *how* the training is driven, step by step. These settings are the same whether you pretrain on thousands of
GPUs or fine-tune on one, and they are the settings you will touch most often.

A training run repeats one loop: take a batch of sequences, compute the loss, compute the gradient (which direction
makes the loss smaller), and move the weights a small step in that direction. Three questions decide how well that
loop goes:

1. **How much data goes into each step?** That is the batch size.
2. **How big is each step?** That is the learning rate, and it should change over the run.
3. **What stops one bad step from ruining everything?** That is gradient clipping.

A useful analogy is walking downhill in fog. The gradient is the slope under your feet. The batch size is how many
spots you feel before deciding on a direction (more spots, a more reliable direction). The learning rate is your
stride: long strides cover ground fast but can overshoot the valley; short strides are safe but slow. Clipping is
refusing to take a stride longer than a set length, however steep a spot feels.

Big picture: batch → gradient → clip → step of size "learning rate" → repeat, while the learning rate follows a
schedule and you watch the loss curve to see whether it is working.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="Batches, schedules and clipping">

**1. The effective batch.** A GPU can only hold a few sequences at a time: the *micro batch*. To get the reliable
gradient of a bigger batch without more memory, we use <Term id="gradient-accumulation">gradient accumulation</Term>:
run several micro batches forwards and backwards, add up their gradients, and only then take one optimizer step. With
several GPUs, each computes its own micro batches and the gradients are averaged across GPUs. So

$$
\text{sequences per step} = \text{micro batch} \times \text{accumulation steps} \times \text{GPUs}
$$

and tokens per step = sequences × sequence length. Example: $4 \times 8 \times 2 = 64$ sequences of 2,048 tokens =
131,072 tokens per step. A 20-million-token dataset then takes $\lceil 20{,}000{,}000 / 131{,}072 \rceil = 153$ steps per
epoch.

*Why it matters next:* the learning rate only means something together with the batch size. A bigger batch gives a
less noisy gradient, so it tolerates a bigger step. When you change GPUs, change the accumulation so the effective
batch stays the same, and the learning rate can stay too.

**2. The learning rate and its schedule.** The <Term id="learning-rate">learning rate</Term> scales each step. One fixed
value is a compromise that fits no part of the run well, so training uses a
<Term id="lr-schedule">learning-rate schedule</Term> with two phases:

- **Warmup.** For the first few percent of steps, the rate climbs from 0 to its peak. *Why:* the Adam optimizer
  divides each gradient by a running estimate of its typical size. At step 1 those estimates are based on almost
  nothing, so a full-size step could be wildly wrong. Small steps give the estimates time to settle.
- **Decay.** After the peak, the rate falls. *Why:* early on, the weights are far from good values and big steps
  make fast progress. Later, the weights are near a good region and big steps just bounce around it; smaller steps
  let them settle in.

The common decay shapes:

| Schedule | Shape after warmup | Where you see it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| constant | flat at the peak | quick experiments |
| linear | straight line down | Hugging Face's default |
| <Term id="cosine-decay">cosine</Term> | flat, then steep, then flat at a floor | most LLM pretraining and fine-tuning recipes |
| warmup-stable-decay (WSD) | flat at the peak, then a short decay at the end | recent pretraining runs; you can stop or extend the run without knowing its length up front |

The cosine formula, part by part: $\text{progress}$ runs from 0 (end of warmup) to 1 (last step). The factor
$0.5\,(1 + \cos(\pi \cdot \text{progress}))$ goes smoothly from 1 to 0. Scaling it between a floor and the peak gives

$$
\text{lr} = \text{floor} + (\text{peak} - \text{floor}) \cdot 0.5\,(1 + \cos(\pi \cdot \text{progress}))
$$

With peak 2e-4, floor 2e-5 (10%), 30 warmup steps and 1,000 steps in total: step 15 is halfway through warmup, so
1e-4; step 515 is halfway through the decay, where the factor is exactly 0.5, so $2\times10^{-5} + 1.8\times10^{-4}
\times 0.5 = 1.1\times10^{-4}$; step 1,000 is the floor, 2e-5.

*Typical peaks*, which the later lessons explain: around 3e-4 for pretraining small models (lower for big ones),
about 1e-5 to 2e-5 for full fine-tuning, and about 1e-4 to 2e-4 for LoRA, which trains far fewer weights.

**3. Gradient clipping.** Sometimes a batch is unusual (a corrupted document, a long run of repeated characters)
and its gradient is enormous. One full step along it can throw the weights far away from everything learned so far.
<Term id="gradient-clipping">Gradient clipping</Term> by norm caps the gradient's overall length: if the length (the
square root of the sum of squared components) is above a limit, usually 1.0, every component is multiplied by
limit / length. The direction is kept; only the size is capped. For gradient (3, 4), the length is 5, so with a limit
of 1 it becomes (0.6, 0.8).

**4. Reading the loss curve.** With these three settings in place, the loss curve is your dashboard. Learn to
recognize five shapes:

- **Healthy:** a steep drop early, then a slow, steady decline. Some noise from batch to batch is normal.
- **Learning rate too high:** the loss falls, then becomes jagged, then climbs or shoots up and stays up
  (divergence). Lower the peak or lengthen the warmup.
- **Learning rate too low:** a slow, almost straight decline that is still falling when training ends.
- **<Term id="loss-spike">Loss spike</Term>:** a sudden jump, often with a huge gradient norm in the logs, that
  recovers within tens of steps. Look at the batch. If spikes repeat, lower the learning rate or tighten clipping.
  Large pretraining runs keep frequent <Term id="checkpoint">checkpoints</Term> so they can rewind past a spike that
  does not recover.
- **Plateau:** the loss flattens early. Check the data pipeline (is it repeating the same batches?) before touching
  the optimizer.

Always log the gradient norm and the learning rate next to the loss. Most diagnoses come from reading the three
together.

</Section>

<Section kind="practice" title="A starting recipe">

- **Warmup 1 to 5% of steps**, then cosine to about 10% of the peak.
- **Clip at a norm of 1.0** (the Transformers default).
- **Pick the effective batch first**, then the learning rate; keep the batch fixed when hardware changes.
- **Evaluate and checkpoint on the same schedule** so the best checkpoint can be kept.
- **Change one setting at a time** and compare runs on the same evaluation set.

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

A training run is a loop of batch, gradient, step. The effective batch (micro batch × accumulation × GPUs) decides how
reliable each gradient is, and the learning rate must be chosen together with it. The learning rate warms up so early
steps are not taken on unreliable optimizer statistics, then decays (cosine is the usual shape) so late steps settle
instead of bouncing. Gradient clipping caps any single step so one odd batch cannot undo the run. The loss curve,
read together with the learning rate and gradient norm, tells you whether the run is healthy, too aggressive, too
timid or hit a spike. The same settings drive fine-tuning, which is where the track goes next: first the data.

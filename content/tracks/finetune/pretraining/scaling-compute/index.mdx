<Section kind="intuition" title="Pretraining is a budget problem">

Most people who fine-tune will never pretrain a model. So why learn about it? Because every decision a lab made in
pretraining shows up in the model you fine-tune: how much it knows, how big it is, how expensive it is to run, and
why a "small" 8B model can beat an older 70B one. And the same arithmetic tells you what *your* fine-tune will cost.

The core idea is simple. Training a model means pushing every token of data through every parameter, forwards and
then backwards. So the work grows with **how big the model is** times **how much data it reads**. Labs have a fixed
amount of compute (GPUs × time), and the big question is how to spend it:

> Bigger model on less data, or smaller model on more data?

The big picture of this lesson is a chain: we turn size and data into **compute**, turn compute into **GPU-hours,
days and dollars**, and then use **scaling laws** to decide how to split a budget between size and data.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="From size and data to compute, time and money">

**Step 1: compute.** The rule of thumb used throughout the field is

$$
C \approx 6 \cdot N \cdot D
$$

where $N$ is the number of parameters, $D$ the number of training tokens and $C$ the
<Term id="training-flops">training compute</Term> in floating-point operations (FLOPs). Where does the 6 come from?

- In the **forward pass**, each parameter is used in one multiply and one add for each token: 2 FLOPs per
  parameter per token.
- In the **backward pass**, the model computes two sets of gradients for every layer: how the loss changes with the
  layer's input (to keep passing the signal backwards) and how it changes with the weights (to update them). That is
  about twice the forward work: 4 FLOPs.

2 + 4 = 6. Attention adds a little on top for long sequences, but for planning, 6·N·D is the number everyone uses.

*Worked example.* A 7B model on 2 trillion tokens: $6 \times 7\times10^{9} \times 2\times10^{12} = 8.4\times10^{22}$
FLOPs. Hard to picture, so the next step converts it.

**Step 2: GPU-hours.** An H100 GPU can do about $989 \times 10^{12}$ dense BF16 operations per second at its peak.
Real jobs lose time to communication between GPUs, memory traffic and pauses, so they reach only a fraction of that
peak. That fraction is the <Term id="model-flops-utilization">model FLOPs utilization (MFU)</Term>; 35 to 50% is good
for large runs. So

$$
\text{GPU-hours} = \frac{C}{\text{peak} \times \text{MFU} \times 3600}
= \frac{8.4\times10^{22}}{989\times10^{12} \times 0.4 \times 3600} \approx 58{,}982
$$

On 512 GPUs that is $58{,}982 / 512 / 24 \approx 4.8$ days. At an assumed $2.50 per GPU-hour, about $147,000 of
compute, before any failed experiments. Now "pretraining is expensive" has a number attached.

**Step 3: how to split the budget.** Given a fixed $C$, you can choose a bigger model and fewer tokens, or the
opposite. Which gives the lowest loss? This is where <Term id="scaling-law">scaling laws</Term> come in. Researchers
trained hundreds of models of different sizes on different amounts of data and found that the loss falls smoothly,
as a power law, with both. That smoothness means you can fit a curve on cheap small runs and predict the loss of an
expensive big one.

The Chinchilla paper (Hoffmann et al., 2022) used this to find the
<Term id="compute-optimal">compute-optimal</Term> split: grow parameters and tokens together, at about **20 tokens per
parameter**. Their 70B model trained on 1.4 trillion tokens (exactly 20 per parameter) beat a 280B model trained with
the same compute on fewer tokens. Before that paper, most large models were too big for their data.

Using the ratio is just algebra. With $D = 20N$, $C = 6 \cdot N \cdot 20N = 120 N^2$, so $N = \sqrt{C / 120}$. For our
$8.4\times10^{22}$ budget: $N = \sqrt{7\times10^{20}} \approx 26.5$B parameters on about 529B tokens.

**Step 4: why modern models ignore that ratio.** The natural question now is "then why was Llama 3 8B trained on 15
trillion tokens, almost 1,900 per parameter?". Because "compute-optimal" only counts *training* compute. A model that
will answer billions of requests also costs money every time it runs, and a smaller model is cheaper per request. So
labs deliberately train small models far past 20 tokens per parameter: the loss keeps improving (more slowly), and
the extra training cost is repaid many times over at inference. Keep the two goals apart:

| Goal | Best choice |
| --- | --- |
| Lowest loss for a fixed training budget | about 20 tokens per parameter |
| Best model at a fixed *serving* cost | a smaller model, trained on far more tokens |

**What goes into the tokens.** $D$ is not just "the internet". It is a <Term id="data-mixture">data mixture</Term>: web
text (filtered heavily for quality), code, books, scientific papers, math, many languages. The proportions decide
what the model is good at, so labs test mixtures on small models first, exactly as they test sizes. Deduplication and
filtering, which you will meet again in the [dataset quality lesson](#/learn/finetune/data/dataset-quality), matter as
much here as in fine-tuning.

</Section>

<Section kind="mistakes" title="Planning mistakes">

<CommonMistake
  title="Using the sparse peak"
  wrong={'hours = C / (1979e12 * 0.4) / 3600   # H100 "1,979 TFLOPS"'}
  error="(no error) the plan promises half the real training time"
  cause="The headline number assumes 2:4 structured sparsity, which normal training does not use. The dense BF16 peak is 989 TFLOP/s."
  fix="Use the dense number for the precision you train in, and a realistic MFU."
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Treating compute-optimal as best"
  wrong={'budget = 1e23\nN = sqrt(budget / 120)   # "the right size"'}
  error="(no error) a model that is cheap to train and expensive to serve"
  cause="Chinchilla minimizes training compute only. It ignores the cost of every future request."
  fix="Include expected inference volume. For widely served models, choose a smaller N and train on more tokens."
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Forgetting the failed runs"
  wrong={'cost = gpu_hours * price   # one run'}
  error="(no error) the budget runs out halfway"
  cause="Real projects include ablations, restarts after loss spikes and hardware failures, and evaluation."
  fix="Budget for the experiments around the final run, often as much again or more."
/>

</Section>

<Section kind="practice" title="Using these numbers at work">

- **Sanity-check claims.** "We trained a 70B model in a day on 64 GPUs" implies an impossible MFU; the arithmetic
  takes a minute.
- **Estimate your fine-tune before renting GPUs**: tokens × epochs × 6 × parameters, divided by peak × MFU.
- **Read model cards with this lens**: parameters and training tokens tell you how over-trained (and how cheap to
  serve for its quality) a model is.
- **For continued pretraining** (adapting a model to a new domain with lots of raw text), the same compute
  arithmetic applies on a smaller D.

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

Training cost follows one chain. Size times data gives compute, about 6·N·D FLOPs, because each parameter does 2
operations per token forwards and 4 backwards. Dividing by the GPU's dense peak times a realistic utilization gives
GPU-hours, and from there days and dollars. Scaling laws show the loss falls smoothly with size and data, so labs plan
big runs from small ones; Chinchilla found the training-compute sweet spot at about 20 tokens per parameter. Models
that will be served at scale are deliberately trained far past it, because a smaller model is cheaper for every
request. The same arithmetic shows a fine-tune costs hours where pretraining costs months.

</Section>

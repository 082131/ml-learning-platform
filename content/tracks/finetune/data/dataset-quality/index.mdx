<Section kind="intuition" title="The model becomes what you feed it">

A fine-tune copies the patterns in its data, the good ones and the bad ones. If a third of your examples are the same
answer reworded, the model learns to give that answer everywhere. If your test questions sneaked into the training
data, your evaluation measures memory, not skill. Most disappointing fine-tunes are data problems, not training
problems, which is why teams often spend more time on data than on everything else together.

This lesson covers the three data problems that matter most, in the order you should tackle them:

1. **Duplicates and near-duplicates** silently over-weight some examples.
2. **Contamination**: evaluation items hiding in the training data make every score meaningless.
3. **Synthetic data**: model-written examples give volume, but copy the generator's mistakes at scale.

The thread that connects them: every one is about making sure the numbers you see (the training signal and the
evaluation score) mean what you think they mean.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="Finding duplicates, leaks and bad synthetic data">

**1. Why duplicates hurt.** During one epoch, each example contributes one gradient step's worth of learning. An
example that appears 400 times contributes 400 times as much. It acts like a hidden weight you never chose. The model
over-fits that pattern, produces it where it does not belong, and is more likely to repeat training text word for
word. Lee et al. (2022) found that deduplicating pretraining data gave models that were as good or better with fewer
training steps, and emitted memorized text far less often. The same logic holds, at smaller scale, for fine-tuning.

Exact duplicates are easy: normalize the text (lowercase, strip punctuation and extra spaces), hash it, keep one copy
per hash. The harder case is the <Term id="near-duplicate">near-duplicate</Term>: "How do I reset my password?" versus
"How can I reset my password?". Hashes differ completely, yet for training they are the same example.

**2. Measuring "almost the same".** We need a similarity score. The standard recipe has two steps:

- **Shingles.** Turn each text into the set of its runs of $n$ consecutive words. With $n = 3$, "open settings choose
  security" gives {"open settings choose", "settings choose security"}. Word runs, rather than single words, keep some
  word order: two texts with the same words shuffled share few shingles.
- **<Term id="jaccard-similarity">Jaccard similarity</Term>.** Compare the two sets:

$$
J(A, B) = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|}
$$

The top counts shingles both texts share; the bottom counts every distinct shingle of either. The result answers
"what fraction of the content do these two have in common?": 1 means identical sets, 0 means nothing shared. For
{a, b, c, d} and {c, d, e}: 2 shared out of 5 distinct, so 0.4.

A threshold turns the score into a decision: pairs at or above it are near-duplicates, and you keep one per cluster.

**Choosing $n$ and the threshold** is a trade-off you will see directly in the explorer. Small $n$ (single words)
finds paraphrases, but also flags different questions that share template words ("Open Settings, choose …").
Large $n$ only finds near-copies. Thresholds around 0.7 to 0.8 on 5-word shingles are common for long documents;
short chat examples need smaller shingles.

**3. Scaling it up with MinHash.** Comparing every pair is fine for thousands of examples. For millions, there are
too many pairs. <Term id="minhash">MinHash</Term> solves this with a neat fact. Hash every shingle with a random hash
function and keep the smallest value. For two sets, the smallest value over their union belongs to one shingle, and
each shingle of the union is equally likely to be it. The two sets have the *same* minimum exactly when that shingle
is in both, so:

$$
P(\text{minimums agree}) = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|} = J(A, B)
$$

Repeat with 128 different hash functions and the share of agreeing positions estimates $J$. Each document is now a
fixed list of 128 numbers, however long it is, and a further trick (locality-sensitive hashing: grouping documents
whose signatures agree in whole bands) finds likely pairs without comparing every pair. This is how web-scale
pretraining data is deduplicated.

**4. Contamination.** <Term id="data-contamination">Contamination</Term> means evaluation items are in the training
data. It is the most expensive data bug, because it does not make the model worse; it makes the *measurement* wrong,
so you ship believing in a gain that is not there. It sneaks in through scraped web pages that quote benchmarks,
public FAQs that overlap your test questions, synthetic data generated from prompts that included test items, and
near-duplicates split across train and test.

The check is n-gram overlap: collect all n-grams of the evaluation items and drop any training example that shares
one. GPT-3's analysis used 13-word n-grams; short examples need a smaller $n$. Two rules make it reliable:

- **Deduplicate before you split**, so near-duplicate clusters cannot straddle train and test.
- **Clean the training side**, never edit the evaluation set to make the check pass.

The warning sign to remember: a jump that is too good to be true (71% to 97%) is contamination until proven
otherwise.

**5. Synthetic data.** <Term id="synthetic-data">Synthetic data</Term> is generated by a model: a strong model writes
answers to your questions, or rewrites examples into new variations. It is cheap and fast, and it is how many open
models were post-trained. It also has predictable failure modes, each with a check:

- *It copies the generator's mistakes.* Review a random sample by hand; verify facts against your documents.
- *It repeats itself.* Generators fall into a few phrasings; deduplicate it like any other data.
- *It drifts in style.* Long, hedged, list-heavy answers become your model's style. Filter on length and format.
- *It can leak the test set* if test items were in the generation prompts. Run the contamination check on it too.

**Quality over quantity.** The LIMA study fine-tuned a 65B model on just 1,000 carefully curated examples and got a
strong assistant. You will usually have more data than that, but the lesson holds: removing bad and duplicated
examples often helps more than adding new ones.

</Section>

<CommonMistake
  title="Splitting before deduplicating"
  wrong={'train, test = split(examples)\ntrain = dedup(train)'}
  error="(no error) test score inflated by near-copies of test items in training"
  cause="Near-duplicate clusters straddle the split, so the model has effectively seen part of the test set."
  fix="Deduplicate (or group clusters) first, then split by cluster."
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Trusting a synthetic dataset unread"
  wrong={'synthetic = [strong_model(q) for q in questions]   # 20,000 answers\ntrain(synthetic)'}

<Section kind="practice" title="A data-quality pipeline">

- **Normalize, then exact-dedup** with a hash.
- **Near-dedup** with shingles and Jaccard (MinHash at scale); inspect a sample of flagged pairs to tune the threshold.
- **Split after deduplication**, by cluster.
- **Decontaminate** every training source against the evaluation set.
- **Filter synthetic data**: sample review, fact checks, length and format filters.
- **Record what you removed and why**, so a surprising result can be traced back to the data.

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

Data quality decides what the model learns and whether you can trust its score. Duplicates act as hidden weights, so
we find near-duplicates by turning texts into sets of word runs and measuring their overlap with Jaccard similarity,
and at scale we estimate that overlap cheaply with MinHash, whose minimum hashes agree with probability equal to the
Jaccard similarity. Contamination, evaluation items hiding in training data, does not hurt the model but corrupts the
measurement, so we deduplicate before splitting and remove training examples that share n-grams with the test set.
Synthetic data adds volume but copies the generator's mistakes, so it gets the same checks plus a human sample review.
With clean, well-formatted data, we can finally train.

</Section>

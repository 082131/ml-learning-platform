<Section kind="intuition" title="A busy kitchen that serves slowly">

A restaurant kitchen can be slow for different reasons. The cooks may be overwhelmed (too much to cook), the
ingredients may arrive slowly from the store room (they wait with idle hands), or each order may be so small that
walking to the stove takes longer than cooking. From the dining room all three look the same: the kitchen is
busy and the food is late. Hiring more cooks only helps in the first case.

A GPU has the same three bottlenecks: arithmetic (compute), moving data to and from its memory (memory), and
the fixed cost of starting each piece of work (launch). Its headline utilization number, like "the kitchen is
busy", cannot tell them apart. This lesson gives you a way to tell which one you have, so you can pick the
optimization that helps.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="Arithmetic intensity and the roofline">

Every kernel does some FLOPs and moves some bytes between GPU memory and the chip. Their ratio is its
<Term id="arithmetic-intensity">arithmetic intensity</Term>, in FLOPs per byte. The
<Term id="roofline-model">roofline model</Term> (first met in the batching lesson) bounds a kernel's speed:

$$
\text{attainable FLOP/s} = \min(\text{peak FLOP/s},\ \text{intensity} \times \text{bandwidth})
$$

The two limits meet at the **ridge point**, peak ÷ bandwidth. For dense FP16 on tensor cores:

| GPU | Peak FP16 (dense) | Bandwidth | Ridge (FLOP/byte) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| T4 | 65 TFLOP/s | 320 GB/s | 203 |
| L4 | 121 TFLOP/s | 300 GB/s | 403 |
| A100 80GB PCIe | 312 TFLOP/s | 1,935 GB/s | 161 |
| H100 SXM | 989 TFLOP/s | 3,350 GB/s | 295 |

Typical inference kernels, with a hidden size of 4,096:

- **A linear layer** at batch $B$ does $2 B d^2$ FLOPs and reads $2 d^2$ bytes of FP16 weights, so its intensity is
  about $B$. At batch 1 it is memory-bound everywhere; past the ridge it becomes compute-bound.
- **Attention while generating** reads each sequence's own KV cache: about 1 FLOP per byte *at any batch*, because
  more sequences bring proportionally more cache to read.
- **Softmax, layer norm, activations and residual adds** do a few FLOPs per element: memory-bound, and when the
  tensors are small, launch-bound, since starting a kernel costs a few microseconds.

**What utilization means.** nvidia-smi's <Term id="gpu-utilization">GPU utilization</Term> is the percentage of
time at least one kernel was running. It finds a GPU that sits idle, waiting for the CPU or for data, but a kernel
that uses 0.5% of the arithmetic units still counts as 100%. To see how well the GPU is used, compare achieved
FLOP/s and bytes/s with the roofs, using hardware counters (DCGM) or a profiler (Nsight Compute).

**Gaps on the timeline** are the other bottleneck: the GPU waiting for the CPU to preprocess the next batch, for a
host-to-device copy, or for Python to launch the next kernel. They show up as utilization below 100% and as gaps
in an Nsight Systems or PyTorch profiler timeline.

<Callout kind="simplification">
  The roofline experiment is an estimate: each kernel takes the larger of FLOPs ÷ peak and bytes ÷ bandwidth, plus
  5 µs to launch. Real kernels reach perhaps 60 to 90% of either roof, and caches can serve some bytes faster than
  memory. The Code section measures a real roofline, on a CPU.
</Callout>

Read it against the two roofs:

- **Compute roof.** At batch 512 the multiplication reaches 511 GFLOP/s. Four cores at 2.1 GHz, each doing 64
  FP32 FLOPs per cycle (two AVX-512 fused multiply-add units), would peak at about 538 GFLOP/s; the VM does not

- **Memory roof.** At batch 1 the multiplication does 28 GFLOP/s while moving 56 GB/s: memory-bound, as its

  come from cache.)

  OpenBLAS most likely uses a matrix-vector routine for one row and a general routine for more, which first copies
  W into blocks: for a few rows, that copy costs more than it saves. The roofline is a ceiling, not a prediction; a
  measurement far below it points at the kernel.
- **The add** reaches 0.7 GFLOP/s at 8.5 GB/s. NumPy runs it on one core, so it is limited by one core's bandwidth,

Things to try:

- With the linear layer on the A100, raise the batch from 1 to 128. Time goes from 22.3 µs to only 23.4 µs while
  the work grows 128 times: the weights are read once either way. At 256 it crosses the ridge and becomes
  compute-bound (32.5 µs, 84.6% of peak).
- Switch to the L4 at batch 256. Its ridge is at 403, so the same layer is still memory-bound there, at 54.3% of
  peak.
- Select attention and move the batch. The dot does not move right: intensity stays at 1.00, and time grows with
  every sequence (39.7 µs at batch 1, 1,114.8 µs at 32).
- Select softmax or add at small batches. They are launch-bound: fusing them into the neighboring kernel, or
  replaying the whole step as a <Term id="cuda-graph">CUDA graph</Term>, removes the launches.

<Section kind="mistakes" title="Bottleneck mistakes">

<CommonMistake
  title="Reading 100% utilization as 'the GPU is saturated'"
  wrong={'# nvidia-smi: utilization.gpu 100 %\n# "We need a bigger GPU."'}
  error="No error. A memory-bound batch-1 workload shows 100% while using a fraction of a percent of the arithmetic."
  cause="utilization.gpu counts time during which any kernel runs, not how much of the GPU the kernel uses."
  fix={'# Check achieved throughput against the roofs first:\n# DCGM_FI_PROF_PIPE_TENSOR_ACTIVE, DCGM_FI_PROF_DRAM_ACTIVE, or ncu --set roofline\n# A memory-bound layer often gets far more throughput from a larger batch than from a larger GPU.'}
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Optimizing arithmetic in a memory-bound model"
  wrong={'# Switch an A100 decode server from FP16 to FP8 compute to "double FLOP/s"'}
  error="No error, and little speed-up: the step was limited by reading weights and the KV cache, not by FLOPs."
  cause="Faster arithmetic raises the flat roof; a memory-bound kernel sits under the slanted one."
  fix={'# Reduce bytes instead: weight-only quantization (8 or 4 bits per weight),\n# a quantized KV cache, larger batches for the weight reads, fused kernels.'}
/>

<Callout kind="warning">
  Do not judge a model by its total FLOPs. Two models with the same FLOPs can differ several times in speed if
  one is made of large matrix multiplications and the other of many small elementwise operations.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section kind="practice" title="A bottleneck checklist">

1. **Is the GPU idle?** Look at utilization and an Nsight Systems or PyTorch profiler timeline. Gaps mean the CPU,
   data loading, copies or Python launches are the bottleneck: add loader workers, pin memory, overlap copies,
   batch requests, or capture CUDA graphs.
2. **Which kernels take the time?** Sort the profiler table by GPU time. Usually a few kernels dominate.
3. **Where do they sit on the roofline?** Compute their intensity from shapes, or let Nsight Compute measure it.
4. **Memory-bound?** Move fewer bytes: batch requests that share weights, fuse neighboring kernels, quantize
   weights or the KV cache.
5. **Compute-bound?** Use tensor cores in a lower precision, a better kernel, or more GPUs.
6. **Launch-bound?** Fuse, compile (`torch.compile`, TensorRT), or replay a CUDA graph.
7. **Measure again** after each change. Fixing one bottleneck exposes the next.

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

- Arithmetic intensity (FLOPs per byte) against the GPU's ridge point tells memory-bound from compute-bound.
- Batching raises the intensity of operations that share weights, not of attention over per-sequence KV caches.
- Small elementwise kernels are often launch-bound: fuse them or replay a CUDA graph.
- nvidia-smi utilization shows whether a kernel runs, not how well it uses the GPU; use counters or a profiler.
- A GPU waiting for the CPU needs overlap and more loader workers, not a faster GPU.

<Section kind="intuition" title="Knowing before your users tell you">

A model service that passed every test can still fail in production: traffic doubles after a launch, a dependency
slows down, a new model version returns errors for one kind of input, a memory leak grows for a week. Users notice
within minutes. The goal of monitoring is to notice first, and the goal of load testing is to know in advance how
much the service can take and what happens beyond that.

Both need the same discipline as the profiling lesson: measure what users experience (percentiles and errors,
not averages), and decide in advance what "too bad" means, so that an alert means "act now" and not "something
twitched".

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="Load tests, golden signals and SLOs">

**Load testing.** Offer the service a known rate of requests with an *open-loop* generator (requests leave on a
schedule, not after the previous reply) and raise the rate step by step. Record served throughput, latency
percentiles and errors at each step. The <Term id="saturation-point">saturation point</Term> is where served
throughput stops following the offered rate: beyond it a queue builds, latency climbs until requests time out, and
errors appear. Run soak tests too (hours at expected load) to find leaks.

**What to monitor.** The four golden signals, per model version:

| Signal | For a model server | Typical metric |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Latency | p50, p95, p99 of request time, successful and failed separately | a histogram |
| Traffic | requests per second, items per second | a counter |
| Errors | share of 5xx responses, timeouts, validation failures | a counter by status |
| Saturation | requests in flight, queue length, CPU, GPU utilization and memory | gauges |

Add model-specific signals: batch sizes actually formed, input statistics (for drift), the distribution of
predicted classes or confidence, and the share of requests hitting a fallback.

**Objectives and alerts.** A <Term id="slo">service-level objective</Term> states what users can expect, for
example "99.9% of requests succeed within 300 ms over 30 days". Its complement is the
<Term id="error-budget">error budget</Term> (0.1% of requests), and the *burn rate* says how fast it is being
spent: at burn rate 1 the budget lasts exactly 30 days, at 14.4 it lasts 50 hours. Alerting on burn rate over two
windows, a long one so the alert means something and a short one so it stops once the problem is fixed, pages for
incidents that threaten the objective and stays quiet for blips.

**Debugging a slowdown.** Start from the symptom on the dashboard (which percentile, since when, which version,
which replicas), then narrow down with <Term id="distributed-trace">traces</Term>: one slow request broken into
spans (parse, queue, model, serialize) shows where the time went. Typical causes: a traffic increase past
saturation, a noisy neighbor or throttled CPU, a larger input size, a new model version, garbage collection or
memory pressure, and a slow dependency.

Up to 450 requests per second the service keeps up: every request is served, the median stays near 4 ms and p99
under 30 ms. At 500 the p99 jumps to between 261 ms and 1.2 s in two runs: the server is at its limit and short
queues form. At 600 it is past saturation. Requests arrive faster than they finish, the queue grows for the whole

degrade gently; latency explodes.

The saturation point here, about 500 requests per second, is lower than the 680 the serving lesson reached with
16 threads, because this generator runs on the same four cores and needs more CPU per request. Load generators
belong on separate machines, and the result belongs to the whole setup, not to the server alone.

Things to try:

- Set a 20% spike for 2 minutes. The 1% threshold pages, but the spike used only 0.9% of the month's budget and
  was over before anyone could act. Neither burn-rate rule fires.

- Keep 5% for 30 minutes and switch the SLO to 99%. The budget is ten times larger, the incident uses 0.3% of it,

<CommonMistake
  title="Averaging percentiles across replicas"
  wrong={'avg(predict_latency_p99)   # each replica reports its own p99'}
  error="No error. The fleet's p99 is wrong, usually too low, because one slow replica's tail is diluted by the fast ones."
  cause="Percentiles do not average. The p99 of the whole fleet needs the whole distribution."
  fix={'histogram_quantile(0.99, sum by (le) (rate(predict_latency_seconds_bucket[5m])))'}
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Load testing with a closed-loop client and calling it capacity"
  wrong={'# 16 threads, each sends the next request when the previous reply arrives\n# "The server handles 700 req/s at p99 50 ms"'}
  error="No error. The client slows down with the server, so overload never shows; real users would see far worse latency at that rate."
  cause="Coordinated omission: the load generator stops sending exactly when the server struggles."
  fix={'# Open loop: send on a fixed schedule and measure from the due time.\n# Report the offered rate, the served rate and the percentiles at each step.'}
/>

<Callout kind="warning">
  Do not alert on CPU or GPU utilization alone. High utilization with good latency is efficiency, not an incident;
  page on what users feel (errors and latency against the SLO) and use saturation signals for scaling and for
  diagnosis.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section kind="practice" title="Before and after launch">

1. **Before launch**: run an open-loop ramp on the production image and hardware; record the saturation point
   and the p99 at your expected and peak rates. Run a soak test for hours at expected load.
2. **Capacity**: plan for peak traffic at no more than 60 to 70% of saturation per replica, plus spare replicas.
3. **Dashboards**: the four golden signals per model version, plus batch sizes, input statistics and prediction
   distribution.
4. **Alerts**: SLO burn-rate pages for errors and latency; tickets for slow burns, disk, certificates and drift.
5. **Runbooks**: for each alert, what it means, the first three things to check, and how to roll back.
6. **After an incident**: a blameless review with a timeline from the metrics, and one change that would have
   caught it sooner.

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

- Load test open-loop with rising rates; the saturation point is where served throughput stops following the
  offered rate and latency climbs.
- Monitor latency percentiles, traffic, errors and saturation per model version, from histograms, not averages.
- Alert on error-budget burn rate over two windows, so pages mean a real threat to the objective.
- Debug from the symptom down: which percentile, since when, which version, then traces of slow requests.

<Section kind="intuition" title="Is the difference real, or just this sample?">

The variant’s visits averaged 0.5 minutes longer. Should you ship it? Run the same experiment again with
different users and the gap would be different: maybe 0.8, maybe −0.1. Every number computed from a sample
carries that wobble.

Statistical inference measures the wobble. A **confidence interval** says how far the true value could
plausibly be from your estimate. A **p-value** says how often chance alone would produce a gap as large as yours.
This lesson computes both by simulation, shuffling and resampling with NumPy, so you can see where they come
from instead of trusting a formula, and then turns them into the rules of a sound A/B test.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="Standard error, intervals, tests and A/B design">

**Sampling variability.** A statistic computed from a random sample (a mean, a conversion rate) varies from
sample to sample. Its standard deviation across samples is the <Term id="standard-error">standard error</Term>.
For a mean, $\mathrm{SE} = \sigma / \sqrt{n}$: four times the data halves the uncertainty.

**Bootstrap confidence interval.** Resample your data **with replacement** many times, recompute the statistic
each time, and take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the results. That gives a 95%
<Term id="confidence-interval">confidence interval</Term>. The 95% describes the method: across many samples,
95% of intervals built this way contain the true value. It works for medians and other statistics without a
formula.

**Permutation test.** The null hypothesis says the label (control or variant) does not matter. So shuffle the
labels many times and record the difference each shuffle produces: that is the distribution of differences
under "no effect". The <Term id="p-value">p-value</Term> is the share of shuffled differences at least as extreme
as the one observed (<Term id="permutation-test">permutation test</Term>). A small p-value means the data would
be surprising if there were no effect. It is **not** the probability that there is no effect, and it says
nothing about how big the effect is: always report the estimate and its interval too.

**A/B tests.** An <Term id="ab-test">A/B test</Term> randomly assigns users to versions and compares one primary
metric chosen in advance. Before starting, fix the significance level (usually 5%), the smallest lift worth
detecting, and the sample size that gives enough **power** (usually 80%) to detect it. Then avoid three traps:
**peeking** (stopping the first time p dips below 0.05 inflates false positives), **many metrics** (test 20 and
about one will look significant by chance; correct with Bonferroni, dividing 0.05 by the number of tests), and
**unequal groups** (check that assignment really was 50/50).

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

- Estimates wobble from sample to sample; the standard error ($\sigma / \sqrt{n}$ for a mean) measures it.
- A bootstrap interval resamples the data; a permutation test shuffles the labels. Both need only NumPy.
- A p-value is the chance of data this extreme if there were no effect: not the chance of no effect, and not a
  size. Report the estimate with its interval.
- Plan A/B tests in advance: one primary metric, a fixed sample size, no peeking, corrections for many metrics.

<Section kind="intuition" title="Two questions every product team asks">

Every online product eventually asks two questions about its users. The first is **"where do people give up?"**:
of everyone who arrives, how many look at a product, how many put it in the cart, how many pay? That chain of
steps is a <Term id="conversion-funnel">funnel</Term>, named after its shape: wide at the top, narrower at each
step. The step with the biggest drop is where the team should look first.

The second question is **"do people come back?"**: of everyone who signed up in January, how many were still
active in February, in March? Grouping users by when they started is a cohort, and the share still active later
is <Term id="cohort-retention">retention</Term>. It is the difference between a product people try once and one
they keep using.

Both questions sound like a COUNT and a division, and that is exactly why they go wrong so often. A rate is
"how many succeeded, out of how many", and every word in that sentence hides a decision. *How many* of what:
events, or people? *Succeeded* how: in order, and within what time? *Out of* whom: everyone who started, or only
the ones the query happened to keep? This lesson builds both reports from the shop's `events` table with the
tools of the last three chapters (conditional aggregation, CTEs, LEFT JOINs and window functions), and each step
makes one of those decisions explicit. You will see the same data give a visit-to-purchase conversion of 37.5,
50 or 75 percent, and a retention table with three cells that look like collapsing customers but are really
months that have not happened yet.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="What exactly are we counting?">

The `events` table has one row per thing a user did: `visit`, `view_product`, `add_to_cart`, `checkout` and
`purchase`, each with a `user_id` and an `event_time`. 8 users, 74 events, from January to early April.

**Decision 1: count units, not rows.** The first instinct is `COUNT(*)` per event. Run it next to
`COUNT(DISTINCT user_id)` and the two disagree everywhere: 21 visits but only 8 visitors, 14 add-to-cart events
but only 7 users who added something. Event counts measure *activity*: Ada visited 5 times, and Ben's app sent
add_to_cart twice in the same minute. A conversion rate is a question about *people* ("what share of visitors
bought?"), so each person should count once per step. Divide event counts and you get 11 / 21 = 52.4 percent,
a number that is neither "share of visitors who bought" (it divides purchases by visits) nor anything else you
would want to report.

<SqlRunner example={eventsVsUsers} title="events_vs_users.sql" />

**The simplest correct funnel: "did the user ever do it?"** Turn each user into one row with a 0 or 1 per step,
then add up the columns. This is <Term id="conditional-aggregation">conditional aggregation</Term> from the
GROUP BY lesson: `event = 'visit'` is 1 or 0 for each row, and `MAX` over a user's rows is 1 if any of them was a
visit. The result is 8, 8, 7, 6, 6.

<SqlRunner example={everFunnel} title="ever_funnel.sql" />

**Decision 2: order and time.** The "ever" funnel ignores *when* things happened. A user who visited in January
and bought in April counts as converted, and so would a user who bought *before* they ever viewed a product
(through a direct link, say). Usually the question is narrower: "of the people who visited, how many went on to
buy, in that order, within some time?". That needs each step to happen after the previous one, and within a
window measured from the first visit. The way to express "after the previous step" in SQL is to build the steps
as a chain of CTEs, where each step only looks at the users who reached the step before and only at events at or
after the time they reached it. Because each step reads from the previous one, a later step can never contain
more users than an earlier step, which is the defining shape of a funnel.

**Decision 3: the denominator.** A funnel gives two kinds of percentage. *Step conversion* divides each step by
the previous one ("of the users who added to cart, 80 percent checked out") and tells you where people drop.
*Overall conversion* divides by the first step ("50 percent of visitors bought") and tells you the end result.
Both are useful; they are different answers, so a funnel report should label which is which. Choosing the
<Term id="metric-denominator">denominator</Term> is choosing the question.

</Section>

- **Any time.** 8, 8, 7, 6, 6, identical to the "ever" funnel above, because in this data nobody did a step
  before the one it follows. Conversion is now 75 percent, double the 1-hour figure.
- **Count event rows.** 21, 17, 14, 11, 11. The top of the funnel is inflated by repeat visits, and the steps no
  longer describe people at all. The window setting does nothing here, because raw event counts have no idea of
  order or time.

The point of the explorer is not that one window is right. "Bought in the same sitting" and "bought within a
week" are both legitimate questions. The point is that a conversion rate without its definition is not a number
you can compare: last month's 1-hour rate against this month's 7-day rate would show a fake 33 percent
improvement.

<Section kind="code" title="The ordered funnel and the cohort table, step by step">

**The ordered funnel as a chain.** Here is the 7-day version from the explorer, written as named steps. Select a
step to see only its rows. `visited` sets each user's clock (`start`, the first visit). Every later step joins
the previous step's users to their events of the next type that happened at or after the previous step's time
(`e.event_time >= p.t`) and no later than 7 days after the start (`datetime(p.start, '+7 days')`). `MIN` keeps the
first such event, which becomes the time the next step must follow.

<CteSteps example={orderedFunnel} title="Ordered 7-day funnel" />

The last two steps are worth a second look. `funnel` stacks the five counts into five rows with UNION ALL, which
changes the grain to "one row per step". Once the steps are rows, the window functions from the last chapter do
the percentages: `LAG(users)` is the previous step, so `users / LAG(users)` is step conversion, and
`FIRST_VALUE(users)` is the top of the funnel, so `users / FIRST_VALUE(users)` is overall conversion. The biggest
drop is view_product to add_to_cart (71.4 percent continue); checkout to purchase loses nobody.

**Cohort retention, the naive version.** A cohort table has one row per cohort (here, signup month) and one
column per "months since signup": month 0 is the signup month itself, month 1 the next calendar month, and so on.
Each cell is "share of the cohort active in that month". The first attempt below gets the mechanics right:
it turns months into numbers (year × 12 + month) so "months since signup" is a subtraction, and uses
`COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN month_n = 1 THEN user_id END)` to count each user once per cell.

<SqlRunner example={naiveCohorts} title="naive_cohorts.sql" />

Now read the bottom row as a manager would: the March cohort went from 100 percent to 0 percent after one month.
That looks like a disaster, and it is an illusion. The data ends on 6 April. March's month 1 is April, which
has had 6 days; its month 2 is May, which has not happened at all. The February row has the same problem in its
last two cells. A 0 in those cells does not mean "we measured and nobody came back", it means "we did not
measure", and the query cannot tell those apart unless we tell it. (Month 0 is 100 percent for every cohort for a
duller reason: in this shop every customer visited in the month they signed up, so month 0 measures nothing. Many teams start the
table at month 1 for that reason.)

There is a second, quieter problem: the naive query takes the cohort size from users *with events*. That is
everyone here, but a customer who signed up and never did anything would vanish from the denominator and lift
every percentage in their row.

**The honest version.** Four steps fix both problems. `cohorts` takes the size from `customers`, the list of
everyone who signed up. `as_of` finds the last *complete* month from the data. `cells` builds every (cohort,
month) cell with a cross join, so empty cells exist, LEFT JOINs the activity onto it, and marks whether the cell's
calendar month is complete. The final step only fills complete cells.

<CteSteps example={honestCohorts} title="Cohort retention with incomplete months left blank" />

The result has the characteristic triangle shape of every real retention table: the oldest cohort has the most
columns, the newest the fewest. January kept everyone in month 1 and two of three (66.7 percent) in month 2;
February kept two of three in month 1. Reading down a column compares cohorts at the same age, which is the
comparison that matters ("are newer customers sticking better than older ones?"). Reading along a row follows
one group over time.

**Averaging a retention column.** If you need one month-1 number, average only complete cells and weight by
cohort size: (3 + 2) / (3 + 3) = 83.3 percent. Averaging the naive column (100, 66.7 and the fake 0) gives 55.6
percent, a number about a month that has not happened. And notice what the triangle implies for later columns:
month 2 here is the January cohort alone. Long-term retention is always measured on the oldest users, who may
behave differently from the newest, so treat the right-hand end of a retention table with care.

</Section>

<Section kind="mistakes" title="Funnel and retention mistakes">

<CommonMistake
  title="Counting events instead of users"
  wrong={'SELECT event, COUNT(*) FROM events GROUP BY event'}
  error="(no error) 21 'visitors', and every conversion rate mixes repeat activity with people"
  cause="One user can produce many events of the same type, including duplicates."
  fix="COUNT(DISTINCT user_id) per step, or build one row per user with step flags first."
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="A filter that shrinks the denominator"
  wrong={"FROM customers c JOIN orders o ON o.customer_id = c.id\nWHERE o.order_date <= date(c.signup_date, '+7 days')"}
  error="(no error) every cohort 'converts' 100 percent"
  cause="Customers without a matching order are removed before counting, so only successes are left to divide by."
  fix="LEFT JOIN orders with the condition in ON, and count customers from the customers table."
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Steps that do not depend on each other"
  wrong={"carted AS (SELECT user_id FROM events WHERE event = 'add_to_cart')   -- not joined to viewed"}
  error="(no error) a later step can be bigger than an earlier one"
  cause="Each step was counted independently, so order was never enforced."
  fix="Build each step from the previous step's users and require event_time >= the previous step's time."
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Zeros for periods that have not happened"
  wrong={'COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN month_n = 1 THEN user_id END) / COUNT(DISTINCT user_id)'}
  error="(no error) the newest cohort appears to churn completely"
  cause="Cells after the end of the data, or in the current partial month, are computed as 0 instead of unknown."
  fix="Compute the last complete period and return NULL for cells beyond it."
/>

</Section>

<Section kind="practice" title="Habits for funnels and retention">

- **Write the definition first, in words**: unit (users), order (each step after the previous), window (7 days
  from the first visit), denominator (previous step or first step).
- **Count each unit once per step** with DISTINCT or one-row-per-user flags.
- **Take the denominator from the full population** (everyone who signed up), not from rows that survived a join.
- **Leave incomplete periods blank** and say what date the data runs to.
- **Weight averages by cohort size**, and remember that late columns describe only the oldest cohorts.

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

Funnels and retention tables are rates, and a rate is only as good as its definition. Counting users instead of
events makes each step describe people. Building each funnel step from the previous step's users, at or after the
previous step's time and within a window from the start, makes the steps ordered and makes the funnel shrink by
construction; LAG and FIRST_VALUE then give step and overall conversion. The same data gives 37.5, 50 or 75 percent
conversion depending on the window, so the window belongs in the report. A cohort table counts each cohort's
active users per month since signup, takes its denominator from everyone who signed up, and leaves months that
have not fully happened blank, which is what gives retention tables their triangle shape.

Funnels measured within a time window raise the next question: what is a "visit", and when does one end? That is
sessions, and with them deduplication and streaks.

</Section>

<Section kind="intuition" title="Questions with no fixed number of steps">

"Who does Sam manage?" is one self-join: find the employees whose `manager_id` is Sam's id. "Who is in Sam's
whole team, including the people his reports manage?" is harder. Uma reports to Sam, and Xia reports to Uma, so
Xia is in Sam's team too. One join finds one level. Two joins find two levels. But a real company might have three
levels in one department and eight in another, and you cannot write "join as many times as needed".

A <Term id="recursive-cte">recursive CTE</Term> solves this by repeating one step until there is nothing left to
find. You give it a starting point ("Sam") and a rule for taking one step ("everyone who reports to the people I
found last time"). The database applies the rule, then applies it again to what it just found, and keeps going
until a step finds nobody new.

The same machinery generates rows that are not in any table. Starting from 1 and repeatedly adding 1 produces a
list of numbers; starting from 1 February and repeatedly adding a day produces a calendar. That turns out to be
exactly what reports need to show days with zero sales, which is the second half of this lesson.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="Anchor, step, and the rows each step can see">

A recursive CTE has three parts, joined by `UNION ALL`:

1. **The anchor** is an ordinary SELECT. It runs once and produces the starting rows.
2. **The step** is a SELECT that reads the CTE itself. Each time it runs, it sees only the rows added by the
   previous iteration (the anchor, the first time), and produces new rows from them.
3. **The stop.** When an iteration's step produces no rows, there is nothing to feed the next iteration, and the
   recursion ends. The result is every row from every iteration.

The smallest example counts to five. The anchor gives 1. The step turns 1 into 2, then 2 into 3, and so on.
When the last row is 5, `WHERE i < 5` is false, the step adds nothing, and the query finishes.

<SqlRunner example={countToFive} title="count.sql" />

Point 2 is the one to understand properly, because it is why the walk moves one level at a time. If the step
could see every row found so far, it would rediscover the same rows on every iteration and never stop. Seeing only
the newest rows means each iteration is "the next level down".

**Walking a hierarchy.** For the org chart, the anchor is the person at the top (`manager_id IS NULL`), and the
step joins `employees` to the rows found last time on `e.manager_id = c.id`: "people whose manager I just found".
Two extra columns carry information down the tree: `level` adds 1 per step, and `path` appends each name, so
every row knows how it was reached.

<SqlRunner example={orgChart} title="org_chart.sql" />

Sorting by `path` prints the tree in reading order: Rosa, then Sam and his whole branch (Uma, Xia, Vic), then
Tara and hers. The query never says how deep the tree is; it finds out by walking.

</Section>

Things to try:

- **Rosa, down.** Anchor: Rosa. Step 1: Sam and Tara. Step 2: Uma, Vic and Wen. Step 3: Xia. Step 4: nobody
  reports to Xia, so it adds nothing. 5 iterations, 7 rows: the whole company.
- **Xia, up.** The step flips direction: instead of "people who report to the last rows", it is "the manager of
  the last rows" (`m.id = c.manager_id`). Xia, Uma, Sam, Rosa, then Rosa's manager is NULL and NULL matches
  nothing, so the walk ends.
- **Wen, down.** The anchor is Wen and the very first step is empty. A recursive CTE always returns at least its
  anchor rows.

A recursive CTE can feed an ordinary aggregate. Here the walk collects the ids of everyone in Sam's team (Sam,

150 + 110 + 105 + 95 = 460.

**Generating a calendar.** Reports often need every day, even days with no data. GROUP BY over `orders` can only
produce rows for days that had orders; February had orders on just 6 days, so a chart drawn from that would
skip the other 22 and connect the dots across them. A <Term id="date-spine">date spine</Term> is the fix: generate
every date in the range with a recursive CTE, then LEFT JOIN the data onto it. `date(day, '+1 day')` is SQLite's
date arithmetic (PostgreSQL has `generate_series`, which does the same in one function call).

<SqlRunner example={dateSpine} title="february_by_day.sql" />

This combines three earlier ideas in one chain: the recursion makes the 28 days, the LEFT JOIN keeps days with no
matching order, and `COUNT(o.id)` turns those unmatched days into 0 (COUNT(*) would wrongly give 1).

**Make sure it stops.** A recursion stops only when a step adds nothing. If the stop condition is missing, or the
data contains a cycle (A manages B and B manages A), it never does. A depth limit costs nothing and is good
practice on real hierarchies:

<SqlRunner example={runaway} title="depth_limit.sql" />

If a runaway query does reach this site's database, it is stopped after 10 seconds and the database is restarted,
so you can experiment safely.

</Section>

<Section kind="mistakes" title="Recursion that never ends or returns too much">

<CommonMistake
  title="No stop condition"
  wrong={'WITH RECURSIVE n(i) AS (SELECT 1 UNION ALL SELECT i + 1 FROM n) SELECT i FROM n;'}
  error="(runs until killed) Stopped after 10 seconds. The query may never finish ..."
  cause="Every iteration adds a row, so there is always something to feed the next one."
  fix="Add a condition to the step: WHERE i < 100. In hierarchies add a level limit as a safety net."
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Joining the step the wrong way round"
  wrong={'JOIN chart AS c ON e.id = c.manager_id   -- meant to go down the tree'}
  error="(no error) the walk goes UP from the anchor instead of down"
  cause="e.id = c.manager_id finds the managers of the rows found so far."
  fix="Down the tree: e.manager_id = c.id (people whose manager I found). Up: m.id = c.manager_id."
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Grouping without a spine"
  wrong={'SELECT order_date, COUNT(*) FROM orders GROUP BY order_date;'}
  error="(no error) days with no orders are missing, and charts draw straight lines across them"
  cause="GROUP BY only makes groups for values that occur."
  fix="Generate the days with a recursive CTE (or a calendar table) and LEFT JOIN the data onto it, counting a column of the data."
/>

</Section>

<Section kind="practice" title="Habits for recursive queries">

- **Write the anchor alone first** and check it returns the starting rows you expect.
- **Carry a level (and, for trees, a path)** so you can see how each row was reached.
- **Always have a stop condition**, plus a depth limit on real hierarchies in case of cycles.
- **Use a date spine** whenever a report must show periods with no data.

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

A recursive CTE starts from anchor rows and repeats a step that reads only the rows the previous iteration added,
so each iteration goes one level further. When a step adds nothing, it stops, and the result is every row from
every iteration. That lets one query walk a hierarchy of any depth (down to a team, up to a chain of command) and
generate rows such as a calendar. A date spine LEFT JOINed to the data makes reports show zero days instead of
skipping them. Every recursion needs a stop condition, and real hierarchies deserve a depth limit.

<Section kind="intuition" title="When a join copies rows you then add up">

The last lesson ended with a warning: a join repeats a row once for every match. That repetition is harmless when
you only look at the rows, and dangerous the moment you SUM them.

Here is the situation in plain words. Order 107 contains 2 items (a desk lamp at 32 and a mug at 9, so 41 in
total), and Eli paid for it in 2 instalments of 20.50. Suppose you want one line per order showing "items total"
and "amount paid", so you join the order to its items and to its payments. The join has to pair every item with
every payment, because both belong to the same order and SQL has no other way to put them in one row. 2 items ×
2 payments = 4 rows. Each item now appears twice and each payment appears twice, so both sums come out at 82
instead of 41. Nothing errors. The report is simply double.

This is called <Term id="join-fan-out">fan-out</Term>, and it is the most common cause of inflated numbers in
analytics. The second half of the lesson covers the opposite need: sometimes you do not want the matching rows at
all, only the answer to "is there a match?" or "is there no match?". Those are semi-joins and anti-joins, and they
avoid duplicates by design.

</Section>

<Section kind="technical" title="Why fan-out happens, and the two ways to avoid it">

**Grain again.** `order_items` and `payments` are both one-to-many from `orders`: one order, many lines; one
order, many payments. Joining `orders` to `order_items` changes the grain to "one order line". Joining that to
`payments` changes it again, to "one (order line, payment) pair". The second join is many-to-many through the
order, which is why the rows multiply rather than add.

The rule to remember: **each sum gets multiplied by the number of rows on the other side**. Order 107's item
total is multiplied by its 2 payments, and its payment total by its 2 items. Order 105 has 2 items but only 1
payment, so its item total is right (31) while its payments are doubled (62).

<SqlRunner example={fanoutBug} title="fanout_bug.sql" />

**Fix 1: aggregate each side to the join key first.** If each side has at most one row per order before it is
joined, nothing can multiply. Compute "items total per order" in one subquery, "paid per order" in another, and
join those one-row-per-order results to `orders`. Now 107 shows 41 and 41.

<SqlRunner example={fanoutFix} title="fanout_fix.sql" />

**Fix 2: do not join what you do not need.** If the question is only about payments, leave `order_items` out.
Every extra one-to-many join is a chance to fan out.

What about `SUM(DISTINCT ...)`? It is a tempting patch and a bad one: DISTINCT removes duplicate values, not
duplicate rows, so two genuine lines that both cost 9.00 would count once. It hides the symptom on test data and
fails on real data.

</Section>

Things to try:

- **2 items, 2 payments.** 4 rows; both sums double, like order 107.
- **3 items, 1 payment.** 3 rows; the item total is right, the paid total is tripled. With only one row on one
  side, that side causes no inflation, but the other side's sum is multiplied by it.
- **4 items, 4 payments.** 16 rows and every sum is 4 times too big. Fan-out grows with the product of the sides,
  so one customer with many orders and many support tickets can dominate a report.
- **Switch the query to "SUM each side first".** Always 1 row and both sums correct, whatever the counts.

</Section>

<Section kind="code" title="Semi-joins and anti-joins: asking only whether a match exists">

**The question behind the query.** "Which customers bought electronics?" is a yes/no question per customer. A
join answers a different question, "list every (customer, electronics line) pair", so Ben appears twice (he bought
headphones in two orders) and so does Chen (headphones and a charger in one order).

<SqlRunner example={joinDuplicates} title="join_repeats.sql" />

A <Term id="semi-join">semi-join</Term> keeps each row of the first table at most once, if at least one match
exists. In SQL it is `WHERE EXISTS (subquery)`. The subquery mentions `c.id` from the outer query, so it is
evaluated per customer: "for this customer, is there at least one electronics line?". `SELECT 1` inside EXISTS is
a convention: EXISTS only cares whether a row comes back, not what is in it.

<SqlRunner example={semiJoin} title="semi_join.sql" />

`WHERE c.id IN (SELECT customer_id FROM ...)` is another way to write a semi-join, and fine when the subquery
does not need the outer row.

An <Term id="anti-join">anti-join</Term> is the opposite: keep the rows with no match. "Customers who never
ordered" and "products never sold" are anti-joins. Two safe spellings:

- `WHERE NOT EXISTS (subquery)`: "there is no matching row".
- `LEFT JOIN ... WHERE right.key IS NULL`: keep every row, then keep only those the LEFT JOIN had to pad with
  NULLs. Test the join key (or another column that cannot be NULL in a real match), so a padded row is the only way
  the test can be TRUE.

<SqlRunner example={antiJoin} title="anti_join.sql" />

**The NOT IN trap, now with a real cause.** The obvious third spelling, `WHERE id NOT IN (SELECT customer_id
FROM orders)`, returns nothing here. The NULL lesson explained why: NOT IN means "not equal to every value in the
list", and the guest order puts a NULL in the list. "id is not equal to NULL" is UNKNOWN, so no customer passes.
NOT EXISTS does not have this problem, because it asks whether a matching row exists, and a NULL key never
matches anything.

</Section>

<Section kind="mistakes" title="Duplicates and disappearing rows">

<CommonMistake
  title="Summing across two one-to-many joins"
  wrong={'FROM orders o JOIN order_items oi ON ... JOIN payments p ON ...\nSELECT SUM(oi.quantity * oi.unit_price), SUM(p.amount)'}
  error="(no error) orders with several items and several payments are counted several times"
  cause="The two child tables multiply each other through the shared order."
  fix="Aggregate each child table to one row per order_id first, then join."
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Patching fan-out with DISTINCT"
  wrong={'SELECT SUM(DISTINCT p.amount) ...'}
  error="(no error) two genuine instalments of 20.50 count as one"
  cause="DISTINCT removes repeated values, not repeated rows."
  fix="Remove the cause: pre-aggregate, or drop the join that is not needed."
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="NOT IN with a nullable subquery"
  wrong={'WHERE id NOT IN (SELECT customer_id FROM orders)'}
  error="(no error) no rows at all"
  cause="One NULL customer_id makes 'id <> NULL' UNKNOWN for every customer."
  fix="WHERE NOT EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM orders o WHERE o.customer_id = customers.id)."
/>

<CommonMistake
  title="Using a join to test for existence"
  wrong={"SELECT c.name FROM customers c JOIN orders o ON o.customer_id = c.id WHERE o.status = 'returned'"}
  error="(no error) customers with two returned orders are listed twice"
  cause="A join returns every matching pair, not one row per customer."
  fix="WHERE EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM orders o WHERE o.customer_id = c.id AND o.status = 'returned')."
/>

</Section>

<Section kind="practice" title="Habits that keep totals honest">

- **Before every join, say what it does to the grain.** One-to-one keeps it; one-to-many changes it.
- **Never join two one-to-many children of the same parent and then sum.** Pre-aggregate each child.
- **Use EXISTS and NOT EXISTS for yes/no questions** instead of joins with DISTINCT.
- **Avoid NOT IN on subqueries**; if you must, filter out NULLs inside the subquery.
- **Reconcile**: a total after joins should equal the same total from the base table alone.

</Section>

<Section kind="summary">

A join repeats rows once per match, so joining two one-to-many tables to the same parent multiplies them: every
sum is inflated by the row count of the other side. The fix is to bring each side to one row per key before
joining (or to leave out joins the question does not need), not DISTINCT. When the question is only whether a
match exists, use a semi-join (EXISTS) or an anti-join (NOT EXISTS, or LEFT JOIN with IS NULL on the key); they
return each row at most once. NOT IN breaks when its list contains a NULL, which ties this lesson back to
three-valued logic.
